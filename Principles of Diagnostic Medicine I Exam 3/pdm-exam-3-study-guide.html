11.1 · Objective a — The conduction system and the cardiac cell

The heart has four chambers. The right atrium receives deoxygenated blood from the two venae cavae, the right ventricle pumps it to the lungs, the left atrium receives oxygenated blood from the lungs and the left ventricle pumps it into the aorta. Seventy to 80 percent of ventricular filling is passive, needing no atrial contraction, which becomes important when the atria stop contracting (atrial fibrillation, Lecture 12).

Four cardiac cell properties. Automaticity: producing its own impulse. Excitability: responding to an impulse or stimulus. Contractility: contracting. Conductivity: conducting the impulse from myocyte to myocyte. Spontaneous firing of the lower cells is prevented by the natural hierarchy of pacemaker function, so the fastest pacemaker, the sinoatrial node, normally sets the rate.

Action potential phaseWhat happens

★ Absolute refractory period: a short time after an action potential (typically about 180 milliseconds) when the cell will not respond to another stimulus. Relative refractory period: during repolarization, when the cell will respond to a second stimulus but is very fragile. The peak of the T wave divides the two.

The heart’s vector. The heart sits in the mediastinum; the true pathway of conduction runs left and down, toward the left foot, and a shift outside the normal range is abnormal. (The deck says it points toward the front of the body; the normal vector points left, down and slightly backward, not forward.) Lead II has a perfect view of that vector, which is why it is the lead used for rhythm interpretation.

Where the slide text and the truth differ. Slides 20–22 say potassium moves into the cell in phases 1, 2 and 3. Repolarization is potassium leaving the cell (an outward positive current makes the inside negative again), and the deck’s own figure on slide 17 shows exactly that efflux. The quizzes key the efflux.

QTc. This deck has no slide on the QTc interval. It is the QT corrected for heart rate, covered in Lecture 7 (Exam 2 guide, section 7.7): the same normal values as the QT, borderline above 450 (460 in women) to 500 milliseconds, and high-risk above 500 milliseconds.

Slide 26 says a small box is “0.04ms” in duration. It is 0.04 seconds (40 milliseconds); five of them make the 0.2-second large box on the same slide.

Slides 33 and 34 tie the R wave to the anterior left ventricle and the S wave to its lateral wall. Standard teaching describes the R wave as depolarization of the main mass of the ventricles and the S wave as the last, basal parts to depolarize, not as single walls. Both agree on the direction (toward lead II draws an upright wave, away from it a negative one), and that is all the quizzes ask.

Also tested

  • Regular or irregular. When the same distance separates every pair of consecutive R waves, the rhythm is regular; when the R to R intervals differ from beat to beat, the rhythm is irregular.
  • The six-second method, worked. Fourteen QRS complexes in six seconds, multiplied by ten, gives a ventricular rate of 140 per minute.
  • The 300 method, worked. Divide 300 by the large boxes between consecutive R waves: 300 divided by four is 75 per minute, and 300 divided by six large boxes is 50 per minute, below 60 and so a bradycardia.

11.4 · Objective d — The systematic approach

Five steps, every strip, in order. 1 Rate (under 60, 60–100, over 100) → 2 Regularity (consecutive R to R intervals) → 3 P waves → 4 PR interval (0.12–0.20 seconds, constant?) → 5 QRS (narrow under 0.12 seconds, or wide).

The P wave questions: Are they all the same morphology? Is there one before each QRS? Are they married to the QRS complexes (is each P wave causing its QRS)? Are they normal (round, upright in lead II, 2.5 millimeters or less, under 0.12 seconds)? Is the PR interval constant across the strip? Atrioventricular nodal blocks, accessory pathways and alternate pacemaker sites can put the PR outside its normal range.

★ A P wave married to every QRS means the rhythm is atrial in origin, whatever the QRS looks like; identical upright P waves, all of one shape, point to the sinoatrial node, while differing shapes point to other atrial sites. A P wave that only sits in front of a QRS by chance, as in complete heart block, does not count, and an inverted P wave just before the QRS is junctional. If you see QRS complexes with no P waves, the atria are not producing normal P waves: the impulse may have started below the atria, as in a junctional or ventricular rhythm, or the atria may be firing chaotically, as in atrial fibrillation.

11.7 · Objective g — Interpreting sinus arrhythmia, sinus block and sick sinus syndrome

Sinus arrhythmia (respiratory sinus arrhythmia) is a benign variation in the intervals between heartbeats, most commonly seen in children. The rate rises on inspiration and falls on exhalation; you can watch it follow the patient’s breathing.

Sinus exit block (sinus block): the sinus node fires as normal, but its impulses are blocked, so one or more whole cardiac cycles are absent (no P wave, no QRS, no T wave). Because the node never stopped, the P to P intervals still march out once activity resumes: the gap is an exact multiple of the normal cycle.

★ Sick sinus syndrome: sinus node problems such as sinus exit block, sinus pause and sinus arrest, often more than one in the same patient, even on the same strip. The deck calls it rare; in practice it is mostly a disease of older adults.

11.9 · Objective i — Anatomy and physiology of sinus node dysfunction

  • The node. The sinoatrial node, in the upper right atrial wall below the superior vena cava, fires at 60–100 per minute and keeps the lower pacemakers quiet through the pacemaker hierarchy; when it fails, the atrioventricular node (40–60) and then the Purkinje network (20–40) take over.
  • Exit block is a conduction problem: the node fires on time but the impulse is blocked from leaving it, so the timing is preserved and the beats march out.
  • Pause and arrest are firing problems: the node stops and starts again, the cycle resets, and the beats do not march out; at least three missed cycles is arrest.
  • Sick sinus syndrome is the umbrella for sinus node problems like these, often more than one in the same patient.
  • Sinus arrhythmia is physiology, not dysfunction: on inspiration intrathoracic pressure falls, arterial pressure falls, the baroreceptors respond, vagal tone is suppressed and the heart rate rises; on exhalation the opposite happens.

The one-line frame for the whole lecture. Early or late, narrow or wide, and what the P wave looks like. A premature beat comes early; an escape beat comes late after a pause. A narrow QRS means the impulse used the normal pathway from above the ventricles; a wide QRS with an opposite T wave means it started in the ventricles. The P wave says where above the ventricles: a new-shaped upright P is atrial, an inverted, hidden or trailing P is junctional, a sawtooth is flutter, chaos is fibrillation.

12.1 · Objective a — Recognizing premature beats

Premature atrial complex. An early ectopic impulse from outside the sinoatrial node, within the atria, from one or several sites, usually from increased automaticity. It arrives early with a P wave of different morphology and (usually) a narrow QRS; the rest of the rhythm is normal.

Premature junctional complex. Comes early, before the next expected R wave, from increased automaticity of the atrioventricular junction. Its P wave is that of any junctional beat: none, inverted, or after the QRS. Isolated premature junctional complexes have no clinical significance.

Premature ventricular complex. Comes early, before the next expected R wave. Ventricular beats are wide and abnormal-looking (QRS typically over 0.12 seconds), with no P wave and a T wave that goes the opposite direction (“contralateral”). Two in a row is a couplet, three a triplet; the same shape means one focus (unifocal), different shapes mean several foci (multifocal). Frequent, especially grouped, premature ventricular complexes can reduce cardiac output.

★ R on T. If the R wave of a premature ventricular complex lands on the T wave of the beat before it, in the relative refractory period, it can set off ventricular tachycardia or ventricular fibrillation.

Patterns. Premature atrial, junctional and ventricular complexes can all recur in a pattern: every other beat is bigeminy, every third beat trigeminy, every fourth beat quadrigeminy.

Also tested

  • Trigeminy. Every third beat is a premature complex: two normal beats then an early one, repeating.
  • Quadrigeminy. Every fourth beat is a premature complex: three normal beats then an early one (here ventricular).
  • A run of consecutive wide beats. Three or more consecutive premature ventricular complexes at a fast rate are ventricular tachycardia; a brief run is nonsustained and needs evaluation.

Atrial flutter. A rapid atrial rate from a reentry circuit, most often in the right atrium, set up by chamber enlargement or scarred or ischemic tissue. On paper it makes a ★ sawtooth pattern that is pathognomonic. Flutter waves (F waves) replace P waves; there is no PR interval; the QRS is narrow or wide. The atrial rate is about 300 (the deck’s range is 200–350); the ventricular rate depends on how many flutter waves pass for each QRS. Document the ratio: two flutter waves per QRS is atrial flutter with 2:1 ratio, three is 3:1. If the ratio changes, the rhythm is irregular and is documented as atrial flutter with variable conduction. To count, draw a line through the flutter-wave peaks and count the troughs to the next QRS; a notch at the end of the QRS counts as a flutter wave too.

Atrial fibrillation. The most treated cardiac arrhythmia: irregular electrical activity of multiple atrial sites that suppresses the sinoatrial node. The atria quiver instead of contracting, so the atrial kick is lost. No P waves, only fibrillatory (f) waves; the atrial rate is indeterminate and the ventricular rate variable; ★ irregularly irregular; no PR interval; QRS narrow or wide. Three types: paroxysmal (converts without intervention), persistent (lasts more than 7 days, or needs a drug or electrical cardioversion to stop), permanent (ongoing, and no further attempt is made to restore normal rhythm).

Atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia, the most common kind of paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (and often simply called supraventricular tachycardia): a regular, narrow-complex tachycardia from a reentry circuit over the slow and fast pathways, usually set off by an ectopic beat, typically a premature atrial complex near the fast (superior anterior) or slow (inferior posterior) tract. ★ Rate 150–300, regular, P waves not visible (or retrograde), no PR (or an RP interval), narrow QRS.

  • Typical (most common): the circuit runs counterclockwise down the slow tract and up the fast tract; no P waves, or retrograde P waves buried at the end of the QRS (pseudo S waves).
  • Atypical (rare): down the fast tract and up the slow; retrograde P waves are clearly visible, with an RP interval rather than a PR.

12.8 · Objective h — Anatomy and physiology of the supraventricular arrhythmias

  • Premature atrial complexes: increased automaticity of an ectopic atrial site.
  • Wandering pacemaker and multifocal atrial tachycardia: at least three atrial sites competing to be the pacemaker, so the P wave shape keeps changing.
  • Atrial flutter: one reentry circuit, most often in the right atrium, in enlarged, scarred or ischemic tissue; the atrioventricular node lets only some flutter waves through, giving the ratio.
  • Atrial fibrillation: many atrial sites firing chaotically, suppressing the sinus node; loss of the atrial kick (the 20–30 percent of ventricular filling that is not passive, Lecture 11).
  • Atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia: a reentry loop over a slow and a fast tract, started by a premature atrial complex; in the typical form down the slow tract and up the fast.
  • The atrioventricular node’s three zones: the transition zone receives the sinus impulse and is very arrhythmogenic (most premature junctional complexes); the compact zone is the core that slows conduction to allow atrial contraction and ventricular filling, resembles the sinus node and can be the backup pacemaker; the trigger zone sends the signal down the His bundle.

Ventricular tachycardia

Ventricular tachycardia (the deck also calls it V-tach) refers to any tachycardia which originates in the ventricles. It can present with or without a pulse, and it can be regular and monomorphic (every complex the same shape) or irregular and polymorphic. On the strip: rate 100 to 300 per minute, regular, wide QRS complexes, no P waves (any that are present are usually dissociated, not married to the QRS) and typically no PR interval. The deck’s two worked strips run at 140 and 190 per minute.

Polymorphic ventricular tachycardia: torsades de pointes

Polymorphic ventricular tachycardia is an irregular ventricular tachycardia at 100 to 300 per minute. The form that comes with a prolonged QT (Q-to-T) interval is called torsades de pointes, French for twisting of the points, from its unique look and shape: the complexes swing from positive to negative around the baseline. ★ It is usually a consequence of a prolonged QT interval; polymorphic ventricular tachycardia with a normal QT interval, for example from ischemia, is not torsades.

Ventricular fibrillation

Ventricular fibrillation is asynchronous, chaotic firing of multiple foci within the ventricles. The heart muscle cannot contract, so it quivers; with no ventricular contraction there is no cardiac output and no pulse. There are no measurable P waves, QRS complexes or T waves: rate 300 to 500 per minute, irregular, no PR, QRS not measurable. Low-amplitude fibrillation is called fine; large chaotic waves are coarse.

Asystole

Asystole is a lack of electrical activity with no pulse, also known as flat line. Untreated, ventricular fibrillation ends in it.

Pulseless electrical activity

Pulseless electrical activity is a condition in which there is organized electrical activity on the monitor but no palpable pulse because the heart is not pumping effectively. The deck describes it as no mechanical activity; ultrasound can show weak contractions. ★ It can look like any organized rhythm, even normal sinus; if there is no pulse, it is pulseless electrical activity. So the strip alone cannot make the diagnosis: the absence of a pulse does.

Ventricular standstill

During ventricular standstill there are atrial contractions but no ventricular contractions: obvious, upright P waves march out across the strip with no QRS complexes, no detectable pulse and no cardiac output. A patient can go through episodes of paroxysmal ventricular standstill, conducted beats and standstill alternating.

13.3 · Paced rhythms (on the deck, read alongside the ventricular rhythms)

  • Atrial pacemakers take over for the sinoatrial node and depend on an intact and properly functioning conduction pathway. In an atrial paced rhythm a pacer spike is followed by a normal P-QRS-T cycle, so the QRS is narrow and normal.
  • Ventricular pacemakers pace the ventricles below the level of the atria, so a ventricular paced rhythm shows a (possible) P wave, a pacer spike, a wide QRS complex and a contralateral T wave, one going the opposite direction of the QRS. ★ A spike married to every QRS, a wide QRS and a contralateral T wave show the ventricle is captured.
  • Patients with a dual-chamber pacemaker, which is common, can have both an atrial and a ventricular pacemaker spike: a spike before the P wave and another before each wide QRS.

13.4 · Objective c — Recognizing the atrioventricular blocks

Atrioventricular junction heart blocks are a delay in, or a complete failure of, the conduction of an atrial impulse through the atrioventricular node. There are four: first-degree; second-degree type I (Wenckebach, also called Mobitz I); second-degree type II (Mobitz II); and third-degree (complete heart block).

First-degree atrioventricular block

A benign PR interval delay from slowed conduction through the atrioventricular node: a delayed conduction between the sinus node and the ventricles. ★ The hallmark is a sinus rhythm with a PR interval beyond 0.2 seconds, and all the P waves are conducted; nothing is dropped. Rate slow or normal, regular, P waves present (normal and married to the QRS), QRS narrow or wide.

Second-degree type I (Wenckebach, Mobitz I)

Caused by blockage of conduction in the atrioventricular node: nodal conduction slows a little more with each beat until one impulse is not conducted (the slide attributes this to the all-or-nothing property of the cardiac cell’s depolarization; see the note at the top of this section). The classic pattern is grouped beats until a non-conducted P wave drops a QRS complex, and then the cycle begins over: a predictable progressive prolongation of the PR interval until a P wave is not conducted. Rate slow or normal; irregular (from the dropped beat); P waves present, normal and married to the QRS except for the dropped beat; PR increasing; QRS narrow or wide.

Second-degree type II (Mobitz II)

Less common but more serious than type I; it is possible to have more than one dropped beat in a row, and it is almost always a sign of underlying disease of the conduction system. ★ The hallmark is a constant PR interval with some non-conducted P waves followed by dropped QRS complexes. Type II is identified when at least two consecutive atrial impulses are conducted with constant PR intervals before the dropped QRS. Rate slow or normal; irregular; P waves present, normal and married to the QRS except for the dropped beats; PR normal or wide, but always constant across the strip; QRS wide or narrow — usually, not always, wide from an accompanying bundle branch block.

Third-degree atrioventricular block (complete heart block)

Atrial and ventricular activity are totally independent: there is a total blockage, and no P waves are conducted through the atrioventricular junction. ★ The hallmark is regular P-P intervals unrelated to regular R-R intervals, with P waves that appear to march through the QRS-T complexes. The atrial rate is faster than the ventricular rate, and there are two independent pacemakers: the sinoatrial node, and either the atrioventricular junction or the Purkinje and bundle-branch fibers. Regular; no PR interval; QRS narrow or wide depending on the origin of the escape.

13.6 · Objective e — Nodal versus infranodal conduction

  • First-degree block sits in the atrioventricular node: slowed conduction, every impulse still gets through.
  • Second-degree type I sits in the atrioventricular node, where conduction slows beat by beat until one impulse fails; he described it as the node progressively fatiguing until it does not release an impulse, then resetting. The P waves march out on time; the node is what falls behind.
  • Second-degree type II is infranodal: low, below the atrioventricular node, at the His bundle, generally in the His-Purkinje system (slide 36). It reflects disease of the conduction system.
  • Third-degree block is a total blockage at the junction. The sinoatrial node keeps driving the atria; the ventricles are driven by an escape pacemaker below the block, in the atrioventricular junction (a narrow QRS) or the Purkinje and bundle-branch fibers (a wide QRS, as in slide 43’s ventricular escape).
  • Ventricular tachycardia comes from a ventricular focus. Its impulse often does not travel back up into the atria, so the atria usually carry on doing their own thing and any P waves are usually dissociated from the QRS complexes (in some ventricular tachycardia the impulse does conduct backward to the atria).

14.3 · Objective c — Recognizing right and left bundle branch block

Both blocks share one requirement: a QRS of 0.12 seconds or longer (three small boxes). The slides say it is “best measured in lead V1”; strictly, QRS duration is measured in the lead where the QRS is widest, and V1 is the lead that decides the side, which is what the quizzes ask. The side is decided in V1 by the J point rule: find the J point and travel backwards to the very first deflection you come to.

Left bundle branch block

  • QRS 0.12 seconds or longer.
  • rS pattern in V1: ★ traveling back from the J point in V1, the very first deflection is negative.
  • Notching of the QRS in the lateral leads (I, aVL, V5, V6): the double QRS complex phenomenon, best seen in V6.
  • No Q waves in the lateral leads.

Right bundle branch block

  • QRS 0.12 seconds or longer.
  • rSR pattern in V1: ★ traveling back from the J point in V1, the very first deflection is positive (a terminal R wave).
  • Slurred S waves in the lateral leads (I, aVL, V5, V6): the double QRS complex phenomenon, best seen in V6.
  • It is commonly taught as “bunny ears” in V1, but the deck advises against relying on that: right bundle branch block has many morphologies in V1, and the constant is the positive first deflection back from the J point.

14.5 · Objective e — Recognizing right and left atrial enlargement

Right atrial enlargement (P pulmonale)

  • In the limb leads (I, II, III): an upright P wave taller than 2.5 millimeters.
  • In V1: a biphasic P wave with a larger positive initial deflection and a smaller negative terminal deflection.
  • ★ Evidence of P pulmonale in one of these leads is confirmatory; the criteria need not be met in every lead.

Left atrial enlargement (P mitrale)

  • In the limb leads (I, II, III): an upright, humped P wave at least 0.12 seconds in duration, with the humps at least 0.04 seconds apart.
  • In V1: a biphasic P wave with a small positive initial deflection and a large negative terminal deflection.
  • Evidence of P mitrale in one of these leads is confirmatory; the criteria need not be met in every lead.

14.7 · Objective g — Recognizing left and right ventricular hypertrophy

Right ventricular hypertrophy

  • Criterion: an R to S ratio of 1 or more in V1 and V2: more R wave than S wave.
  • Supportive: right atrial enlargement, right axis deviation, a strain pattern.
  • Makes the criterion unreliable: right bundle branch block, posterior wall infarction and children under the age of 7 or 8 also give a tall R wave in V1.
  • Strain pattern: the shape of the ST segment from the J point into an inverted, asymmetrical T wave, in V1 and V2.

Left ventricular hypertrophy

The thick wall pulls the electrical vector to the left, so left ventricular hypertrophy can cause left axis deviation, with deep S waves in V1 and V2 and tall R waves in the lateral leads (I, aVL, V5, V6). It is present by any one of these:

  • The deeper S wave in V1 or V2 plus the taller R wave in V5 or V6 adds up to 35 millimeters or more.
  • The R wave in aVL is greater than 12 millimeters.
  • Any chest lead is greater than 45 millimeters.

14.9 · Objective i — The anatomy behind conduction blocks and chamber enlargement

Also tested

  • Left anterior fascicular block. The left anterior hemiblock is the most common intraventricular conduction abnormality during an acute myocardial infarction.

The conduction system and the left bundle’s fascicles

The left bundle is a small piece of tissue that arises from the His bundle and quickly splits into the posterior fascicle, the anterior fascicle and the interventricular septal fascicle. The left ventricle and septum are normally depolarized from left to right, starting with the septum, which is what makes the normal septal Q wave.

Why a bundle branch block widens the QRS

In left bundle branch block (a total failure of the left bundle, so a block through all its fascicles), impulses from the His bundle travel down the right bundle normally, so the right ventricle contracts first. The left ventricle then depends on myocyte-to-myocyte transmission across the heart, much slower than the conduction pathway, so its contraction is delayed and the QRS is wide. Because the ventricles contract in sequence, not at the same time, the QRS shows the two QRS complex phenomenon. And because the septal fascicle is blocked, the septal Q wave is lost: no Q waves in the lateral leads.

Right bundle branch block is the mirror image: impulses travel down the left bundle normally, the left ventricle contracts normally, and the right ventricle waits for myocyte-to-myocyte spread; the delay widens the QRS and gives the same two-complex phenomenon.

15.2 · Objective b — Recognizing ischemia, injury and infarction

The J point and the baseline you measure it against

The junction point, or J point, lies between the QRS complex and the ST segment. The ST segment begins at the J point and ends at the onset of the T wave. In a healthy heart the J point and the ST segment are both at baseline. Cardiac events such as myocardial ischemia (acute coronary syndrome) can move the J point above or below the baseline, called ST-segment elevation or ST-segment depression.

Compare the J point with the TP segment, the resting baseline between beats. At fast heart rates the TP segment can be hard to locate; then it is appropriate to compare the J point with the beginning of the QRS complex.

In lecture: avoid the PR segment as the baseline when you can, because the atria are still active then; the TP segment is the moment of complete rest [part 1, 14:04–15:06].

Ischemia: hyperacute T waves first

★ Hyperacute T waves are often the very first sign of cardiac ischemia. They are broad, increased in amplitude and symmetrical. Normal T waves are asymmetrical, with a slow initial upstroke and a sharper negative terminal deflection.

Ischemia: ST depression. Subendocardial ischemia is represented by ST-segment depression, often widespread (most obvious in the lateral chest leads, and often with elevation in aVR). Injury: ST elevation. Transmural ischemia, also referred to as injury, is represented by ST-segment elevation. Infarction: pathologic Q waves. Pathological Q waves usually represent infarction, the actual death of cardiac tissue. Each is detailed in 15.3.

Pathologic Q waves = infarction

Pathological Q waves usually represent infarction and actual death of cardiac tissue, from either a previous or an acute cardiac event (similar Q waves can come from other causes such as cardiomyopathy or pre-excitation). A Q wave is pathologic when it is:

  • longer than 0.04 seconds in duration (one small box), and
  • deeper than 2 mm (two small boxes), or, when there is an R wave, deeper than 25% of the height of the R wave.

With no R wave at all, the single negative deflection is technically a QS complex; in the setting of an infarction it is read as a pathological Q wave. Q waves on a tracing from a patient with no reported heart history point to a prior infarction the patient did not know about [part 2, 11:20–11:57].

15.6 · Objective f — Acute coronary syndrome patterns

The ST-elevation criterion and reciprocal changes

An ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction is considered when there is 1 mm of elevation or more in 2 or more contiguous leads, with or without reciprocal changes (in V2 and V3 the threshold is higher; see the note at the top of this section).

  • ST depression should be considered a reciprocal change until proven otherwise if ST elevation also exists in the same tracing.
  • ★ Reciprocal changes are a confirmatory sign of cardiac ischemia when ST elevation is present, but their absence does not exclude cardiac ischemia.

The three classic territories

Posterior infarction: record V7 to V9

  • Isolated posterior wall infarctions account for 3% to 4% of all acute myocardial infarctions, typically from occlusion or stenosis of the left circumflex artery.
  • ★ Isolated ST depression in V1 to V4 with no ST elevation anywhere should always be considered a reciprocal change for a posterior infarction until proven otherwise, and a posterior tracing must be obtained.
  • To obtain it, leads V4, V5 and V6 are moved to the back and relabeled V7, V8 and V9, and the tracing is repeated; this is at times called a 15-lead electrocardiogram. In lecture: V7 at the posterior axillary line, V8 at the tip of the scapula, V9 between the scapula and the spine, all at the same level [part 2, 46:35–47:06].
  • ST elevation in V7 to V9 confirms a posterior wall infarction.

Inferior infarction with right ventricular involvement: record V4R

  • Inferior infarctions involving the right coronary artery can include the right ventricle.
  • Suspect it whenever a patient with acute coronary syndrome symptoms presents with hypotension, clear lung sounds and jugular vein distension.
  • To rule it in or out, V4 is moved to the right side of the sternum, fifth intercostal space, midclavicular line, and the tracing is repeated; the lead is marked V4R.
  • Elevation in V4R confirms right ventricular involvement.
Which extra tracing? Isolated ST depression in V1 to V4 → posterior leads V7 to V9 (the circumflex and the back wall). Inferior elevation with hypotension, clear lungs and distended neck veins → V4R (the right coronary artery and the right ventricle).

15.7 · Objective g — The anatomy and physiology behind the changes

On the slides: subendocardial ischemia (the innermost layer) gives ST depression; transmural ischemia, also called injury, gives ST elevation; pathologic Q waves mean tissue death; and myocardial ischemia (acute coronary syndrome) is what moves the J point off the baseline (15.2, 15.3).

In lecture (the mechanism, not on a slide):

  • Why the subendocardium starves first. The coronary arteries run on the epicardial surface and dive inward, getting smaller. Past a narrowing, the epicardium takes what blood there is, and the endocardium gets what is left [part 1, 33:26–35:31].
  • The cause. Atherosclerotic plaque causes most ischemic heart disease. Stable angina comes with a predictable amount of exertion and is relieved within about 20 minutes of rest; when a plaque ruptures, platelets aggregate on it, the narrowing worsens, and pain persists at rest [part 1, 23:22–32:19].
  • Why the baseline moves. Without oxygen the cells make no ATP (adenosine triphosphate), the sodium-potassium pump fails and potassium leaks out. The neighboring cells rest less negative and depolarize early. Early depolarization toward the lead (inner layer only) shifts the baseline up, so the ST segment looks depressed; across the full wall it runs away from the lead and the ST segment looks elevated [part 1, 36:04–45:03; part 2, 1:45–5:28].
  • The R wave fades as cells die. Dead cells cannot depolarize, so the R wave shrinks toward a Q wave [part 1, 21:37–22:16; part 2, 14:26–15:28].

The one-line frame for the whole lecture. Most of this lecture is ST elevation that is not a coronary occlusion, plus the patterns that hide or imitate one. A true infarction is localized, convex and has reciprocal depression; pericarditis and early repolarization are global, concave and have none. Then learn the shapes that name a cause: coved V1 to V3 (Brugada), a terminal notch (Osborn, cold), a slurred upstroke with a short PR (Wolff-Parkinson-White), peaked T waves (potassium high), U waves (potassium low), a long or short ST segment (calcium low or high).

Numbers: laboratory values in the quizzes come with their reference ranges; the cutoffs below (25% in V6, 1 mm and 5 mm for Sgarbossa, 32°C for Osborn waves) are part of the patterns themselves.

16.1 · Objective a — Early repolarization, acute pericarditis and long QT

Acute pericarditis

Pericarditis is inflammation of the pericardium, the two-layered sac around the heart (visceral and parietal layers); the whole pericardium, including over the atria, is inflamed, which is why the PR segment changes too. Its tracing shows:

  • PR-segment depression, and PR-segment elevation in aVR;
  • global, concave ST-segment elevation;
  • ★ no reciprocal ST depression anywhere on the tracing, except in aVR and V1.

If there is ST depression in any lead other than aVR and V1, or any new Q wave, it is not typical pericarditis, and an acute ST-elevation infarction must be considered.

Benign early repolarization

Benign early repolarization is also known as normal variant ST elevation. It is seen mostly in young men and has no clinical significance. Its tracing shows:

  • global, concave ST-segment elevation;
  • terminal QRS notching, known as the J wave (also the fishhook sign, or Osborn wave);
  • large T waves, sometimes symmetrical;
  • no reciprocal ST depression outside aVR and V1;
  • and, unlike pericarditis, no PR-segment depression [part 1, 11:17–11:39].

Long QT syndrome

The deck has no slide on long QT syndrome. What the deck does give is where QT prolongation appears: hypocalcemia (a long ST segment lengthens the QTc), hypothyroidism (prolonged QTc), amiodarone (slows phase 3) and tricyclic antidepressant overdose (16.4, 16.6). In lecture: judge prolongation on the corrected QT (QTc), which the machine prints next to the raw QT [part 2, 2:30–5:20].

The lead V6 phenomenon

When pericarditis and early repolarization are hard to tell apart, use lead V6: measure the height of the ST elevation (from the end of the PR segment to the J point) and compare it with the T-wave amplitude. An ST/T ratio above 25% suggests pericarditis; below 25% suggests benign early repolarization. In other words, early repolarization has more T wave than ST elevation, and pericarditis has more ST elevation than T wave.

Brugada syndrome

An inherited arrhythmogenic disease affecting the sodium channels of the right ventricular outflow tract, most commonly seen in young men of Southeast Asian descent (in lecture: these patients are at greater risk of lethal dysrhythmias such as polymorphic ventricular tachycardia and ventricular fibrillation). Two main patterns, ★ both isolated to V1 to V3:

  • Type 1: convex ST elevation, the coved-shaped type.
  • Type 2: saddle-shaped (saddleback) ST elevation (the slide also calls it the “carousel horses sign”).
  • Some literature mentions a type 3, a combination of both on one tracing.

Left bundle branch block, ventricular pacing and Sgarbossa’s criteria

Left bundle branch blocks and ventricular paced rhythms cause ST and T wave changes that point opposite to the QRS (often ST elevation in the right chest leads) and wide QRS complexes, which make an ST-elevation infarction difficult to locate. Sgarbossa’s criteria (Dr. Elena Sgarbossa) are used to diagnose an acute myocardial infarction in the presence of a left bundle branch block or a ventricularly paced rhythm. The first two are enough on their own; the third counts for less and is not enough alone (in the original scoring the three are worth 5, 3 and 2 points, and 3 or more is positive):

  1. Concordant ST elevation of 1 mm or more in any lead with a positive QRS complex;
  2. Concordant ST depression of 1 mm or more in V1 to V3;
  3. Discordant ST elevation of 5 mm or more in any lead with a negative QRS complex.

Concordant means the ST change is on the same side of the baseline as the QRS; discordant means the opposite side. Some discordance is normal in the block, which is why discordant elevation must reach 5 mm.

16.3 · Objective c — Potassium and calcium on the tracing

Also tested

  • U waves. A small wave after the T wave is a U wave; prominent U waves, with T-wave flattening, are classic signs of hypokalemia, which prolongs repolarization.

Hyperkalemia

Hyperkalemia (high serum potassium) can cause a host of changes, such as bradycardias, tachycardias, ST elevation, T-wave abnormalities and atrioventricular and bundle branch blocks. Its classic signs, in order of appearance:

  1. Peaked, symmetric T waves (the early sign);
  2. flattened P waves;
  3. a wide QRS;
  4. a sine wave (the end stage).

Hypokalemia

Hypokalemia is a serum potassium below 3.5 mEq/L. It prolongs repolarization, so its early signs are changes to the ST segment, the T wave and the U wave. Classic signs: T-wave flattening, prominent U waves, inverted T and U wave fusion, prominent P waves, and ST depression with ST elevation in aVR.

Hypocalcemia

Hypocalcemia prolongs phase 2 of the cardiac action potential, so it prolongs the ST segment without affecting the duration of the T wave; a longer ST segment means a prolonged QTc. Classic findings: prolonged QTc, T waves with normal morphology, prolonged ST segment.

Hypercalcemia

Hypercalcemia shortens phase 2, shortening the ST segment, so the QTc shortens and at times the T wave is pushed right against the QRS. Findings: shortened QTc, T waves close to or attached to the end of the QRS, shortened ST segment.

16.5 · Objective e — COPD, pulmonary embolism and hypothermia

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD/COLD)

The deck has no slide on chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and it did not come up in the recording. Nothing in the quizzes asks about it.

Pulmonary embolism

  • Tachycardia, from sympathetic nervous system activation by hypoxia.
  • A more rightward axis, from dilation and enlargement of the right ventricle under high pressure.
  • ST-segment and T-wave changes, from localized myocardial ischemia.
  • The S1Q3T3 pattern: a deep S wave in lead I, a Q wave in lead III and an inverted T wave in lead III (spelled out in lecture [part 2, 53:43–54:35]).

Hypothermia

As the body temperature approaches 35°C (95°F), sinus bradycardia occurs, typically followed by prolongation of the intervals. Below 32°C (90°F), Osborn waves (J waves, a notch at the end of the QRS) become apparent, and they are frequently mistaken for an ST-elevation infarction.

16.7 · Objective g — Pacemaker rhythms and pacing artifacts

The deck shows one pacemaker rhythm, the ventricular paced rhythm: a sharp vertical pacing spike (the pacing artifact) before each beat, a wide QRS complex, and contralateral (discordant) T waves pointing opposite the QRS, often with ST elevation opposite a deep negative QRS. Like a left bundle branch block it hides an infarction, so Sgarbossa’s criteria (16.2) are used to look for one.

16.8 · Also in the deck — Preexcitation and accessory pathway tachycardias

Normally the impulse from the sinoatrial node must pass through the atrioventricular node, where it is slowed, before reaching the His-Purkinje network. Preexcitation is activation of the ventricles by a supraventricular impulse earlier than the normal pathway would allow, through an accessory pathway that connects the atria to the ventricles, bypassing the atrioventricular node. It shows as a short PR interval and a delta wave. The most common preexcitation syndrome is Wolff-Parkinson-White.

  • The accessory pathway in Wolff-Parkinson-White is the Kent bundle (bundle of Kent).
  • Diagnostic triad: short PR interval, wide QRS complex, delta wave.
  • The delta wave is a slurring of the upstroke of the QRS: impulses bypassing the atrioventricular node start depolarizing the ventricles early, and once the normal impulse reaches the His-Purkinje network the rest of the QRS looks normal. Because the pathway bypasses the atrioventricular node, the PR interval is short.
  • Type A: a left-sided Kent bundle (left atrium to left ventricle) gives a QRS mostly positive in V1. Type B: a right-sided Kent bundle gives a QRS mostly negative in V1.

Atrioventricular reentrant tachycardia

A premature atrial or ventricular complex near the accessory pathway can start premature depolarization, and the accessory pathway with the normal pathway forms a reentry circuit that sustains a tachycardia:

  • Orthodromic: down the normal pathway (toward the ventricles) and up the accessory pathway → a regular narrow-complex tachycardia.
  • Antidromic: down the accessory pathway and back up the normal pathway (toward the atria) → a regular, monomorphic, wide tachycardia. Because the ventricles are activated entirely through the accessory pathway, the QRS is wide and can look like ventricular tachycardia.
  • At times it is impossible to tell orthodromic reentry from supraventricular tachycardia, or antidromic reentry from ventricular tachycardia, on the tracing alone. History helps if the patient knows of the pathway; if not, the slide says the only way to make the interpretation is pharmacological or electrical cardioversion “to slow down the rate and look for delta waves”. Strictly, cardioversion ends the tachycardia, and the delta wave is looked for on the sinus-rhythm tracing afterwards; until then an undiagnosed wide regular tachycardia is treated as ventricular tachycardia.

16.9 · Also in the deck — Thyroid disorders

  • Hyperthyroidism is a state of adrenergic hyperactivity: sinus tachycardia is the most commonly observed finding, and atrial fibrillation the second most common.
  • Hypothyroidism slows the metabolic rate and myocardial contractility: sinus bradycardia, with global low-voltage QRS complexes, flattened or inverted T waves, a prolonged QTc and low-amplitude P waves.
