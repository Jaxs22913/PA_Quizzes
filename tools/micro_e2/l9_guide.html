9 · Cocci of Medical Importance

Instructional Objectives

  1. Compare and contrast the morphology, medium, laboratory tests, biochemical reactions, diseases, and methods of treatment, and prevention of cocci of medical importance.
  2. Describe the modes of transmission by which humans acquire cocci of medical importance.
Two objectives, fifteen organisms. The syllabus heads this lecture “Cocci of Medical Importance – see Appendix 2 for full list of organisms”, and that appendix names them: Staphylococcus aureus, Staphylococcus epidermidis, Staphylococcus hominis, Staphylococcus capitis, Staphylococcus saprophyticus, Streptococcus pyogenes, Streptococcus agalactiae, the viridans streptococci, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Enterococcus faecalis (Gram-positive) and Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Neisseria meningitidis, Branhamella, Moraxella, Acinetobacter baumanii (Gram-negative). Objective 1 is answered organism by organism in 9.1–9.9; objective 2, transmission, is drawn together in one table in 9.10. Branhamella and Moraxella are one organism: Branhamella catarrhalis was reclassified into Moraxella. Learn the decision tree in 9.1 first — Gram stain, catalase, coagulase, hemolysis, then bacitracin or optochin — and every Gram-positive organism places itself.

9.2 · Objective 1 — Staphylococcus aureus

Staphylococci are common inhabitants of the skin and mucous membranes; 31 species are in the genus. Staphylococcus aureus grows in large, round, opaque colonies that may be slightly yellowish (aureus = golden), with an optimum growth temperature of 37°C. It is a facultative anaerobe that thrives with or without oxygen, withstands high salinity, extremes of pH and high temperatures, and produces many virulence factors.

Virulence factorWhat it does
CoagulaseCoagulates plasma and blood; produced by 97% of human isolates; a significant diagnostic trait for this organism
HyaluronidaseDigests connective tissue
StaphylokinaseDigests blood clots
DeoxyribonucleaseDigests deoxyribonucleic acid
LipasesDigest oils and other lipids; enhance colonization on skin
PenicillinaseInactivates penicillin (beta-lactamase behaves similarly)
Hemolysins (alpha, beta, gamma, delta)Lyse red blood cells — visible on blood agar
LeukocidinLyses leukocytes, especially neutrophils and macrophages
EnterotoxinInduces gastrointestinal distress (food intoxication)
Exfoliative toxinSplits the upper layers of the epidermis apart (scalded skin syndrome); the slide says it separates the epidermis from the dermis
Toxic shock syndrome toxinInduces fever, vomiting, shock and systemic organ damage

Epidemiology. Present in most environments frequented by humans and readily isolated from fomites. Carriage rate in healthy adults is 20–60%, mostly in the anterior nares, skin, nasopharynx and intestines. Predispositions: poor hygiene and nutrition, tissue injury, a preexisting primary infection, diabetes, immunodeficiency. Methicillin resistance is increasingly community acquired, no longer just hospital acquired, so strains are designated community-acquired or hospital-acquired.

FolliculitisSuperficial inflammation of a hair follicle; usually resolves, can progress

ImpetigoBubble-like swellings that break and peel away; most common in newborns
OsteomyelitisInfection is established in the metaphysis and an abscess forms

Toxic shock syndromeToxemia leading to shock and organ failure; classically from improper tampon use in females (see 9.11)

Treatment. 95% of staphylococci produce penicillinase and are resistant to penicillin and ampicillin. Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus carries multiple resistance; older teaching (and the slide) describes some strains as resisting all major drug groups except vancomycin, but vancomycin and several newer drugs still work against most strains. Abscesses have to be surgically perforated, and the wounds kept thoroughly clean and covered. Systemic infections may need intensive, lengthy antimicrobial therapy.

Prevention. Universal precautions by healthcare providers prevent, or at least reduce, nosocomial infections. Appropriate personal hygiene, and not sharing towels, washcloths, razors or toothbrushes.

Identification. Staphylococci may be isolated from pus, tissue exudates, sputum, urine and blood, and are identified by cultivation, the catalase reaction, biochemical testing and the coagulase reaction.

9.3 · Objective 1 — The coagulase-negative staphylococci

Coagulase-negative staphylococci are frequently implicated in nosocomial and opportunistic infections, and they often initiate polymicrobial biofilms, which can lead to further medical complications.

9.4 · Objective 1 — Streptococci in general: Lancefield groups and hemolysis

Streptococci are Gram-positive spherical or ovoid cocci in long chains, commonly found in pairs; non-spore-forming, nonmotile; can form capsules and slime layers; facultative anaerobes. They do not produce catalase (catalase negative) but do have a peroxidase system. Most parasitic forms are fastidious and require enriched media, growing as small, nonpigmented colonies on blood agar — but they do not grow well on mannitol salts agar, which is too saline. They are very sensitive to drying, heat and disinfectants.

ClassificationBased on
Lancefield (Rebecca Lancefield, 1933)Cell wall antigens — the C-carbohydrates — giving groups A, B, C and so on. Know A and B; the other letters are not examined.
HemolysisOften combined with 16S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing. Beta = complete hemolysis on blood agar plate: groups A, B, C, G and some D. Alpha = partial hemolysis on blood agar plate: Streptococcus pneumoniae and the others called viridans (viridis is Latin-derived for green, after the greenish color of partial hemolysis)

Human streptococcal pathogens: Streptococcus pyogenes, Streptococcus agalactiae, the viridans streptococci and Streptococcus pneumoniae, with Enterococcus faecalis alongside. Enterococcus, with Lactococcus, was separated from Streptococcus in 1984 using 16S ribosomal RNA gene analysis; the taxonomy is still evolving.

9.5 · Objective 1 — Streptococcus pyogenes (group A)

Beta-hemolytic Streptococcus pyogenes is the most serious of the streptococcal pathogens — Lancefield group A, so sometimes “GAS” (group A Streptococcus). It is a strictly human parasite inhabiting the throat, nasopharynx and occasionally the skin.

Epidemiology. Humans are the only reservoir of Strep pyogenes; other streptococci may be zoonotic. Inapparent carriers exist. Transmission is by contact, droplets, food and fomites; the portal of entry is generally the skin or pharynx. Children are the predominant group affected, for both skin and throat infections. Systemic infections and progressive sequelae are possible if untreated.

Treatment. Groups A and B are commonly treated with penicillin unless it is contraindicated (resistance or allergy). Long-term penicillin prophylaxis may be prescribed for those with a history of rheumatic fever or recurrent strep throat. Big problems abound with antibiotic resistant strains.

9.6 · Objective 1 — Group B Streptococcus, the enterococci, and the identification tests

OrganismKey facts
Group B Streptococcus, Streptococcus agalactiaeRegularly resides in the human vagina, pharynx and large intestine. Can be transferred to the infant during vaginal delivery, causing severe infection: the most prevalent cause of neonatal pneumonia, sepsis and meningitis. Pregnant women should be screened and treated for GBS (group B Streptococcus), usually with intravenous antibiotics during vaginal delivery (see 9.11 on the drug). Also wound and skin infections and endocarditis in debilitated patients
Group D enterococci: Enterococcus faecalis, faecium, duransNormal colonists of the human large intestine; cause opportunistic urinary, wound and skin infections, particularly in debilitated persons. Treatment usually requires multiple antibiotic therapy
Groups C and GCommon animal flora, often isolated from the upper respiratory tract; pharyngitis, glomerulonephritis, bacteremia (not examined; “the other letters”)

Identification. Cultivation and diagnosis ensure proper treatment, preventing complications. Rapid diagnostic tests use monoclonal antibodies that react with the C-carbohydrates (serological or immunological methods). Phenotypic culture methods: the bacitracin disc sensitivity test, the CAMP test and bile esculin hydrolysis.

The CAMP test (named for Christie, Atkins and Munch-Petersen, 1944): a CAMP-factor protein works synergistically with beta-hemolytic Staphylococcus aureus to produce a flame or arrow shape on the blood agar plate. It is time consuming but has been effective in detecting Listeria monocytogenes, a Gram-positive rod whose temperature range is 0–40°C, from food and clinical specimens.

9.8 · Objective 1 — Neisseria gonorrhoeae and Neisseria meningitidis

The family Neisseriaceae: Gram-negative cocci, and therefore only a thin layer of peptidoglycan; residents of the mucous membranes of warm-blooded animals; genera Neisseria, Branhamella, Moraxella (with reclassifications by 16S ribosomal RNA gene analysis). The two primary “frank” pathogens are Neisseria gonorrhoeae and Neisseria meningitidis.

Genus NeisseriaKey facts
MorphologyGram-negative, bean-shaped diplococci; no flagella or spores (non-motile, non-spore-forming); capsules common; pili (short protein fibers made of pilin) enhance attachment
PhysiologyStrict parasites that do not survive long outside the human host. Aerobic or microaerophilic (requiring a low oxygen concentration). Produce both catalase and cytochrome oxidase
CulturePathogenic species need enriched complex media and carbon dioxide-enhanced chambers, such as chocolate agar in a “candle jar” (see 9.11)

Survives no more than 1–2 hours on fomitesPrevalent cause of meningitis, sporadic or epidemic. Reservoir: the nasopharynx of 3–30% of adults, higher in institutional settings.
