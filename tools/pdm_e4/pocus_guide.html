Instructional Objectives

Topic Outline 20: Point-of-Care Ultrasound and Diagnostic Integration (the calendar’s Lecture #20)

  1. Distinguish between focused point-of-care ultrasound and comprehensive ultrasonography.
  2. Discuss the role of point-of-care ultrasound as an adjunct to the history and physical examination.
  3. Interpret basic point-of-care ultrasound findings by distinguishing normal and abnormal findings.
  4. Employ established ultrasound terminology to communicate findings effectively.
  5. Discuss common point-of-care ultrasound applications in:
    1. Cardiovascular assessment
    2. Pulmonary assessment
    3. Abdominal assessment
    4. Soft tissue assessment
  6. Integrate laboratory, imaging, and electrocardiographic findings into clinical decision-making.

The one-line frame for the whole lecture. Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) is a focused, limited, goal-directed scan done at the bedside and read by the clinician who performs it, to answer one diagnostic question in real time; the comprehensive study is ordered after the evaluation, performed by a sonographer, read by a radiologist, and takes hours to days. To read any image: black is fluid (anechoic), gray is soft tissue (hypoechoic), white is dense (hyperechoic: bone, stones), and the artifacts are clues, not noise. The lung lives on artifacts: A lines (horizontal) are normal, B lines (vertical) are pulmonary edema.

Abbreviations you will meet: POCUS (point-of-care ultrasound), US (ultrasound), EFAST (extended focused assessment with sonography in trauma), BLUE (bilateral lung ultrasound in emergency), RUSH (rapid ultrasound in shock and hypotension), PSLA and PSSA (parasternal long and short axis), IVC (inferior vena cava), RV and LV (right and left ventricle), RA and LA (right and left atrium), DVT (deep vein thrombosis), PTX (pneumothorax), LVOT (left ventricular outflow tract), RUQ and LUQ (right and left upper quadrant), PCF (pericholecystic fluid).

20.1 · Objective a — Focused point-of-care ultrasound against comprehensive ultrasonography

Point-of-care ultrasoundTraditional (comprehensive) ultrasound
Used at the bedside to give real-time answers to diagnostic questionsOrdered after evaluating the patient
Focused, limited, goal directedComprehensive, but takes time: hours to days
Interpreted by the provider performing itPerformed by a certified sonographer, interpreted by a radiologist
Can be an extension of the history and physical exam, often when the patient may not be able to give enough information
May often still need formal imaging to follow; a complement to the other workup

When the bedside scan is not the answer. A stable patient with a likely deep vein thrombosis (leg pain and swelling after knee replacement, no chest pain or shortness of breath, normal vital signs) “needs formal US, probably not POCUS in this case”. Point-of-care scanning earns its place when the question cannot wait, as in the patient in sudden, undifferentiated respiratory distress (20.10). Slides 64 and 65.

20.2 · Objective b — Its role beside the history and physical examination: indications, contraindications, procedures

Point-of-care ultrasound is an extension of the history and physical examination, used at the bedside for real-time answers, and a complement to the rest of the workup (20.1).

When and where it is indicatedWhen it is contraindicated (“not very often!”)
Trauma · Cardiac · Pulmonary · Vasculature · Ophthalmic · Renal · Gallbladder · Appendix · Scrotal · Obstetric/gynecologicGlobe rupture
Should not delay lifesaving measures
Some anatomical locations are not amenable to ultrasound

Common applications: cardiac, pulmonary, abdominal, soft tissue and vascular scanning; protocol-based scans (EFAST, RUSH, BLUE; 20.8, 20.7); and procedural guidance: central venous catheter insertion, paracentesis, thoracentesis. Vascular uses: central lines, ultrasound-guided IVs and DVTs.

Artery or vein? In general, arteries are pulsatile if compressed and have minimal compressibility; veins are completely compressible (unless filled with clot). This identifies structures, especially for vascular procedures. Slide 46.

20.4 · Objective d — Terminology I: the machine, the probe, orientation and echogenicity

TermWhat it means
UltrasoundSound above what human ears can hear (more than 20,000 hertz) used to create an image
Piezoelectric crystalsTiny crystals in the probe: a current makes them vibrate and send a sound wave; the returning echo vibrates them again, and that is converted to electricity, which the computer turns into a real-time image
FrequencyHow often sound waves are emitted. Higher frequency: better resolution, less depth. Lower frequency: better depth, less resolution. Short wavelength = high frequency
DepthHow far you are zoomed in
GainBrightness
Transducer selectionDifferent probes suit different sites: phased array, linear, curvilinear, endocavitary
Transducer movementsFan, rock, sweep, slide, rotate, compress
Indicator (probe marker, orientation marker)Orients you to the image. Keep it pointed to your left (the patient’s right); cardiology puts it opposite. It should match the orientation marker on the screen
PlanesTransverse (axial): indicator to your left, look through the patient; sagittal (longitudinal); coronal (frontal)

Echogenicity: how bright a tissue appears compared to another

TermLooksBecauseExamples
AnechoicBlackDoes not echo sound waves backFluid-filled structures
HypoechoicGrayReturns less echo than surrounding structuresLiver, spleen
HyperechoicWhiteDense; returns more echoes than surrounding structuresBone, stones
IsoechoicSame as its surroundingsSame density as surrounding structures

20.5 · Objective d — Terminology II: artifacts, modes and color Doppler

ArtifactWhat happensLook for
Acoustic shadowingHappens with hyperechoic structures; air can shadow too, “dirtier” than shadowing from boneA dark band behind ribs or stones
Posterior acoustic enhancementSound through a fluid-filled structure enhances the images deep to it, making them bright; it can obscure themBright area behind the bladder or gallbladder
Edge artifactA refraction artifact: sound waves “bend” off the edge of a fluid-filled structureA dark line off the curved edge
Mirror image artifactA reverberation artifact: sound hits a strong reflector, and the image is mirrored on the other side of itLiver copied above the diaphragm (normal)
Ring down artifactCaused by foci of airBright streaks, as with hepatic pneumobilia
Side lobe artifactSound reflected by a strong reflector outside the central beam appears as if it is in itEchoes inside the gallbladder lumen
A lines (reverberation)Horizontal repeats of the pleural lineThe normal lung
Comet tails (reverberation)Vertical hyperechoic lines; may come off calcifications, cholesterol, metalShort bright tails off the gallbladder wall
B lines (reverberation)Vertical hyperechoic lines in the lung from an air-water interfacePulmonary edema

Modes and color Doppler

ModeWhat it shows
B-mode (brightness)The standard view: shades of gray producing a two-dimensional image
M-mode (motion)One line of the B-mode image showing movement over time; common uses are fetal ultrasound and pneumothorax
Color DopplerDirection and velocity, represented by color. BART: Blue Away, Red Towards; perpendicular = black. Blue and red do not necessarily mean venous and arterial. It has its own artifacts (twinkle artifact, aliasing)

20.6 · Objective e.i — Cardiovascular assessment: the four windows

Cardiac scanning uses the phased array (cardiac) probe and four main viewing windows.

WindowHowWhat it is for
Parasternal long axisBeside the sternum; the heart laid out lengthwise (RV on top, LV, aorta, LA)Often the easiest view to obtain
Parasternal short axisRotate the probe 90 degrees so the marker is toward the patient’s right hipCross-section of the ventricles; good view of the mitral and aortic valves
Subxiphoid (subcostal)Probe under the xiphoid, just to the patient’s right of midline; uses the liver as a windowBest view for pericardial effusion, since most effusions are inferior
Apical four chamberAt the apex; sometimes difficult to obtainAll four chambers at once; important for RV dilation or strain. Rotate for a five chamber view that adds the aortic valve/LVOT

ConditionWhat the slides show
Pulmonary edemaB lines: vertical hyperechoic lines from an air-water interface

20.8 · Objective e.iii — Abdominal assessment: gallbladder, inferior vena cava, aorta and the protocols

Abdominal point-of-care targets: the gallbladder, the inferior vena cava, the aorta and many others.

Cholecystitis on ultrasound: a stone (with a posterior acoustic shadow), wall thickening and pericholecystic fluid, “plus sonographic murphy sign are signs of cholecystitis”.

20.10 · Objective f — Integrating the findings into the decision

The deck integrates the bedside scan with the history, the examination and the vital signs; it shows no laboratory values and no electrocardiogram, so nothing here goes beyond that. Its two cases set the principle:

The patientThe bedside reasoning
Sudden, undifferentiated respiratory distress in a patient with very high blood pressure, then diffuse crackles and dyspnea without chest painAn unstable, undifferentiated patient needs a bedside answer now; diffuse B lines with crackles and the blood pressure point to flash pulmonary edema (the slide’s answer: “Flash pulmonary edema/SCAPE”, SCAPE being sympathetic crashing acute pulmonary edema; she improved on BiPAP (bilevel positive airway pressure) and a nitroglycerin drip)
Stable leg pain and swelling after knee replacement, no chest pain or shortness of breath, normal vital signs“Likely DVT, but stable”: formal ultrasound, probably not point-of-care (Note: current practice also accepts a point-of-care compression ultrasound for a suspected deep vein thrombosis in trained hands; the slide’s answer, formal ultrasound, is what the exam keys.)

The protocols are integration built in: EFAST answers the trauma questions (free fluid, pericardial effusion, pneumothorax) in one pass; the inferior vena cava answers the volume question in a hypotensive patient; BLUE sorts acute dyspnea into edema, embolism, pneumonia, pneumothorax and obstructive disease.
