The one-line frame for the whole lecture. Human error at the bedside, not the laboratory, drives fatal reactions, so identification comes first. ABO antibodies are naturally occurring IgM that lyse cells inside the circulation within minutes; Rh antibodies are acquired IgG that clear cells in the spleen, gradually. Red cell and plasma compatibility run in opposite directions (O is the universal red cell donor, AB the universal plasma donor). The testing sequence is type and screen (ABO, Rh, unexpected antibodies) then crossmatch; the direct antiglobulin test separates the hemolytic reactions (positive) from the non-hemolytic ones (negative).

Abbreviations you will see on the slides and in orders: DAT (direct antiglobulin test, direct Coombs), IAT (indirect antiglobulin test, indirect Coombs), AHG (antihuman globulin, Coombs serum), T&S (type and screen), T&C (type and crossmatch), IS (immediate-spin crossmatch), HDFN (hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn), RhIG (Rh immune globulin, RhoGAM), AHTR (acute hemolytic transfusion reaction), DHTR (delayed hemolytic transfusion reaction), FNHTR (febrile non-hemolytic transfusion reaction), LDH (lactate dehydrogenase), DIC (disseminated intravascular coagulation), ATN (acute tubular necrosis), MRN (medical record number), FFP (fresh frozen plasma), MTP (massive transfusion protocol).

21.1 · Objective a — Why pre-transfusion testing matters: human error at the bedside

The clinical anchor case. A trauma patient in bay 1 receives a unit intended for bay 2 because of bedside misidentification. Within 5 minutes the patient develops rigors, severe flank pain, tachypnea, acute hypotension and dark port-wine urine. The root cause is a mislabeled sample (“wrong blood in tube”) or a failure of bedside two-person verification. Takeaway: fatal ABO incompatibility is almost universally clerical — the blood bank testing was accurate, but the blood was delivered to the wrong patient.

Core safety protocolWhat it requires
Bedside sample labelingLabel immediately at the bedside in front of the patient; never pre-label tubes or label away from the patient.
Two-identifier verificationMatch the patient name, medical record number (MRN), unit blood type and unit serial number directly against the wristband.
The critical first 15 minutesInfuse the first 25–50 mL slowly; severe acute hemolytic reactions manifest rapidly, after as little as 25 mL.
The second-sample ruleIf the patient has no prior verified blood type on record, a second separate sample must be drawn and tested to confirm the ABO/Rh type.

Cardinal rule 1 — human error drives mortality. Most fatal hemolytic reactions stem from bedside clerical errors, not laboratory testing. Verify patient identity and blood unit at the bedside.

21.2 · Objective b — ABO: carbohydrate antigens and naturally occurring IgM

Carbohydrate antigensNatural IgM antibodiesThe intravascular cascade
A and B antigens are oligosaccharides (terminal sugars) attached to cell membrane glycoproteins.
Group O expresses only the unmodified H antigen core structure (no A or B sugars).
Group A possesses N-acetylgalactosamine transferase.
Group B possesses D-galactosyltransferase.
Group AB expresses both A and B antigens simultaneously.		 Naturally occurring: individuals consistently form antibodies against the ABO antigens they lack, without prior transfusion.
Environmental priming: triggered during infancy by cross-reactive gut flora and food carbohydrates.
IgM dominance: a large pentameric structure, highly efficient at fixing complement (C1q binding).
The rule: you always produce antibodies against the ABO antigens absent from your own red blood cells.		 Complement activation: full assembly of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) directly in the circulation.
Immediate lysis: red cell rupture releases free hemoglobin into the plasma (hemoglobinemia).
Vasoactive storm: the anaphylatoxins C3a and C5a cause systemic vasodilation, hypotension and shock.
Renal and hemostatic crisis: free hemoglobin causes tubular precipitation / acute tubular necrosis; microvascular procoagulants trigger disseminated intravascular coagulation.

The slide spells the rule “Landeiner’s Rule”; the standard name is Landsteiner’s rule.

21.3 · Objective b — Compatibility: red cell and plasma rules run in opposite directions

The inverse compatibility rule. Red cell transfusions: group O is the universal red cell donor (no ABO antigens to react with recipient antibodies); group AB is the universal red cell recipient. Plasma transfusions: group AB is the universal plasma donor (its plasma lacks anti-A and anti-B); group O is the universal plasma recipient.

21.4 · Objective b — Emergency release: O-negative against O-positive in massive hemorrhage

O-negative: the scarce universal red cellO-positive: protocol stewardship
Only about 6–7% of the US population is O-negative.
A strict emergency reserve: mandatory for females of childbearing potential (<50 years old) with an unknown blood type in acute trauma.
Preventing alloimmunization: inadvertent RhD exposure in young females risks lifelong anti-D formation and fetal hydrops in future pregnancies.
Emergency release: sent uncrossmatched while the blood bank proceeds with rapid confirmatory testing.		 About 37–39% of the population is O-positive.
Massive transfusion protocol standard: used for adult males and post-menopausal females.
No hemolytic collapse: an Rh-negative male given O-positive red cells will NOT suffer immediate acute intravascular hemolysis.
Calculated risk: alloimmunization (anti-D formation) may occur, but survival of acute hemorrhagic shock takes clinical precedence.

21.5 · Objective c — The RhD antigen: a protein that needs prior exposure

RhD protein immunogenicityNo natural antibodiesExtravascular clearance
Unlike the ABO carbohydrates, RhD is a transmembrane structural protein.
High potency: the most immunogenic red cell antigen outside the ABO system.
Terminology: “Rh-positive” strictly denotes the presence of the RhD polypeptide; “Rh-negative” denotes its absence.
Distribution: about 85% of Caucasians and more than 90% of African Americans and Asians are RhD-positive.		 Sensitization required: Rh-negative individuals have ZERO pre-existing anti-D at baseline.
Exposure: transfusion of Rh-positive red cells or feto-maternal hemorrhage during pregnancy or delivery.
Anti-D is monomeric IgG (predominantly IgG1 and IgG3).
Delayed production: weeks to months for the primary IgM-to-IgG response to mature.		 IgG-coated red cells are recognized by splenic Fc-gamma receptors.
Opsonized cells are destroyed by macrophages in the reticuloendothelial system.
Gradual course: spherocyte formation, mild hyperbilirubinemia, low haptoglobin.
Absence of shock: rarely triggers acute complement-mediated intravascular collapse or disseminated intravascular coagulation.

21.6 · Objective c — Rh incompatibility: hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn, and Rh immune globulin

Pathophysiology of hemolytic disease of the fetus and newbornThe Rh immune globulin (RhoGAM) protocol
Sensitization: an RhD-negative mother carries an RhD-positive fetus; transplacental feto-maternal hemorrhage occurs during delivery or trauma.
Maternal alloimmunization: she mounts an immune response, producing high-affinity IgG anti-D.
Subsequent pregnancies: maternal IgG readily crosses the placenta via syncytiotrophoblast Fc receptors into the fetal circulation.
Fetal destruction: anti-D binds fetal red cells → splenic destruction → profound anemia, extramedullary hematopoiesis, high-output heart failure (hydrops fetalis).		 Mechanism: the given anti-D binds and masks fetal D-positive cells, clearing them before the maternal B cells mount an immune response.
Routine prophylaxis: at about 28 weeks of gestation to all non-sensitized RhD-negative mothers.
Post-partum dose: within 72 hours of delivering an RhD-positive neonate.
Potentially sensitizing events: must be given after miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy, abortion, amniocentesis or abdominal trauma.
Kleihauer-Betke test: quantifies the volume of fetal-maternal hemorrhage to calculate whether additional vials of Rh immune globulin are required.

Beyond the slides: anti-D is the IgG the deck teaches crossing the placenta; IgG anti-A and anti-B can also cross and cause ABO hemolytic disease of the newborn. The deck does not test this.

Cardinal rule 2 — ABO and Rh operate under distinct rules. ABO antibodies are naturally occurring IgM that cause rapid intravascular lysis. Rh antibodies are acquired IgG causing extravascular clearance.

21.9 · Objective d — The antibody screen (an indirect antiglobulin test) and antibody identification

The antibody screenAntibody identification and allocation
Purpose: identifies unexpected circulating IgG alloantibodies against clinically significant red cell antigens (for example Kell, Duffy, Kidd, Rh C/E).
Method: patient plasma is incubated in vitro with 2 to 3 group O reagent red cell vials expressing comprehensive antigen profiles.
Antihuman globulin is added to crosslink IgG-sensitized red cells, producing visible agglutination.
Negative screen: no unexpected red cell antibodies (about 95–98% of patients).
Positive screen: the patient has formed an alloantibody from prior transfusion, transplantation or pregnancy.		 Identification panel: when the screen is positive, an 11- to 16-cell panel is tested to map the antibody’s specificity.
High-risk specificities: anti-Kell (anti-K), anti-Kidd (anti-Jka/Jkb), anti-Duffy (anti-Fya), anti-Rh (anti-c, anti-E).
Antigen-negative sourcing: the blood bank must phenotypically screen donor units to find and crossmatch red cells lacking that antigen.
Clinical consequence: compatible units can take hours or days; order the type and screen well in advance of elective procedures.

21.12 · Objective d — Type and screen against type and crossmatch, and the 3-day specimen rule

Type and screenType and crossmatchThe 3-day specimen rule
Determines ABO, Rh, and tests plasma for unexpected alloantibodies.
Blood allocated: ZERO units assigned or held.
Setting: transfusion possible but not immediately expected (routine surgery, a stable gastrointestinal bleed).
Advantage: preserves inventory; if transfusion is needed, units can be crossmatched rapidly.		 Completes the type and screen and physically matches donor units to the patient’s serum.
Blood allocated: specific numbered units are tagged and RESERVED in the blood bank.
Setting: transfusion is planned, highly probable or actively ongoing.
Stewardship: held units are unavailable to other patients until explicitly released.		 The sample is valid for only 72 hours (3 days) if the patient was (1) transfused within the prior 3 months, (2) pregnant within the prior 3 months, or (3) has an uncertain clinical history.
Rationale: new alloantibodies can form rapidly following secondary exposure.
Impact: an expired sample requires an entirely new redraw and repeat testing.

In the recording the 3-day rule sounded universal (“regardless of what we do, there’s always a three-day specimen rule”, 31:00; “you have to repeat it again in three days, especially if you’ve transfused them”). The slide limits it to the three conditions above; the slide is what is keyed.

Cardinal rule 3 — understand the testing sequence. The type and screen checks the patient’s status; the crossmatch allocates specific donor units. A positive screen requires an antibody panel and a full antihuman globulin crossmatch.

21.14 · Objective e.i — Acute hemolytic transfusion reaction: intravascular collapse

Pathophysiology and presentationLaboratory fingerprint and management
Cause: ABO incompatibility (90%+ clerical error) triggering IgM complement-mediated intravascular lysis.
Timing: rapid onset, within minutes of starting (as little as 25 mL can be fatal).
Classic triad: (1) high fever and rigors; (2) flank or lower back pain (renal vasoconstriction); (3) red or dark urine (hemoglobinuria).
Severe progression: sense of impending doom, dyspnea, hypotension and shock, acute tubular necrosis, and catastrophic disseminated intravascular coagulation with bleeding from intravenous sites.		 Direct antiglobulin test: newly positive (anti-A or anti-B coating the remaining cells); it may be negative if all donor cells are instantly lysed.
Plasma and urine: pink or red plasma (hemoglobinemia) and red urine (hemoglobinuria).
Hemolysis profile: undetectable haptoglobin, markedly elevated LDH (lactate dehydrogenase), then a rise in indirect bilirubin.
Coagulation: disseminated intravascular coagulation labs: prolonged prothrombin time (and INR) and partial thromboplastin time, elevated D-dimer, decreased fibrinogen and platelets.
Renal: rising urea nitrogen and serum creatinine; hyperkalemia.
Management: aggressive intravenous hydration (target urine output 100–200 mL/hr), vasopressors for shock, maintain renal perfusion.

In the recording, red urine was explained as bilirubin in the urine (35:03); the slide calls the red or dark urine hemoglobinuria, and the slide is what is keyed. She also mentioned irradiated, leukoreduced red cells for vulnerable patients (37:58); that product is not on the slides.

21.15 · Objective e.ii — Delayed hemolytic transfusion reaction: anamnestic IgG clearance

Pathophysiology and time courseLaboratory findings and the sickle cell danger
Mechanism: a secondary (anamnestic) IgG response against minor red cell antigens (Kidd, Rh, Duffy, Kell).
Timeline: typically 3 to 30 days after transfusion (peak 5–15 days).
The anamnestic trap: the historical antibody was undetectable on the pre-transfusion screen; re-exposure to the donor antigen stimulates rapid IgG production.
Clinical features: often indolent or silent; an unexplained drop in hemoglobin (or failure to rise by 1 g/dL per unit), low-grade fever, mild jaundice.
Extravascular clearance: red cells phagocytosed by splenic macrophages; hemodynamic collapse is rare.		 Diagnostic labs: a newly positive direct antiglobulin test (IgG coating the transfused cells); a newly positive antibody screen with a new alloantibody identified (for example anti-Jka); elevated LDH (lactate dehydrogenase) and indirect bilirubin, reduced haptoglobin; spherocytes on the peripheral smear.
Sickle cell disease alert: a high risk of a delayed reaction mimicking a vaso-occlusive pain crisis; hemoglobin A falls disproportionately.
Hyperhemolysis syndrome: antibodies destroy BOTH donor cells AND the patient’s own red cells; reticulocytopenia develops; further transfusions can be fatal.

21.16 · Objective e.iii — Febrile non-hemolytic transfusion reaction: a diagnosis of exclusion

Pathogenesis and featuresWorkup and management
Incidence: common (about 1% of transfusions; higher with platelets).
Dual cause: (1) cytokines (interleukin-1, interleukin-6, tumor necrosis factor alpha) accumulated in stored plasma; (2) recipient antibodies against donor white blood cell (HLA, human leukocyte antigen) antigens.
Definition: a temperature rise of at least 1 °C (1.8 °F) to at least 38 °C (100.4 °F) during or within 4 hours of transfusion.
Associated: chills, rigors, headache, malaise.
Hemodynamic stability: blood pressure and breathing stay completely stable.		 Diagnosis of exclusion — all laboratory workup is completely normal: the direct antiglobulin test is negative; free plasma and urine hemoglobin are negative; haptoglobin, LDH (lactate dehydrogenase) and bilirubin stay normal; the blood bag Gram stain and culture are negative.
Rule out sepsis: if the fever is 2 °C or more, the patient is hypotensive, or the product was platelets, work up for bacterial sepsis.
Treatment: antipyretics (acetaminophen); meperidine for severe shaking rigors.
Prevention: pre-storage leukoreduction dramatically reduces rates. Routine premedication is discouraged.

21.17 · Objective e.iv — Allergic transfusion reaction: the one reaction with a restart rule

Pathophysiology and featuresThe exclusive restart protocol
Mechanism: type I hypersensitivity mediated by preformed recipient IgE against soluble donor plasma proteins (localized mast cell activation).
Frequency: common (1–3% of transfusions); most frequent with plasma-rich products (fresh frozen plasma, platelets).
Presentation: pruritus, localized urticaria (hives), erythema, flushing.
Strict negative criteria: NO fever or chills; NO respiratory distress or wheezing; NO hypotension or tachycardia.
Laboratory evaluation: normal. Direct antiglobulin test negative; no hemolysis markers.		 1. Pause the infusion temporarily while evaluating.
2. Give an H1-blocker antihistamine (diphenhydramine 25–50 mg intravenous or oral).
3. Re-evaluate at 20–30 minutes: verify the complete absence of systemic or cardiopulmonary signs.
4. The only unit that can be restarted: if symptoms are strictly confined to the skin and completely resolve, the SAME unit may be restarted at a slower rate within the 4-hour hang time limit.
5. Critical red flag: if fever, dyspnea, wheezing or hypotension occur, DO NOT RESTART; escalate to the full reaction protocol.

The restart rule in one line: the allergic reaction is the only transfusion reaction in which the unit can be restarted — when the symptoms are confined to the skin and fully resolve after the pause and the antihistamine, the same unit may be restarted at a slower rate within the 4-hour hang time; with fever, dyspnea, wheezing or hypotension it is never restarted.

21.18 · Objective e.v — Anaphylactic transfusion reaction: anti-IgA and epinephrine

Pathophysiology and severe symptomsDiagnostics and emergency protocol
Classic cause: a patient with severe congenital IgA deficiency (<0.05 mg/dL) who has formed circulating anti-IgA antibodies.
Trigger: infusion of donor plasma containing normal IgA.
Timing: sudden, within seconds to minutes after only 1 to 5 mL of product.
Presentation (NO FEVER): laryngeal edema, stridor, bronchospasm, dyspnea; rapid circulatory collapse, severe hypotension, shock; angioedema, generalized flushing, nausea and vomiting.
Key contrast: absence of fever distinguishes anaphylaxis from septic shock and acute hemolytic reactions.		 First-line: intramuscular epinephrine into the anterolateral thigh (adult 0.3 to 0.5 mg of 1 mg/mL; pediatric 0.01 mg/kg, maximum 0.5 mg). Never delay epinephrine for antihistamines or steroids.
Supportive care: high-flow oxygen, airway protection, aggressive intravenous crystalloid boluses.
Diagnostic testing: serum tryptase (drawn within 1–3 hours); quantitative IgA level and anti-IgA antibody titers.
Future transfusions: must be washed red cells (strips plasma IgA) or products from verified IgA-deficient donors.
