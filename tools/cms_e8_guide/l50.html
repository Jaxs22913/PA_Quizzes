4.1 · Chronic lymphocytic leukemia: definition, pathogenesis and risk factors

The deck opens the lymphoid half under one heading, Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia/Small Lymphocytic Lymphoma (CLL/SLL) (slide 3): the same disease, called a leukemia in the blood and a lymphoma in the nodes.

  • Definition (slide 4): chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) is a proliferative leukemia of mature B-cell lymphocytes in blood, bone marrow, lymph nodes, and spleen. Rare mature T-cell leukemias are called T-cell prolymphocytic leukemia (T-PLL).
  • Epidemiology (slide 4): “Most common adult form of leukemia” (see the note above), 1% of all new cancer cases in the United States; median age of diagnosis 70 years.
  • Pathogenesis (slide 5): mutagenesis occurs in mature B cells, giving a clonal presentation. 80% of cases come from loss or addition of chromosomal material in one of four chromosomal abnormalities: deletion 13q, deletion 11q, trisomy 12, deletion 17p.
  • Risk factors (slide 6): no known occupational or environmental risk factors; age (median age at onset 70); most common leukemia in the Western Hemisphere and more common in Eastern Europeans and those of Jewish descent; twice as common in males.

4.2 · Chronic lymphocytic leukemia: presentation, work-up and differential

★ “High white count, painless lymphadenopathy, underline, underline, star, star” (slide 7)

“If you see lymphocytosis and you see the painless lymphadenopathy, you gotta think CLL.” Then slide 8: “Flow cytometry is the most important workup we can do here”; CD19 plus CD5 “is pathognomonic for CLL. That’s why you don’t need the bone marrow biopsy.”

  • Signs and symptoms (slide 7):
    • Frequently diagnosed while asymptomatic, on routine blood work.
    • Painless lymphadenopathy (50–90% of cases): localized or generalized, firm, mobile.
    • 5–10% of patients develop B symptoms (weight loss, night sweats, fevers).
    • Anemia: fatigue. Thrombocytopenia: easy bruising, bleeding. Ineffective white blood cells (WBCs): infections.
    • Exam: splenomegaly (~40%), less commonly hepatomegaly.
    • Labs: lymphocytosis (must be >5K; often >100K); frequently anemia.
  • Work-up and diagnosis (slide 8):
    • Diagnosis is confirmed by flow cytometry (underlined on the slide), which identifies a clonal B-cell population.
    • Peripheral blood: absolute lymphocyte count (ALC) > 5K for 3 months, or ALC < 5K with lymphadenopathy or other symptoms; coexpression of CD19 and CD5 on the lymphocytes.
    • Cytogenetics are important for prognosis and have some treatment considerations.
    • Usually does not require a bone marrow biopsy for diagnosis.
  • Differential diagnosis (slide 9):
    • Monoclonal B lymphocytosis: elevated ALC but <5K lymphocytes, with no lymphadenopathy, organomegaly, cytopenias, or other symptoms; progresses to chronic lymphocytic leukemia at 1–2% per year.
    • Atypical lymphocytosis with some infections, including pertussis.
    • Other lymphoproliferative disorders: Waldenström macroglobulinemia, hairy cell leukemia, lymphomas in leukemic phase; told apart on morphology and immunophenotype.

4.3 · Chronic lymphocytic leukemia: Rai staging

  • Rai classification (slide 10): historically used to describe prognostic groups. It does not require advanced imaging and can be done with the physical exam and labs. It may be used to decide the indication for treatment (treatment begins with symptomatic disease or rapid progression).
  • More contemporary prognosis uses the CLL International Prognostic Index (CLL-IPI), named on the slide; its items are not shown.

4.4 · Chronic lymphocytic leukemia: management, prognosis and patient education

★ Richter transformation: “you should highlight or know about this” (slide 12)

And on observation (slides 11, 13): “an isolated white count alone does not equal treatment”; a count that doubles within six months is treated.

  • Management (slide 11):
    • Asymptomatic patients may be observed (watch and wait) until evidence of rapid progression (doubling of the lymphocyte count in < 6 months) or disease-related symptoms.
    • Targeted therapies: Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors, usually continuous if monotherapy: ibrutinib, acalabrutinib, zanubrutinib. BCL-2 inhibitors, combined with monoclonal antibodies for a fixed duration: venetoclax. Monoclonal antibodies: rituximab, obinutuzumab, ofatumumab.
    • A trend toward combination therapies.
  • Prognosis (slide 12): 5-year relative survival ~88% (beginning in 2013), much improved over historical chemotherapy because of novel agents. Risk of Richter transformation: chronic lymphocytic leukemia transforming to an aggressive B-cell lymphoma, with poor outcomes. With novel therapies in development (CAR-T (chimeric antigen receptor T-cell) therapy, bispecific antibodies) and the elderly age at onset, many patients will die with chronic lymphocytic leukemia rather than from it.
  • Patient education (slide 13): requires referral to and management by a hematologist/oncologist. Unlikely to require hospitalization; treatment is likely based on patient preferences and mutations: pill therapy, once or twice daily and indefinite; infusion + pill, time-limited but more intensive; combination therapies under development. Follow-ups for lab monitoring and treatment, but a very good long-term prognosis is likely.

4.5 · Non-Hodgkin lymphoma: definition and the indolent lymphomas

  • Definition (slide 15): non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) is a diverse group of malignancies of lymphoid tissues derived from B cells, T cells, or NK (natural killer) cells that present frequently with lymphadenopathy. 4% of all new cancer diagnoses in the United States. 85% are B-cell; 15% are T-cell or NK-cell.
  • The range (slide 15): from very aggressive (Burkitt, diffuse large B-cell, T-cell leukemia/lymphoma) to indolent (follicular, marginal zone, chronic lymphocytic leukemia/small lymphocytic lymphoma).

Follicular lymphoma (slide 16):

  • The most common indolent histology of non-Hodgkin lymphoma; typically an indolent presentation with waxing and waning painless lymphadenopathy.
  • Caused by the chromosomal translocation t(14;18), which places the BCL-2 gene on chromosome 14, preventing cell death.
  • Microscopy often shows clusters of packed follicles.
  • Frequently no treatment required (watch and wait), but may be treated with intermittent low-intensity therapy if symptomatic; high-risk follicular lymphoma may need more aggressive chemotherapies and immunotherapies.

Marginal zone lymphomas (slide 17):

  • 5–17% of all non-Hodgkin lymphoma, most commonly the extranodal subtype (MALT). More common in older adults.
  • Subtypes: extranodal or mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphoma, the most common, involving organs with mucosa (gastrointestinal tract, eyes, lungs); gastric MALT: 2/3 of cases associated with H. pylori (Helicobacter pylori). Nodal marginal zone. Splenic marginal zone: associated with hepatitis C.

Lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma (LPL) (slide 18):

  • Also known as Waldenström macroglobulinemia (WM). MYD88 mutation frequently found (~90% of cases).
  • Affects both lymphoplasmacytoid cells and plasma cells (terminally differentiated B cells), giving excess IgM production: a large antibody that causes hyperviscosity, often blurred vision, headache, rarely a cerebrovascular accident (CVA, stroke).
  • Good prognosis overall: relative 5-year survival 78%.
  • Risk factors: genetic (familial) and environmental (farming, pesticides); out loud: “kind of always choose that on the test if you see it.”

4.6 · Non-Hodgkin lymphoma: the aggressive and T-cell lymphomas

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) (slide 19):

  • The most common non-Hodgkin lymphoma in adults; many subtypes and varieties based on location, mutations and infectious associations.
  • Symptoms: a fast-growing mass/swelling and/or B symptoms (fever, fatigue, night sweats, weight loss).
  • Risk factors: older age, family history, autoimmune conditions, infections, environmental.
  • Education (slide 34): often treatable and curable with multi-agent chemotherapy (the slide’s cure percentage was corrected out loud; see “Where the slides are loose or disagree” at the top).

4.7 · Non-Hodgkin lymphoma: risk factors, presentation, work-up and staging

★ “On a test you would probably see HIV, EBV, HTLV-1, H. pylori” (slide 23)

Work-up (slide 25): “Excisional biopsy is the key for diagnosis. If you ever see somebody with a negative fine needle aspiration, don’t trust it”; LDH (lactate dehydrogenase) “is a super important test to get on anybody with a lymphoma.” Staging (slide 26): “It’s all based on the diaphragm, so this is important.” A large mediastinal mass in a young woman with chest pressure: “That’s a lymphoma until proven otherwise” (slide 24).

  • Risk factors (slide 23): higher incidence in White individuals and in developed countries; male gender; immunosuppression: prior immunotherapy, chemotherapy, radiation, or malignancy; prior transplantation; autoimmune conditions (systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), Sjögren, celiac); Crohn’s; environmental exposures, including DDT (farming); chronic infections: H. pylori gastritis (MALT), HTLV-1, hepatitis C, HIV (human immunodeficiency virus), EBV (Epstein-Barr virus), Lyme disease, HHV-8 (human herpesvirus 8) (she has never seen a Lyme or HHV-8 lymphoma); family history.
  • Signs and symptoms (slide 24):
    • Peripheral lymphadenopathy in >2/3 of patients.
    • Other: B symptoms (weight loss, night sweats, fever of unknown origin), rash, hypersensitivity reactions, fatigue, pruritus, malaise, ascites, effusion.
    • About half develop extranodal disease (lymphoma outside the lymph nodes). Central nervous system (CNS) lymphoma: headaches, lethargy, neurologic defects that mirror primary CNS malignancies.
    • Primary mediastinal B-cell lymphoma: a large mediastinal mass with cough and an abnormal chest X-ray.
  • Work-up and diagnosis (slide 25):
    • Diagnosis is made by excisional lymph node biopsy: reserve for significant enlargement (2 cm), persistent, and progressive increase in size. Avoid fine needle aspiration (FNA): an excisional biopsy is required to see the lymph node architecture, and FNA may miss the diagnosis.
    • Imaging (usually CT (computed tomography) or PET (positron emission tomography)) is required to characterize and stage.
    • Also a complete blood count (CBC) (cytopenias with bone marrow involvement), chemistries (electrolytes, particularly ruling out tumor lysis syndrome) and LDH (lactate dehydrogenase) (rapid proliferation); bone marrow biopsy.
    • High-grade lymphomas, including Burkitt, require a lumbar puncture.
  • Staging (slide 26): by the Lugano classification, a modified version of the older Ann Arbor system.

    • A medical emergency from rapid tumor breakdown and release of cellular products (potassium, phosphorus, uric acid).

    • Aggressive lymphomas: often treatable and often cured with chemotherapy (the slide’s percentage was corrected out loud; see the top note), 5-year survival approximately 60–70%.

4.9 · Burkitt lymphoma

★ “If a pathologist ever says starry sky, you think Burkitt’s ... you’ll definitely see that on a test” (slide 32)

And: “The endemic region of this is Sub-Saharan Africa. You’ll probably see this on a test. It is strongly, strongly, strongly associated with EBV.”

  • What it is (slide 31): a very aggressive lymphoma variant associated with MYC gene upregulation, which stimulates cell growth and division.
  • Presentation (slide 31): very rapidly growing, often painless swelling (changes visible over the course of one day); commonly B symptoms (fever, night sweats, weight loss).
  • Three types (slide 32):
    • Endemic: largely sub-Saharan Africa and New Guinea, strongly associated with EBV (Epstein-Barr virus).
    • Sporadic: typically the United States and Western Europe, more in males, almost always age < 35.
    • Immunodeficiency-associated: associated with HIV/AIDS.
  • Pathology (slide 32): the “starry sky” appearance: sheets of lymphoma with benign macrophages clearing out dying lymphoma cells.
  • Management (slide 33): treatment must start promptly, with emergent referral to a hematologist. The doubling time is extremely fast: the tumor may be seen expanding within the same 24 hours. Excellent long-term prognosis if identified early and treated with multi-agent chemotherapy: >90% in children, 50–90% in adults. Caution when starting therapy because of tumor lysis syndrome. A high-grade lymphoma, so the work-up includes a lumbar puncture (slide 25); Burkitt lymphoma requires rapid transfer of care (slide 34).

4.10 · Hodgkin lymphoma: definition, risk factors and presentation

★ “Highlight, underline, star. Make a note of this” (slide 36)

“When you see the Reed-Sternberg cells, I want you to automatically think of Hodgkin’s lymphoma.” And slide 38: node pain after drinking (“every Friday night when I go out ... for a drink, it really is acting up”) “if you hear this on a test” points to Hodgkin.

  • Definition (slide 36): a type of B-cell lymphoma characterized by few malignant cells (Reed-Sternberg cells) among numerous immune effector cells. Reed-Sternberg cells are binucleated, neoplastic B cells.
  • ~10–15% of all lymphoma diagnoses worldwide (slide 36).
  • Marked by contiguous spread through lymph nodes (an orderly fashion) (slide 36).
  • Risk factors (slide 37): bimodal incidence: adults in their 20s and adults > 60 years old; same-sex siblings have a 10× increased risk; EBV (Epstein-Barr virus) suspected (identified in 40–50% of cases); others including HIV, HHV-6 (human herpesvirus 6) and TB (tuberculosis).
  • Signs and symptoms (slide 38):
    • Painless, asymptomatic lymphadenopathy (70%), usually cervical, but could be supraclavicular or mediastinal.
    • Pain may worsen with alcohol consumption.
    • Mediastinal mass on chest X-ray.
    • Splenomegaly (~30%), hepatomegaly sometimes.
    • Pruritus, may be very intense if present.

4.12 · Plasma cell disorders and multiple myeloma: pathogenesis and risk factors

  • Plasma cell disorders (slide 44): a spectrum of malignant and premalignant conditions arising from terminally differentiated B cells (plasma cells), ranging from MGUS (monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance): asymptomatic, low risk of progression, to malignant myeloma: an incurable malignancy. Multiple myeloma is 10% of all hematologic malignancies.
  • Pathogenesis of multiple myeloma (slide 45):
    • Normal plasma cells produce antibodies; clonal evolution of myeloma produces a single, overproduced antibody type, most commonly IgG.
    • It activates osteoclasts, releasing calcium into the bloodstream.
    • Renal dysfunction often comes from light chain and calcium phosphate deposition.
    • Anemia is multifactorial: renal dysfunction and bone marrow involvement.
  • Risk factors (slide 46): age (risk rises with age; most diagnoses over 65); race: African Americans at higher risk than other races; family history; prior MGUS or SMM (smoldering multiple myeloma); environmental exposures: radiation, chemical, occupational.

4.14 · Multiple myeloma: differential, diagnosis and R-ISS staging

★ “Myeloma though, you only need 10. So remember that for your test too” (slide 50)

Acute leukemia needs 20% marrow blasts (Lecture 49); myeloma needs only 10% plasma cells. And: “You’ll probably want to remember Bence Jones protein”: a patient who is anemic, with bone pain, renal failure and Bence Jones protein has myeloma.

  • Differential diagnosis (slide 49):
    • Multiple myeloma must be told from smoldering myeloma (a more indolent process, often monitored) and MGUS (asymptomatic) (4.16).
    • Waldenström macroglobulinemia often produces an IgM monoclonal protein, with a different pathophysiology and treatment (4.5).
    • Others with a paraprotein: chronic lymphocytic leukemia/small lymphocytic lymphoma, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, amyloidosis.
    • Reactive hypergammaglobulinemia (cirrhosis, chronic inflammation).
  • Work-up and diagnosis (slide 50):
    • Diagnosis: ≥ 10% bone marrow plasma cells or a biopsy-proven plasmacytoma, + 1 or more CRAB feature.
    • Monoclonal protein on SPEP (serum protein electrophoresis) is characteristic.
    • Positive urine monoclonal protein: “Bence Jones protein”.
    • Additional work-up: CBC, metabolic/electrolyte panel; serum protein electrophoresis; 24-hour urine protein electrophoresis; bone marrow biopsy with flow; light chains (kappa and lambda): check the ratio; serum beta-2 microglobulin (printed “macroglobulin”); imaging for bone lesions, classically whole-body CT.
  • Staging (slide 51): the R-ISS (Revised International Staging System) classification, historically used to describe prognostic groups; characterized by chromosomal abnormalities, proliferative status and the impact on normal protein production. While prognostic, it does not broadly change treatment decisions (out loud: “we don’t use staging too much in myeloma”; “If you’re not stage one or stage three, then you’re stage two”).

4.15 · Multiple myeloma: management, prognosis and patient education

  • Goal of treatment (slide 52): as myeloma is by definition a symptomatic condition and is considered incurable, the goal is maintaining prolonged remissions to improve survival and maintain quality of life.
  • Management (slide 53):
    • Newly diagnosed patients: a 3- or 4-drug regimen with a proteasome inhibitor and immunomodulatory drug backbone with steroids.
    • If a transplant candidate: autologous transplant improves overall survival, as does maintenance therapy after transplant.
    • Most patients will relapse and will need continuous therapy to maintain disease control.
    • Novel therapies (CAR-T, bispecifics, antibody-drug conjugates, other inhibitors) are currently used for relapsed myeloma.
  • Prognosis (slide 54): 5-year relative survival approximately 60% (2018–2022 data). Each line of therapy gives shorter remissions, and the disease remains incurable/fatal. Novel therapies (CAR-T, bispecific antibodies) may change the landscape; recent studies suggest newly diagnosed patients today may survive > 10 years.
  • Patient education (slide 55): referral to and management by a hematologist/oncologist, ideally a myeloma expert; treatment is likely lifelong, with periods of remission (disease control) followed by worsening symptoms; currently incurable (may change during the patient’s life); life-long follow-ups for lab monitoring and treatment.

4.16 · MGUS and smoldering myeloma

  • MGUS, monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined (the slide: “unknown”) significance (slide 56): a clonal proliferation, a low-level plasma cell disorder; by definition, asymptomatic. Definition: M-protein < 3 g/dL; clonal bone marrow plasma cells < 10%; no myeloma-defining characteristics. Risk of progression to myeloma ~1% per year.

Rate of progression approximately 10% per year, but some high-risk groups have a >50% risk of progression within 2 years.

Definition
An intermediate-stage clonal plasma cell disorder: M-protein >3 g/dL AND 10–60% bone marrow plasma cells, no myeloma-defining events (slide 57).

Not covered in the lecture.

Not covered in the lecture.

M-protein >3 g/dL and 10–60% marrow plasma cells without anemia, bone lesions or renal failure (slide 57).

Progression ~10% per year; some high-risk groups >50% within 2 years (slide 57).

4.17 · Across the lifespan: what the deck says by age

The deck has no population slide; these are the age points it does make (syllabus objectives 2 and 4).

PopulationWhat the deck says
InfantsNot covered in the lecture.
ChildrenBurkitt lymphoma: endemic type in sub-Saharan Africa and New Guinea (Epstein-Barr virus), the jaw swelling pictured in a child; long-term prognosis >90% in children with early multi-agent chemotherapy (slides 31–33).
Adolescents and young adultsHodgkin lymphoma: first peak in adults in their 20s (slide 37). Sporadic Burkitt: almost always under 35, more in males (slide 32).
AdultsDiffuse large B-cell lymphoma is the most common non-Hodgkin lymphoma in adults (slide 19); Burkitt prognosis 50–90% in adults (slide 33); mantle cell median age 60–70, more in men (slide 20).
ElderlyChronic lymphocytic leukemia: median age at diagnosis 70; with the elderly age at onset, many patients die with it rather than from it (slides 4, 6, 12). Marginal zone lymphoma: more common in older adults (slide 17). Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma: older age a risk factor (slide 19). Hodgkin lymphoma: second peak over 60, with inferior results in older patients (slides 37, 42). Non-Hodgkin lymphoma: age > 60 is a poor prognostic factor (slide 30). Multiple myeloma: risk rises with age, most diagnosed over 65 (slide 46).

Referral, education and prognosis in one place: every disease on the deck goes to a hematologist/oncologist (chronic lymphocytic leukemia, slide 13; non-Hodgkin lymphoma “ideally a lymphoma expert” and Burkitt “rapid transfer of care”, slide 34; myeloma “ideally myeloma expert”, slide 55). Chronic lymphocytic leukemia: outpatient, pill or infusion + pill, very good long-term prognosis (slide 13). Non-Hodgkin lymphoma: hospitalization only for aggressive disease with electrolyte abnormalities; fluids and allopurinol on starting treatment (slide 34). Hodgkin lymphoma: >90% alive at 5 years (slide 42). Myeloma: lifelong treatment, remissions then worsening, incurable for now, life-long follow-up (slide 55).
