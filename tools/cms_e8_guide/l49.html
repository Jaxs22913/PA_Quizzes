3.1 · Normal hematopoiesis and clonality

★ “I want you to ingrain this in your brains ... It cannot emphasize enough, learn this.” (slide 5)

The hematopoiesis tree is the map for the whole lecture: where the clone starts on it decides the disease (3.2).

  • Normal hematopoiesis (slide 5) begins in the bone marrow with hematopoietic stem cells, which differentiate into the myeloid and lymphoid progenitors and further differentiate into the terminally differentiated cell lines.
    • Myeloid line (from the myeloid progenitor cell): megakaryocyte (→ platelets), eosinophil, basophil, erythrocytes, monocyte (→ dendritic cell and macrophage), neutrophil.
    • Lymphoid line (from the lymphoid progenitor cell): T cell, B cell, NK (natural killer) cell; the B cell gives rise to the plasma cell.
  • What the cells do (slide 5): immunity (neutrophils, NK cells, lymphocytes), oxygen exchange (erythrocytes), the clotting cascade (platelets), and many other functions.
  • Clonality and tumorigenesis (slide 6): cancer development is generally believed to be a multi-stage process; the initial step is an oncogenic mutation in a single somatic cell (clone). Mutations that cause cancer give the cell clonal advantages, so the mutant clone expands and accumulates additional genetic alterations, resulting in an irreversible, invasive, proliferative disease.

3.2 · Hematologic malignancies: definition, families, epidemiology and risk factors

  • Definition (slide 7): cancers originating from blood-forming tissues or the lymphatic system, resulting from mutation in and proliferation of a hematologic clone (monoclonal).
  • Classification (slide 7): leukemias, lymphomas and plasma cell disorders; the underlying cell of origin determines the type (lymphoid or myeloid). On the slide’s diagram: the acute leukemias circle the stem cell and the early progenitors; the chronic leukemias circle the mature cells; the lymphomas circle the lymphoid progenitor and the T, B and NK cells; myeloma and other plasma cell disorders circle the plasma cell.
  • Epidemiology (slide 8): new cases of leukemia, lymphoma and myeloma were estimated at 9.4% of all new cancer cases in the United States in 2025; about 1.8 million people in the United States are living with or in remission from a hematologic malignancy; hematologic malignancies cause an estimated 9.1% of cancer-related deaths (the slide’s pie chart: lymphoma 46%, leukemia 35%, myeloma 19% of the 192,070 estimated new cases).
  • Risk factors (slide 9):
    • Environmental factors: radiation and specific chemical exposures (like benzene).
    • Genetic factors: family history is important; some hematologic malignancies have a higher incidence with trisomies or other genetic disorders.
    • Autoimmune disorders: may be secondary to intrinsic immunosuppression or to treatments.
    • They vary by disease but broadly increase in incidence with older age; the few exceptions include acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) and some kinds of lymphomas.
    • The slide’s picture adds lifestyle factors (smoking, poor diet and toxins) and age (“risk increases with age due to cumulative genetic damage”).

3.4 · The major categories: leukemias, lymphomas and multiple myeloma

★ “If you’re ever taking a test and you see a Reed Sternberg cell, they want you to remember Hodgkin lymphoma.” (slide 15)

And “I want you to make sure you’re writing down what are B symptoms”: fevers, drenching night sweats, weight loss (slides 15, 22).

  • Leukemia (slide 14): a progressive malignant condition of the bone marrow and other blood-forming organs resulting in increased numbers of immature and/or abnormal leukocytes.
    • Acute leukemias arise from more immature, progenitor white blood cells (WBCs) and cause rapid proliferation of this clone.
    • Chronic leukemias involve proliferation of mature malignant white blood cells.
    • Because one dysfunctional white cell line proliferates, production of the other cell lines falls: anemia, thrombocytopenia, and most commonly significant leukocytosis from the proliferating cancer.
  • Lymphomas (slide 15): malignancies arising from lymphoid tissue, often lymph nodes but sometimes the spleen or thymus; over 100 subtypes; historically split into Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin.
    • Non-Hodgkin lymphoma: 90% of all lymphomas, both B-cell and T-cell lymphomas.
    • Hodgkin lymphoma: 10% of all lymphomas, characterized by few malignant cells (Reed-Sternberg cells) with numerous immune effector cells.
    • Manifestations: often enlarged lymph nodes (lymphadenopathy), night sweats, weight loss and/or fevers.
  • Multiple myeloma (slide 16): a malignancy of terminally differentiated B-cells, called plasma cells. Because plasma cells produce antibodies, myeloma is characterized by excess production of a single antibody (monoclonal antibody) called an M protein. Manifestations: bone pain, anemia, renal dysfunction (with hypercalcemia, together the CRAB features) and increased infections from immune compromise. (Lymphoma and myeloma are taught in Lecture 50, section 4.)

3.5 · Acute lymphoblastic leukemia: definition and pathogenesis

★ “Remember that ALL is associated with this BCR-ABL mutation ... chromosome nine and 22. ... Write it down.” (slide 20)

And “remember that this is a bimodal distribution ... ALL in children and then older adults” (slide 18).

  • Definition (slide 18): acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is a leukemia originating with the lymphoid progenitor/stem cells: very immature cells with a rapid ability to proliferate.
  • Bimodal distribution (slide 18):
    • The most common malignancy under the age of 15; in the United States the 5-year survival is >90%.
    • Adults: peak incidence around 50 years of age; outcomes for patients over 40 years old are poor (~30–40% achieve long-term remission).
  • Pathogenesis (slide 19): mutagenesis occurs at the lymphoblast stage, at various stages of differentiation into B or T lymphocytes.
    • B-cell precursor ALL often presents in a leukemic phase: primarily blasts and marrow involvement.
    • T-cell ALL is rarer, usually with lymphoma-like characteristics, and may involve the thymus.
    • Subtype: lymphoblastic lymphoma, immature and aggressive but with more lymphoma features.
  • Genetics (slide 20):
    • Particularly in children: often excess copies of a chromosome (hyperdiploidy), a favorable prognosis.
    • In adults, the most common mutation is the BCR-ABL mutation, caused by translocation of chromosomes 9 and 22 (the Philadelphia chromosome).
    • 1/3 of both adult and pediatric patients have no chromosome abnormalities: mutation analysis is needed, but outcomes are relatively favorable.

3.6 · Acute lymphoblastic leukemia: risk factors, presentation, work-up and diagnosis

★ “This is important, write this down. The way to ... definitively diagnose [leukemia] is 20% or greater blast in the bone marrow.” (slide 25)

And the lumbar puncture: “the big one I would want you to remember here. ALL has a high propensity for going into the spinal fluid” (slides 23, 25).

  • Risk factors (slide 21): radiation exposure (prior radiation for cancer treatment; substantial risk for those exposed to the atomic bomb in Japan); chemical exposures (benzene, certain chemotherapies); viral infections (EBV (Epstein-Barr virus), HTLV-1 (human T-lymphotropic virus type 1; Caribbean countries)); genetic syndromes (Down syndrome, Fanconi anemia, others; ?family history); age (children under 5 and adults over 50); male gender.
  • Signs and symptoms (slide 22): symptoms of both excessive immature white cell (blast) production and lymphoid (nodal) characteristics.
    • Anemia: fatigue, weakness, lightheadedness, shortness of breath, pale skin.
    • Thrombocytopenia: bruising (often petechiae or purpura), bleeding.
    • Ineffective white cells: infections.
    • B-symptoms: weight loss, fever, night sweats, loss of appetite.
    • >50% present with lymphadenopathy, hepatomegaly or splenomegaly.
    • Bone or joint pain from very excessive marrow production of leukemia cells.
  • Labs (slide 22): anemia, thrombocytopenia, and either leukopenia or marked leukocytosis; white count often > 30K, sometimes much higher, but 30% have neutropenia. A mediastinal mass may be seen on the chest X-ray in T-cell ALL.
  • Work-up (slide 23): the diagnosis is confirmed by flow cytometry and bone marrow biopsy.
    • Flow cytometry identifies the markers of the leukemia and whether it is B-cell or T-cell.
    • Bone marrow biopsy shows the degree of cancer infiltration of the marrow, allows precise chromosome and mutation testing, and gives clues to the impact on normal hematopoiesis.
    • Also: imaging for the degree of spread, other blood tests (counts, electrolytes, coagulation) and lumbar puncture.
  • Differential diagnosis (slide 24): other aggressive malignancies, including acute myeloid leukemia (AML); lymphoproliferative diseases like chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), aggressive lymphomas; and hairy cell leukemia. Pathologists differentiate these with the clinical presentation. ALL and lymphoblastic lymphoma are difficult to tell apart but are treated similarly. Atypical lymphocytosis can occur with some infections, including mononucleosis and pertussis; flow cytometry separates these by the clonal nature of ALL.
  • Diagnosis (slide 25):
    • Defined by blast cells of lymphoid origin ≥ 20% in bone marrow.
    • Often diffuse marrow and peripheral blood involvement, in contrast to aggressive lymphomas, which involve nodal or extranodal sites with minimal marrow involvement.
    • Cytogenetics and mutation analyses are important for prognosis.
    • Central nervous system (CNS) involvement can occur and is an adverse risk factor, so the lumbar puncture is important.

3.7 · Acute lymphoblastic leukemia: management, prognosis and patient education

  • Management (slide 27):
    • Philadelphia chromosome [t(9;22)]-negative (Ph-) ALL: pediatric-like regimens, usually for patients < 40 years old: intensive chemotherapy with induction, consolidation and maintenance phases, multiple chemotherapies, steroids and often targeted agents. In older adults, therapy changes and dose adjustments may be needed for tolerance.
    • Philadelphia chromosome-positive (Ph+) ALL: tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKI) + chemotherapy.
    • Allogeneic stem cell transplant where feasible for adverse risk ALL or relapsed disease.
  • Prognosis (slide 28): often positive, particularly for children: five-year overall survival for < 15 years old is ~90% but needs intensive chemotherapy for 2–5 years. For adults, 5-year overall survival has historically been ~40%, with a cure rate that falls with age; improving with new treatments, including immunotherapies.
  • Patient education (slide 29): referral to and management by a hematologist/oncologist, ideally an ALL specialist; most patients with acute leukemia are admitted for work-up and initiation of treatment; frequent follow-up for labs, treatment and life-long surveillance; consider clinical trials where feasible.

3.8 · Acute myeloid leukemia: definition, pathogenesis and subtypes

★ “I want you to highlight, star, underline this ... Auer rods ... pathognomonic for AML.” (slides 31, 36)

“So yes, remember, Auer rods, pathognomonic, AML.” Auer rods are fused lysosomes; seeing them confirms a myeloid leukemia.

  • Definition (slide 31): acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a leukemia originating with the myeloid progenitor/stem cells: very immature cells with a rapid ability to proliferate. Auer rods are pathognomonic.
  • Who (slide 31): “most common form of leukemia, comprising 80% of all cases” (see the note above); mostly adults, particularly older adults (>60 years old); unusual in children outside of neonates.
  • Pathogenesis (slide 32): multiple subtypes depending on the initial mutation and stage of maturation; tremendous variety of cytogenetic abnormalities and mutations, so risk is broken down into favorable, intermediate and poor risk AML. Secondary AML: AML arising from another myeloid neoplasm (myelodysplastic syndrome, myeloproliferative neoplasm, others) or after treatment of another cancer → poor outcomes.
  • Subtypes (slide 33): historically ~8 FAB (French-American-British) subtypes, now 18 subtypes recognized by WHO (World Health Organization).
    • Acute myelomonocytic/monocytic leukemias (M4 and M5) tend to have more extramedullary involvement.
    • Acute erythroid leukemia and acute megakaryocytic leukemias are very rare.
    • Acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL) is discussed separately (3.11).

  • Signs and symptoms (slide 35): symptoms of excessive immature white cell (blast) production and immune dysfunction and infections: anemia (fatigue, weakness, lightheadedness, shortness of breath, pale skin); thrombocytopenia (bruising, often petechiae or purpura, bleeding); infections; B-symptoms (weight loss, fever, night sweats). Cytogenetics and mutation analyses are important for prognosis. Subgroup modifiers: AML with prior MDS or MPN (other myeloid neoplasms) and therapy-related (prior cancer), together called secondary AML, associated with a poor outcome.

3.10 · Acute myeloid leukemia: management, prognosis and patient education

  • Management (slide 40):
    • Fit patients: intensive chemotherapy, often cytarabine + anthracycline (7+3).
    • Unfit patients: low-intensity therapy, frequently hypomethylating agents +/- a BCL-2 inhibitor (venetoclax).
    • Whatever the first treatment, allogeneic stem cell transplant is typically used for most intermediate- and high-risk patients IF they achieve remission.
  • Prognosis (slide 41): treatment brings initial remission in many, but AML frequently relapses and treatment-related mortality is high. Overall survival only 48% at 1 year and about 25–30% at 5 years. Aside from APL, highest for favorable risk AML (about 2/3 alive at 5 years) and younger patients; lowest for patients over 70 (only 5% at 5 years) and those with adverse risk or secondary AML (about 10% overall).
  • Patient education (slide 42): referral to and management by a hematologist/oncologist, ideally a leukemia specialist; most patients with acute leukemia are admitted for work-up and initiation of treatment; frequent follow-up and life-long surveillance; consider clinical trials.

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML)

Defining feature: myeloid blasts ≥ 20% with Auer rods (pathognomonic) in an older adult; treatment by fitness

Definition
A leukemia of the myeloid progenitor/stem cells: very immature, rapidly proliferating cells; risk groups favorable, intermediate and poor; secondary AML arises from MDS, MPN or prior cancer treatment (slides 31–32).
Who gets it
Mostly adults, particularly over 60; unusual in children outside of neonates; male gender (slides 31, 34).
Risk factors
Prior high-dose radiation; benzene, petroleum, alkylator chemotherapy; HIV; Down syndrome, Fanconi anemia, ?family history; age over 60; male gender; prior MDS or MPN (slides 32, 34, 38).
Classic signs & symptoms
Fatigue, weakness, lightheadedness, shortness of breath; bruising, bleeding; infections; B-symptoms (weight loss, fever, night sweats) (slide 35).
Physical exam findings
Pale skin; petechiae or purpura; gum hyperplasia or extramedullary disease in the myelomonocytic and monocytic (M4, M5) subtypes (slides 33, 35).
Diagnostics / tests

3.11 · Acute promyelocytic leukemia

★ “The thing I want you to remember about APL, bleeding, DIC, coagulopathy, and that it does not need chemotherapy. ATRA and arsenic are the treatments for APL.” (slide 43)

“The only chemotherapy-free leukemia you can treat with a vitamin.”

  • Slide 43: acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL) is a rare AML subtype (FAB M3) caused by arrested differentiation at the promyelocyte.
  • Caused by a translocation of chromosomes 15 and 17, fusing the PML-RARA genes.

  • Unique treatment: arsenic trioxide and all-trans-retinoic acid (ATRA).

3.12 · Myelodysplastic syndrome

★ The blast drill (slide 47): “If it’s 10%, is it leukemia? No. If it’s 15%, leukemia? No.” At 20%, it is leukemia.

Higher-risk myelodysplastic syndrome may have blasts, but fewer than 20%; at 20% it is acute myeloid leukemia.

  • Introduction (slide 45): myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) are a group of myeloid malignancies defined by dysplasia (abnormal appearing) of myeloid cells. They often distort hematopoiesis and cause immune dysregulation and genomic changes (mutations). They present clinically as cytopenias, frequently anemia, and related complications (fatigue, infections, bleeding).
  • Pathogenesis (slide 46): often evolves from milder abnormal stem cell clones (clonal hematopoiesis) until the dysplasia of MDS develops. Typically a hypercellular marrow, but the cells are not functional. Presentation varies widely: low risk MDS is commonly defined by anemia with a low risk of AML progression; very high-risk MDS has elevated blasts and substantial overlap with AML. Often idiopathic and in the older population (median 70 years); sometimes prior chemotherapy or radiation; more common in men.
  • Signs and symptoms (slide 47): many are asymptomatic and found incidentally. Symptoms come from ineffective blood cell production and immune dysfunction: fatigue, weakness, pale skin (anemia); infections (ineffective white cells); bleeding (thrombocytopenia).
  • Labs (slide 47): most frequently anemia; neutropenia; occasional thrombocytopenia. Neutrophils show morphological abnormalities; higher risk MDS may have blasts but < 20% (otherwise AML); often red cell abnormalities (multinucleated or hyperplastic).
  • Treatment (slide 48): highly dependent on blood counts, risk status and mutations.
    • Low risk + anemia: erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESA) and red cell transfusions as needed.
    • Neutropenia: myeloid growth factors (careful not to overstimulate the blasts).
    • Higher-risk: chemotherapy or hypomethylating agents.
    • The only curative therapy is allogeneic stem cell transplant, but it is high risk with a limited role.
  • Prognosis (slide 49): highly variable by risk. Lower-risk: median survival > 5 years and a very low risk of AML. Very high-risk: median overall survival 9 months and a > 80% risk of AML within 5 years. Low risk patients often have complications from anemia, bleeding and/or infections; the goal of treatment is quality of life.

Myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS)

Defining feature: cytopenias (most often anemia) with dysplastic myeloid cells in a hypercellular marrow in an older man; blasts under 20% (20% or more = AML)

Definition
A group of myeloid malignancies defined by dysplasia (abnormal-appearing) of myeloid cells; evolves from clonal hematopoiesis (slides 45–46).
Who gets it
Older adults (median 70 years); more common in men (slide 46).
Risk factors
Often idiopathic; prior chemotherapy or radiation; older age; male sex (slide 46).
Classic signs & symptoms
Many asymptomatic and found incidentally; fatigue, weakness; infections; bleeding (slide 47).
Physical exam findings
Pale skin (slide 47); otherwise not covered in the lecture.
Diagnostics / tests
Most often anemia; neutropenia; occasional thrombocytopenia; abnormal neutrophil morphology; red cell abnormalities (multinucleated or hyperplastic); blasts < 20% in higher risk (slide 47); hypercellular marrow with non-functional cells (slide 46).
First-line treatment
By counts, risk and mutations: low risk + anemia → erythropoiesis-stimulating agents and transfusions; neutropenia → myeloid growth factors (with care); higher risk → chemotherapy or hypomethylating agents; allogeneic transplant is the only cure (high risk, limited role) (slide 48).
Complications

3.13 · Myeloproliferative neoplasms and polycythemia vera

★ “Polycythemia vera, JAK2 mutation, you may come across this on your testing, you might wanna remember that or write that down.” (slide 52)

The JAK2 mutation; the classic clue is itching after a warm bath or shower.

  • Myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPN) (slide 51) are rare myeloid malignancies characterized by atypical proliferation of mature cell lines:
    • Polycythemia vera: over-production of red blood cells, increasing blood viscosity.
    • Myelofibrosis: over-production of mature myeloid cell lines.
    • Essential thrombocythemia: over-production of platelets.
  • Polycythemia vera (PV) (slide 52):
    • Pathogenesis: commonly a result of a JAK2 mutation.
    • Presentation: usually non-specific: fatigue, headaches, blurry vision. Classic: pruritus with warm baths or showers.
    • Complications: thrombosis, splenomegaly, bleeding.
    • Treatment: phlebotomy, hydroxyurea.
    • Prognosis: excellent, estimated survival of 14 years; leukemic transformation is rare (~5% of cases).
    • Refer to a hematologist and advise to stop smoking.

3.14 · Myelofibrosis

  • Pathogenesis (slide 53): abnormal JAK-STAT pathway mutations → elevated cytokines and marked excess proliferation of all myeloid cell lines, eventually causing fibrosis of the marrow.
  • Presentation: usually non-specific: fatigue and abdominal symptoms from splenomegaly (pain, early satiety and/or bloating); night sweats, fever, weight loss are also common. The most aggressive of the MPNs.
  • Complications: severe splenomegaly, portal hypertension, bleeding, progression to secondary AML.
  • Treatment: often targeted inhibitors (JAK inhibitors), which are palliative.
  • Prognosis: highly variable: low risk can live 10–15 years; high risk often less than 2 years. Refer to a hematologist upon diagnosis.

3.15 · Essential thrombocythemia

  • Pathogenesis (slide 54): often gene mutations in the JAK-STAT pathway, causing overproduction of platelets; rarely inherited.
  • Presentation: mostly asymptomatic, but may present with clotting (headaches, fatigue, paresthesias or vision changes) or bleeding. Bleeding with high platelets can occur from acquired von Willebrand factor deficiency, a shortage of the factor at the site of injury.
  • Complications: clotting, including CVA and MI.
  • Treatment: aspirin, hydroxyurea, or other agents to reduce clotting risk.
  • Prognosis: excellent; generally a normal life span with appropriate management. Refer to a hematologist; advise to stop smoking.

3.16 · Chronic myelogenous leukemia: definition, pathogenesis and epidemiology

  • Definition (slide 56): chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML) originates from hematopoietic stem cells but produces increased numbers of granulocytes (more mature than blasts). It historically progresses from a chronic condition to acute leukemia in ~4–6 years.
  • Hallmark (slide 56): the BCR-ABL mutation, from fusion of chromosomes 22 and 9 → the Philadelphia chromosome.
  • Until tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), it was considered fatal; it is now largely a chronic disorder and most patients have long-term survival and a good prognosis (slide 56).
  • Pathogenesis (slide 57): the translocation between chromosomes 9 and 22 attaches the BCR gene to the ABL1 gene, making a fusion protein. The BCR-ABL fusion protein is a functional enzyme that promotes cell proliferation and reduces apoptosis: the cell “can’t turn off” and constantly divides. (Same picture as slide 20, in 3.5.)
  • Risk factors and epidemiology (slide 58): may be more common in radiation bomb victims (Japan), but no other identified risk factors; 15–20% of all leukemias; median age 45–55; either sex, slightly more common in men.

3.17 · Chronic myelogenous leukemia: presentation, work-up, differential and phases

  • Signs and symptoms (slide 59): ~50% are asymptomatic at diagnosis, found from leukocytosis on a complete blood count. Anemia (fatigue, weakness, lightheadedness, shortness of breath); abdominal swelling or discomfort from splenomegaly; B-symptoms (weight loss, fever, night sweats, loss of appetite).
  • Labs (slide 59): marked leukocytosis, thrombocytosis, anemia: white count often > 100K and up to 500K; platelets usually elevated; usually very low blasts unless accelerated or blast phase.
  • Work-up and diagnosis (slide 60): confirmed by bone marrow biopsy and PCR (polymerase chain reaction) for the BCR-ABL1 mutation. PCR for BCR-ABL1 in a patient with a marked increase in granulocytes confirms the diagnosis, and the assay is also used to monitor response to treatment. The marrow shows marked granulocytic proliferation and myelocytes in chronic phase. Also flow cytometry (lineage) and mutation analysis.
  • Differential diagnosis (slide 61): other myeloproliferative disorders, namely myelofibrosis (the slide also names CMML, never expanded): in CML the red cell morphology is normal and the hematocrit is not elevated; it is definitive when BCR/ABL1 is positive on PCR. Reactive leukocytosis with some infections: no splenomegaly, white count usually < 50K, BCR/ABL1 not positive.

Risk factors
Possibly radiation bomb exposure (Japan); no other identified risk factors (slide 58).
