7.1 · Definitions, potassium and oxygen (objective b)

  • Normal systemic arterial pH: 7.35–7.45. Acidemia = pH below 7.35; alkalemia = pH above 7.45 (slide 3).
  • Acidosis: a pathologic condition with excessively acidic body fluids or tissues. Alkalosis: a pathologic condition with excessively alkaline body fluids or tissues (slide 3).
  • Simple (also called primary) acid-base disorder: a primary disturbance and its corresponding compensation. Mixed acid-base disorder: more than one primary disturbance at the same time (slide 4).
  • Anion: an ion with a negative charge. Cation: an ion with a positive charge (slide 4).

★ The hypokalemia of alkalosis is a shift into cells, not a loss of the body’s potassium; she came back to it when treating metabolic alkalosis (“careful not to overcorrect,” 7.11).

7.4 · Acid-base regulation, acid generation and buffers (objectives a, c)

  • Plasma hydrogen ion varies very little and is normally at very low levels; precise regulation is necessary for all cellular and enzymatic functions (slide 12).
  • pH below 6.7 or above 7.6 is unsustainable. It may cause cardiac hypocontractility or irritability, seizures, cerebral edema and metabolic disturbances, and may warrant therapy to normalize the serum pH along with treatment of the underlying cause (slide 12).
★ Her “golden rule” (slides 12, 23)

“If we’re looking for a definitive treatment for an acid-base disorder, find and treat that underlying condition.” Slide 23: the underlying condition MUST be corrected to restore pH balance.

The buffer systems Slide 14

  • Renal / respiratory: the most important overall buffer system.
    • Bicarbonate: hydrogen ions and bicarbonate move relatively slowly across the cell membrane.
    • Carbon dioxide: respiration can be adjusted more quickly.
  • Intracellular buffers (60–70% of body fluid buffering): proteins, phosphate, hemoglobin.
  • Bone.

How buffering works (slides 15–17): H2O + CO2 ↔ H2CO3 ↔ H+ + HCO3− — water and carbon dioxide ↔ carbonic acid ↔ hydrogen and bicarbonate ions. The respiratory side acts on the water-and-carbon-dioxide end (slide 16); the renal side acts on the hydrogen-and-bicarbonate end (slide 17).

Pulmonary acid excretion Slide 22

  • Removal of carbon dioxide by ventilation, regulated by the brain stem (cerebral pH). An increase in carbon dioxide → increased respiratory rate.
  • Hyperventilation: ↑ carbon dioxide excretion, ↓ pCO2. Hypoventilation: ↓ carbon dioxide excretion, ↑ pCO2.

Renal acid excretion Slides 20–21

  • Maintenance of acid-base balance requires reabsorption of filtered bicarbonate (in the proximal tubule) and excretion of acid.
  • The kidney is unable to excrete free hydrogen ions: they are combined with urinary buffers in the collecting tubule — ammonium (NH4+) and titratable acids (HPO4, urate) — which generates bicarbonate. Urine pH may fall as low as 4.0.
  • Net acid excretion = (titratable acids + NH4+) − urinary HCO3−: titratable acids plus ammonium, minus urinary bicarbonate.
  • ★ Urinary bicarbonate should = 0 (reabsorption!). Titratable acid depends on daily phosphate intake; the major titratable acid is HPO4 (hydrogen phosphate). Hydrogen ion excretion increases by increasing ammonium production and excretion.

She said the kidney physiology is not for deep reading: “what I want you to get out of this” is that urine bicarbonate should be zero and that the titratable acid is mostly hydrogen phosphate, set by phosphate intake.

7.6 · Compensation (objective e)

  • If the cause is respiratory, compensation is renal: bicarbonate is retained or excreted. If the cause is metabolic (the slide says “renal”), compensation is respiratory: carbon dioxide is retained or excreted (slide 23).
  • Inadequate or excessive compensation points to a mixed acid-base disorder. There cannot be more than one primary respiratory disorder (slide 23).
★ “Tattoo on the inside of your eyeballs” (slide 23)

The compensatory response will NOT fully correct the pH disturbance; the underlying condition MUST be corrected to restore pH balance. In her words, compensation “will bring it closer to normal but it’ll never make it perfectly normal until the underlying condition is eliminated.”

So a normal pH does not mean there is no acid-base problem: “it means there’s not a simple acid-base problem but it does not mean that there’s not a mixed problem.” An acidosis and an alkalosis can pull the pH in opposite directions and leave it in the normal range. Always look at the pH, the carbon dioxide and the bicarbonate together (slide 58: a normal pH = no abnormality or a mixed acid-base disorder).

7.7 · Approach to the patient and mixed disorders (objectives d, e, f)

Approach to the patient Slide 26

  1. Identify the primary disorder: order an arterial blood gas and electrolytes. Acidosis or alkalosis? Metabolic or respiratory?
  2. Calculate the anion gap: sodium − (bicarbonate + chloride). It can be abnormal even when sodium, chloride and bicarbonate are within normal range. An anion gap over 20 mEq/L suggests a primary metabolic disorder. A large anion gap is never associated with compensation for a respiratory disorder alone!
  3. Evaluate compensation and identify any mixed disorder: calculate the expected compensation (slide 24 table); excess or inadequate compensation → a mixed disorder is present.
★ Said three times (slides 4, 23, 26, 30)
  • “We can only have one respiratory problem at a time, but we can have multiple metabolic problems at a time” (slide 23: cannot have more than one primary respiratory disorder). You cannot breathe too fast and too slow at once.
  • “If we have a big anion gap that’s always going to be a metabolic problem. Respiratory disorders are not gonna cause large anion gaps like that” (slide 26).

One step further: the change in anion gap against the change in bicarbonate Slides 27, 50

  • Change in anion gap = calculated anion gap − normal anion gap (10 is an accepted value).
  • Change in bicarbonate = lab result for bicarbonate − normal bicarbonate (25 is an accepted value); use the size of the fall from 25.
  • The change in anion gap should equal the change in bicarbonate; differences indicate mixed disorders are present (slide 27).
  • Examine the patient again: do the clinical findings support the acid-base analysis? (slide 27, in red).

7.8 · Respiratory acidosis (objective d.a)

  • Increased pCO2 due to decreased alveolar ventilation — NEVER increased carbon dioxide production. The body attempts to increase bicarbonate by cell buffering and increased acid secretion; the two mechanisms occur over different time periods. May be acute or chronic (slide 31).
  • Acute: increased carbon dioxide → increased plasma carbonic acid; plasma bicarbonate increases 1 mEq/L for every 10 mm Hg increase in pCO2 (slide 33).
  • Chronic: after 24–48 hours of sustained increased pCO2, plasma bicarbonate increases 3.5 mEq/L for every 10 mm Hg; urinary excretion of titratable acid and ammonium → increased plasma bicarbonate (slide 34).

★ Acute or chronic: read the bicarbonate (slides 33–35)

Slide 35: if the pCO2 is 80 mm Hg, how can we tell whether it is acute or chronic? Look at the bicarbonate on the arterial blood gas and compare it with the calculated expected bicarbonate.

“Is it closer to the 29, or is it closer to 39?” Closer to 29 is acute; closer to 39 is chronic. The kidneys have “kicked in” in the chronic case, which is why the bicarbonate rise is larger.

Treatment Slides 36–37

  • Treat the underlying condition. Look at medications: stop respiratory depressants — narcotics, benzodiazepines.
  • Acute or severe impairment of carbon dioxide excretion: PaCO2 above 80 mm Hg and life-threatening hypoxia (PaO2, arterial oxygen, below 40 mm Hg): adequate ventilation (may involve emergency airways, a mechanical ventilator); clear secretions, assure airway patency; administer oxygen.
  • Chronic: treatment similar to acute but with careful oxygen administration. A rapidly falling PaCO2 in chronic respiratory acidosis may trigger complications associated with alkalosis: cardiac arrhythmias, reduced cerebral perfusion, seizures.

★ She singled out narcotics as respiratory depressants (slides 32, 36), and slow, careful oxygen in the chronic retainer, because the compensating bicarbonate takes days to come back down (slide 37).

7.9 · Respiratory alkalosis (objective d.b)

  • pH above 7.45, decreased pCO2 (slide 38). Compensation: bicarbonate falls 2 mEq/L per 10 mm Hg fall (acute) or 5 (chronic) (slide 24).
  • Some causes (slide 39): anxiety; hypoxia (high altitude → physiologic respiratory alkalosis); lung disease with or without hypoxia (decreased lung compliance — interstitial fibrosis); central nervous system disease (catastrophic central nervous system event — intracerebral hemorrhage).

7.10 · Metabolic acidosis (objectives d.c, e)

  • Excess of acid to buffer, or net loss of bicarbonate ions from extracellular fluid. Compensation → decrease pCO2 by increasing respiration (slide 41).
  • Symptoms (slide 42): primarily associated with the underlying disorder; compensatory hyperventilation may be present; ★ Kussmaul breathing can be seen with severe acidosis: deep, regular, sighing breaths.
★ Her mnemonics again (slide 43): MUDPILES and USEDCAR

Increased anion gap → MUDPILES. Normal anion gap → USEDCAR. “Are we dealing with our MUDPILES or are we dealing with our USEDCAR? And this gives us our differential list that we need to work from.”

Bromide intoxication.

★ A low anion gap is “nine times out of ten” a low albumin: correct it (7.2) and it usually lands in the normal or raised range. Plasma cell dyscrasias and bromide are “pretty much zebra conditions.”

★ “1.5 times our bicarb plus eight plus or minus two” (slide 49)

Compensation for metabolic acidosis, increased-gap and normal-gap alike: determine whether there is adequate respiratory compensation or a concurrent respiratory disorder.

Winter formula: expected pCO2 = 1.5 × (HCO3−) + 8, plus or minus 2 — expected carbon dioxide = 1.5 times the measured bicarbonate, plus 8, plus or minus 2.

Metabolic acidosis

Defining feature: low pH with a low bicarbonate; calculate (and albumin-correct) the anion gap, then check the carbon dioxide with the Winter formula

Definition
An excess of acid to buffer or a net loss of bicarbonate from extracellular fluid, with a decreased pH and bicarbonate; increased, normal or decreased anion gap.
Who gets it
“The usual suspects”: ketoacidosis (diabetic, alcoholic, starvation), lactic acidosis, renal failure, toxins; for a normal gap, gastrointestinal bicarbonate loss and renal tubular acidosis.
Risk factors / causes
Increased gap: MUDPILES (methanol, metformin, uremia, ketoacidoses, paraldehyde, propylene glycol, isoniazid, iron, lactic acidosis, ethylene glycol, salicylates, starvation; also renal failure, rhabdomyolysis). Normal gap: USEDCAR (ureteral diversion, saline infusion, exogenous acid, diarrhea, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, adrenal insufficiency, renal tubular acidosis; also chlorine gas, hyperalimentation).
Classic signs & symptoms
Primarily those of the underlying disorder; compensatory hyperventilation may be present.
Physical exam findings
Kussmaul breathing (deep, regular, sighing breaths) with severe acidosis.
Diagnostics / tests
Arterial blood gas and electrolytes; anion gap = sodium − (bicarbonate + chloride), corrected for albumin; Winter formula for the expected carbon dioxide; change in anion gap against change in bicarbonate (and chloride).
First-line treatment
No treatment slide for this disorder; slide 12: treat the underlying cause, and an unsustainable pH (below 6.7) may warrant therapy to normalize the serum pH along with it.
Complications
Hyperkalemia from the hydrogen-potassium shift (slide 5); at pH below 6.7, cardiac hypocontractility or irritability, seizures, cerebral edema (slide 12).

  • Treat fluid deficits and potassium deficits if needed — don’t overcorrect!

  • Consider whether these are present: excess alkali intake; glucose ingestion after a period of starvation; high-dose penicillin treatment; profound potassium depletion; endogenous mineralocorticoid excess.
★ “Careful not to overcorrect” (slides 5, 54)

The hypokalemia of alkalosis is potassium shifting into cells (slide 5), not a loss of total body potassium; once balance is restored, the potassium moves back out. Replace potassium only if needed, and don’t overcorrect (slide 54), or the potassium ends up too high.

Metabolic alkalosis

Defining feature: high pH with a high bicarbonate; urine chloride below 25 (saline responsive: vomiting, diuretics, volume contraction) or above 40 (saline unresponsive, then sort by blood pressure)

Definition
pH above 7.45 with decreased hydrogen ion and increased bicarbonate; saline-responsive and saline-unresponsive types.
Who gets it
Less common than metabolic acidosis; patients with volume contraction, diuretics, vomiting or gastric suction, steroid or mineralocorticoid excess.
Risk factors / causes
Responsive: volume contraction, diuretics, vomiting or gastric suction, alkaline drugs, post-hypercapnia. Unresponsive normotensive: severe potassium depletion, hypoparathyroidism and hypercalcemia. Unresponsive hypertensive: primary aldosteronism, exogenous alkali, mineralocorticoids, licorice.
Classic signs & symptoms
Primarily those of the underlying disorder; weakness from hypokalemia; rarely tetany and neuromuscular irritability.
Physical exam findings
Hyporeflexia (from hypokalemia); blood pressure normal or raised in the saline-unresponsive types.
Diagnostics / tests
Arterial blood gas: pH above 7.45, bicarbonate above 26; carbon dioxide rises 0.75 mm Hg per 1 mEq/L bicarbonate rise; urine chloride below 25 (responsive) or above 40 mEq/L (unresponsive).
First-line treatment
Treat the underlying condition; stop diuretics, nasogastric suction, corticosteroids (or switch to less mineralocorticoid activity); treat fluid and potassium deficits if needed without overcorrecting.
Complications
Hypokalemia (a shift into cells) and decreased oxygen delivery to tissues (slide 5); potassium overcorrection.

7.12 · Step-by-step acid-base diagnosis (objective f)

  1. Obtain an arterial blood gas and electrolytes simultaneously; compare the bicarbonate on the blood gas and on the electrolytes to verify accuracy (slide 56).
  2. Acidosis or alkalosis? Look at the pH (slide 56).
  3. Respiratory or metabolic? Look at the PCO2 and bicarbonate (slide 56).
  4. Calculate the anion gap and correct for hypoalbuminemia if needed (slide 57).
  5. Estimate the compensatory response: is compensation appropriate? (slide 57)
  6. Determine what mixed disorders may be present: consider pH, carbon dioxide, bicarbonate; the change in anion gap, bicarbonate and chloride (slide 57).
  7. Does your assessment of the acid-base disorder match the clinical picture? (slides 56–57, in red).
★ “Always, always, always circle back to our patient” (slides 27, 56–57)

Even when the pH is normal, still look at the bicarbonate and the carbon dioxide; then ask whether the physical exam and the clinical picture match the numbers. Conclusion (slide 63): use a step-wise method; identify the primary disorder; calculate the anion gap; assess compensatory mechanisms; is there a second disorder? If so, identify the underlying causes and treat both diagnoses!
