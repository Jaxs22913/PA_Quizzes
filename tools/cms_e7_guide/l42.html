4.1 · Cor pulmonale: definition and causes

Cor pulmonale is an alteration in the structure and function of the right ventricle (RV) (enlargement, hypertrophy, or dilation) caused exclusively by a primary disorder of the respiratory system or pulmonary vasculature (slide 4). Slide 18 says it the same way: RV hypertrophy, dilation, or failure secondary to pulmonary hypertension or primary lung disease. The hemodynamic picture is an elevated mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) with a normal pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP).

  • Group 2 pulmonary hypertension (left heart failure) is NOT cor pulmonale. The diagnostic rule: you must exclude elevated left-sided filling pressures (Group 2 pulmonary hypertension) to diagnose cor pulmonale (slide 4).
  • The most common cause of RV failure overall is left ventricular (LV) failure; pure cor pulmonale isolates the lung–heart axis and needs a diagnostic and therapeutic approach distinct from standard heart failure management (slide 18).
The roots of resistance: parenchymal vs vascular disease (slide 5)
Parenchymal pathologies (primarily chronic)Vascular pathologies (acute & chronic)
Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD): ★ the absolute leading cause. Cor pulmonale is present in >50% of patients with chronic obstructive lung disease.Acute pulmonary thromboembolism (PE): sudden macroscopic occlusion.
Interstitial lung disease (ILD): fibrotic destruction of lung architecture.Primary pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH): idiopathic vascular remodeling (Group 1).
Sleep-disordered breathing: chronic nocturnal alveolar hypoventilation.Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH): unresolved clot burden (Group 4).

4.2 · Cor pulmonale: pathophysiology and hemodynamics

★ “Alveolar hypoxia is the most important driver” (slide 6)

Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (pathophysiology engine I). The protective reflex (targeted): in healthy lungs, localized hypoxia triggers focused vasoconstriction that shunts blood to better-oxygenated areas, optimizing the ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) ratio. The pathological trap (global bottleneck): in chronic lung disease (especially “Blue Bloaters” with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)), global alveolar hypoxia and hypercapnia trigger diffuse, system-wide vasoconstriction → a massive increase in pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) → a relentless increase in right ventricular afterload. Alveolar hypoxia is the single most important driver of pulmonary hypertension in COPD.

Pathophysiology engine II: vascular bed obliteration (slide 7), shown for emphysema, interstitial lung disease (ILD)/fibrosis and pulmonary embolism (PE):

  1. Structural effacement: increased resistance isn’t just functional (constriction); it is anatomical.
  2. Loss of surface area: the total cross-sectional area of the pulmonary vascular bed is severely reduced, increasing resistance.
  3. The compounding factor: polycythemia (driven by chronic hypoxia) increases blood viscosity, further increasing cardiac workload against an obliterated vascular bed.

How the right ventricle (RV) responds to pressure (slide 8). The RV is intrinsically a thin-walled, high-compliance, low-pressure chamber, highly sensitive to sudden afterload changes. The acute crash (e.g., massive PE): sudden pressure outpaces adaptation; wall tension rises instantly → acute RV dilation, stretch and microinfarction. The chronic burden (e.g., chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, ILD): a gradual pressure rise allows time for compensatory RV hypertrophy; the walls thicken to maintain flow until eventual exhaustion.

The master matrix: acute vs chronic cor pulmonale (slide 9)

Acute manifestations: the chain of collapse (ventricular interdependence and shock, slide 10): massive pulmonary obstruction spikes RV wall tension → the RV acutely dilates under the sudden pressure → the shared interventricular septum bows leftward into the left ventricle (LV) → LV diastolic distensibility and filling drop drastically → LV cardiac output plummets, causing systemic hypotension and cardiovascular collapse. Clinical signs of the crash: distended neck veins (backed-up RV), tricuspid regurgitation murmur (stretched annulus), tachycardia and pallor.

Chronic manifestations: the plumbing backlog (slide 11). In chronic cor pulmonale the hypertrophied RV eventually decompensates; elevated diastolic pressures transmit backward into the systemic venous circulation, dramatically increasing hydrostatic pressure.

4.3 · Cor pulmonale: diagnosis and management

The diagnostic funnel (slide 12): evaluating pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale requires systematically separating parenchymal disease (Group 3), thromboembolic disease (Group 4) and primary vascular disease (Group 1).

Isolating the etiology: the clinical pathway for suspected pulmonary hypertension / cor pulmonale (slide 12)
StepWhat it doesResult that sorts the patient
Step 1: chest X-ray (CXR) & pulmonary function tests (PFTs)CXR: assess for interstitial markings (interstitial lung disease) or hyperinflation (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) vs clear fields. PFTs: confirm obstructive vs restrictive patterns.Interstitial lung disease or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease → parenchymal disease (Group 3); normal / non-diagnostic → step 2
Step 2: V/Q (ventilation/perfusion) scan or CT pulmonary angiography (CT-PA)Exclude segmental defects (chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension, pulmonary embolism).Defects → thromboembolic disease (Group 4); no thromboembolism → step 3
Step 3: right heart catheterization (RHC) & echoEchocardiogram and catheterization: confirm right ventricular strain and measure precise hemodynamics.Elevated mean pulmonary artery pressure, wedge pressure ≤15 mmHg → primary vascular disease (Group 1)

Assessing the lungs (slide 13). Pulmonary function tests (PFTs): the obstructive pattern (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) = reduced FEV1/FVC (forced expiratory volume in 1 second over forced vital capacity), “the most common cause”; the restrictive pattern (interstitial lung disease) = reduced total lung capacity (TLC). PFTs are crucial for classifying Group 3 pulmonary hypertension. Chest X-ray hallmarks: flattened diaphragms (hyperinflation in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) and enlarged central pulmonary arteries (high pressure). In patients with pulmonary arterial hypertension or pulmonary embolism, the peripheral lung fields may appear deceptively “clear” despite severe, life-threatening vascular disease.

Assessing the right heart (slide 14). Electrocardiogram (EKG): peaked P waves indicate right atrial enlargement; a tall R wave in V1 indicates right ventricular hypertrophy; the EKG is abnormal in 70% of acute pulmonary embolism cases; in chronic cor pulmonale look for severe right axis deviation. Echocardiography evaluates right ventricular enlargement and estimates pulmonary artery systolic pressure. ★ The visual hallmark of severe right heart pressure overload is septal flattening during systole, deforming the left ventricle into a “D-shape.”

Management principles: treat the root cause (slide 15). The prime directive: you cannot fix cor pulmonale without addressing the underlying pulmonary pathology.

  • “Oxygen is a drug” (reversing hypoxic vasoconstriction): for Group 3 pulmonary hypertension (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, interstitial lung disease), continuous supplemental oxygen is paramount; it directly reverses alveolar hypoxic vasoconstriction, mechanically lowering pulmonary resistance.
  • Targeted etiologic therapies: chronic obstructive pulmonary disease = bronchodilators and aggressive treatment of underlying respiratory infections; acute pulmonary embolism = thrombolytics and anticoagulation (expanding blood volume if hypotensive); pulmonary arterial hypertension (Group 1) = specialized targeted therapies including phosphodiesterase-5 (PDE5) inhibitors, endothelin receptor antagonists and prostacyclins (4.7).

Fluid management: the preload tightrope (slide 16). Too much fluid (overload) = hepatic congestion and severe edema; too little fluid (under-filled) = decreased left ventricular filling and a catastrophic drop in cardiac output; the answer is careful diuresis.

  • The goal: diuretics (loop diuretics, spironolactone) treat fluid retention, diminishing hepatic congestion and peripheral edema.
  • The danger: the damaged right ventricle strictly relies on elevated filling pressures (preload) to push blood past the high pulmonary resistance.
  • The consequence: decreasing right ventricular preload too aggressively starves the left ventricle of blood, giving a disastrous drop in systemic cardiac output. Furthermore, hypokalemia can trigger lethal arrhythmias.

Cor pulmonale

Defining feature: right ventricular hypertrophy, dilation or failure from lung or pulmonary vascular disease, with a raised mean pulmonary artery pressure and a normal wedge pressure (left heart failure excluded)

Definition
Alteration in the structure and function of the right ventricle (enlargement, hypertrophy, or dilation) caused exclusively by a primary disorder of the respiratory system or pulmonary vasculature; elevated mean pulmonary artery pressure with normal pulmonary capillary wedge pressure. Left heart failure (Group 2) is not cor pulmonale. Acute (massive pulmonary embolism, minutes to hours, sudden dilation) vs chronic (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, interstitial lung disease, pulmonary arterial hypertension; months to years; concentric hypertrophy, then late dilation).
Who gets it
Patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease above all (the absolute leading cause; present in >50% of them); interstitial lung disease; sleep-disordered breathing; acute pulmonary embolism; pulmonary arterial hypertension; chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension.
Risk factors
Global alveolar hypoxia and hypercapnia (the “Blue Bloater”); loss of the vascular bed (emphysema, fibrosis, emboli); polycythemia from chronic hypoxia (raises blood viscosity).
Classic signs & symptoms
Acute: severe sudden dyspnea, sweating, rapid pulse, pallor, syncope. Chronic: unproductive cough, progressive exertional dyspnea, “Blue Bloater” phenotype.
Physical exam findings
Acute: distended neck veins, tricuspid regurgitation murmur, tachycardia, pallor. Chronic: right ventricular heave (parasternal lift, left sternal border), intensified P2, systolic tricuspid regurgitation murmur; tender hepatomegaly, ascites, dependent peripheral pitting edema.
Diagnostics / tests
Stepwise: chest X-ray and pulmonary function tests (Group 3), then ventilation/perfusion scan or CT pulmonary angiography (Group 4), then right heart catheterization and echo (Group 1: raised mean pulmonary artery pressure, wedge ≤15 mmHg). EKG: peaked P waves, tall R in V1, severe right axis deviation (chronic). Echocardiography: right ventricular enlargement, estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure, systolic septal flattening (D-shaped left ventricle). Chest film: flattened diaphragms, enlarged central pulmonary arteries; fields may look deceptively clear in pulmonary arterial hypertension or embolism.
First-line treatment
Treat the underlying lung disease (the prime directive). Continuous supplemental oxygen in Group 3 (oxygen is a drug: it reverses hypoxic vasoconstriction); bronchodilators and aggressive treatment of infection in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease; thrombolytics and anticoagulation (volume if hypotensive) in acute embolism; targeted therapy in pulmonary arterial hypertension. Careful diuresis (loop diuretics, spironolactone), never aggressive.
Complications
Acute: decreased left ventricular filling and immediate cardiovascular collapse (shock) from the septum bowing into the left ventricle. Chronic: progressive volume overload, hypoxemia and right heart failure. Over-diuresis: a disastrous drop in cardiac output; hypokalemia: lethal arrhythmias.

4.4 · Pulmonary hypertension: classification and pathophysiology

The core axiom: the lung dictates the fate of the right heart (slide 18). Cor pulmonale is right ventricular hypertrophy, dilation, or failure secondary to pulmonary hypertension or primary lung disease; although the most common cause of right ventricular failure overall is left ventricular failure, pure cor pulmonale isolates the lung–heart axis.

How pressure destroys the pump (slide 20; he asked only for the sequence, not the charts):

  1. Stage 1, compensatory hypertrophy: the right ventricular wall thickens to overcome rising pulmonary vascular resistance.
  2. Stage 2, dilation & failure: the Starling mechanism fails; the chamber dilates as end-diastolic volume increases.
  3. Stage 3, ventricular interdependence: the dilated, pressure-overloaded right ventricle bows the interventricular septum into the left ventricle.

Clinical consequence: septal bowing mechanically impairs left ventricular diastolic filling, reducing systemic cardiac output and causing exertional syncope, despite normal left ventricular contractility.

4.5 · Pulmonary hypertension: presentation and examination

From vague dyspnea to fixed cardiac output (slide 21)
Stage (World Health Organization functional class)Symptoms
Early / mild (WHO class I–II)Exertional dyspnea and fatigue: often the only early symptom; frequently dismissed or misattributed to deconditioning.
Moderate (WHO class III)Dizziness and lightheadedness; marked limitation of physical activity.
Severe (WHO class IV)Syncope / presyncope: an ominous sign indicating a fixed, low cardiac output incapable of increasing with exertion. End-stage: abdominal bloating, early satiety, edema; present even at rest.

Signs of venous congestion on examination (slide 22):

  • The jugular vein: elevated jugular venous pressure (JVP); look for large “a” waves (early) or regurgitant “v” waves (tricuspid insufficiency).
  • Cardiac auscultation and palpation: palpable parasternal lift (right ventricular heave), accentuated pulmonic valve closure (P2), and a right ventricular S3 gallop (indicating right ventricular dysfunction).
  • The hepatojugular reflux: applying sustained moderate pressure on the liver increases the jugular venous pressure by >1 cm, correlating with elevated right-sided filling pressures.
  • The extremities: peripheral pitting edema (extends to hips/sacrum in severe cases); cool extremities due to low cardiac output and peripheral vasoconstriction.

His memory aid for the gallops: the S3 is “a big floppy left ventricle,” the S4 “a stiffened, filled ventricle.”

The endothelin receptor antagonists and prostacyclins he gave “for completeness” (“specialty sorcery”): know them by class and example.

Foundational management: fluid, oxygen and conditioning (slide 27):

  • Fluid management: careful diuresis is central to managing right ventricular volume overload. Intravenous loop diuretics produce venodilation prior to diuresis. Avoid aggressive over-diuresis, which can drop preload and crash cardiac output.
  • Oxygen therapy: maintain PaO2 (arterial oxygen pressure) >60 mmHg. Hypoxia is a potent pulmonary vasoconstrictor; supplemental oxygen is critical, especially during sleep or exertion.
  • Exercise and conditioning: avoid strict bedrest. Regular, steady aerobic exercise in a supervised rehabilitation program is safe, prevents muscle deconditioning and improves fatigue.
  • Preventive care: annual influenza and pneumococcal immunizations are mandatory to prevent acute respiratory decompensations.
Therapeutic algorithm: from functional class to pharmacy (slide 28; top level)

End-stage management and the limits of the pump (slide 29):

  • Advance directives: because sudden death or lethal syncope can be the first indication of disease progression, early palliative care discussions and advance directives are essential.
  • 6% survival at 90 days following cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) in pulmonary arterial hypertension: the hypertrophied, failed right ventricle rarely recovers from arrest.
  • Ultimate interventions: for highly selected patients refractory to maximal medical therapy (or those with pulmonary veno-occlusive disease (PVOD)), lung transplantation remains the only definitive therapeutic option; continuous intravenous support may serve as a bridge to transplant.

Pulmonary hypertension

Defining feature: exertional dyspnea and fatigue (often blamed on deconditioning) with a loud P2, right ventricular heave and clear lungs; the echo screens, right heart catheterization confirms

Definition
Raised pressure in the pulmonary circulation that loads the right ventricle; classified by the World Health Organization into five groups by mechanism: pre-capillary Group 1 (pulmonary arterial hypertension), Group 3 (lung disease/hypoxia), Group 4 (chronic thromboembolic); post-capillary Group 2 (left heart disease); Group 5 (unclear/multifactorial). No pressure threshold is on the slides.
Who gets it
Group 1: idiopathic, connective tissue disease, HIV, schistosomiasis; Group 2: heart failure (reduced or preserved ejection fraction), mitral stenosis; Group 3: chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, interstitial lung disease, sleep apnea; Group 4: chronic thromboembolic occlusion; Group 5: hematologic, systemic, metabolic (e.g., sarcoidosis).
Risk factors
Chronic hypoxia (a potent pulmonary vasoconstrictor); chronic lung disease; previous pulmonary embolism (chronic thromboembolic disease); left heart disease.
Classic signs & symptoms
Exertional dyspnea and fatigue (often the only early symptom, misattributed to deconditioning, class I–II); dizziness, lightheadedness, marked limitation (class III); syncope or presyncope (ominous: fixed low output) and end-stage abdominal bloating, early satiety, edema at rest (class IV).
Physical exam findings
Elevated jugular venous pressure with large a waves or regurgitant v waves; right ventricular heave; accentuated P2; right ventricular S3; hepatojugular reflux (>1 cm); pitting edema to hips and sacrum; cool extremities; lungs typically clear.
Diagnostics / tests
Echocardiography = the most useful initial (screening) test (right ventricular size, TAPSE, pulmonary pressures, left-sided mimics); ventilation/perfusion scan = essential to exclude chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (do not rely on CT angiography alone); right heart catheterization = mandatory confirmation (mean pulmonary artery pressure, wedge pressure, pulmonary vascular resistance; wedge ≤15 mmHg in pre-capillary disease). Chest film: enlarged central pulmonary arteries with peripheral pruning.
First-line treatment
Careful diuresis (no aggressive over-diuresis); oxygen to keep PaO2 >60 mmHg, especially in sleep or exertion; supervised aerobic rehabilitation (no strict bedrest); annual influenza and pneumococcal vaccines. Vasoreactive: high-dose oral calcium channel blockers. Not vasoreactive: low/intermediate risk = oral combination (endothelin receptor antagonist + phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitor); high risk = continuous intravenous or subcutaneous prostanoids (epoprostenol). Refractory: lung transplantation.
Complications
Right ventricular hypertrophy, then dilation and failure; septal bowing with exertional syncope; sudden death or lethal syncope (may be the first sign of progression); 6% survival at 90 days after cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Prognosis by the slide 25 table: right ventricular failure signs, rapid progression, repeated syncope, class IV or walk <165 m = high risk (>10% 1-year mortality). Referral: transplantation; early palliative care and advance directives.

4.8 · Pulmonary edema: mechanism and causes

The Starling forces (slide 32):

  • Outward force: elevated capillary hydrostatic pressure (Pc), the engine of cardiogenic edema.
  • Inward force: plasma oncotic pressure, the failing counter-balance.
  • The barrier: the semi-permeable capillary membrane, intact but overwhelmed.

Pulmonary edema fundamentally manifests when the outward hydrostatic pressure exceeds inward oncotic pressure, or when the barrier itself shatters, flooding the alveolar airspace.

The great divide: identifying the source of the flood (slide 33)

His first question in any edema: “pipes, pumps, or filter? … your differentials all spring from the answer to that question.”

4.9 · Pulmonary edema: presentation and diagnosis

At the bedside (slide 35; “not a shopping list, not a checklist”):

  • Pink, frothy sputum: the visual proof of profound alveolar fluid shift and microvascular hemorrhage (he called it a late sign).
  • Bilateral rales / crackles and wheezing: the acoustic signature of fluid-filled airways and peribronchial cuffing.
  • S3 gallop: the marker of an overloaded, failing ventricle.
  • Acute dyspnea, diaphoresis, tripoding: the catecholamine surge and extreme work of breathing.

4.10 · Pulmonary edema: management and prognosis

Acute emergency management: the LMNOP rescue (slide 39) — “like my toolbox, not a checklist”
L – Loop diuretics (IV)Rapid volume reduction; aggressive diuresis (furosemide, bumetanide, torsemide).
M – MorphineUse with caution. Historically used for venodilation and anxiolysis, but carries respiratory depression risks.
N – Nitrates (IV)Immediate preload and afterload reduction via potent venodilation.
O – Oxygen / upright positioningOptimizing ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) matching and physically dropping venous return via gravity.
P – Positive airway pressure (NIPPV) (noninvasive positive pressure ventilation)Mechanical alveolar stenting and preload reduction.

Mechanisms of rescue (slide 40). Noninvasive positive pressure ventilation: more than just oxygenation; positive pressure mechanically stents open flooded alveoli, forces fluid back across the membrane, and decreases intrathoracic preload and afterload. Nitrate venodilation: rapidly shifts volume into the peripheral venous capacitance system, directly lowering the outward capillary hydrostatic pressure (Pc) in the lungs.

Chronic heart failure optimization (slide 41, a refresher): moving from acute volume management to long-term mortality reduction requires blocking deleterious neurohormonal activation. “Quadruple therapy” provides the greatest mortality reduction in HFrEF (heart failure with reduced ejection fraction):

  • ARNI (angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor; sacubitril-valsartan): vasodilation and natriuresis.
  • Beta-blockers: halting sympathetic toxicity and remodeling.
  • MRA (mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists): now proven effective in both reduced and preserved/mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFpEF/HFmrEF).
  • SGLT-2 inhibitors (sodium-glucose cotransporter-2): the newest class for mortality reduction.

Adult vs elderly strategy (slide 42) is in 4.14.

Prognosis and the stage D cliff (slide 44)
Stages A & BAt risk and pre-symptomatic: focus on prevention and risk modification.
Stage CClinical heart failure: the domain of quadruple therapy.
Stage D (the break point)Marked symptoms at rest despite maximal medical therapy; frequent hospitalizations.
The referral triggerReaching stage D mandates immediate evaluation for advanced therapies: heart transplant, mechanical circulatory support / LVAD (left ventricular assist device), or palliative hospice care.

The complete picture (slide 45), in four phases: (1) microscopic failure, disrupted Starling forces (capillary hydrostatic pressure exceeds oncotic pressure); (2) hemodynamic crisis, elevated wedge pressure (>18 mmHg) triggering alveolar flooding; (3) acute rescue, LMNOP shifts volume out of the central circulation and stents alveoli; (4) chronic rebuilding, quadruple therapy remodels the neurohormonal landscape to prevent recurrence. “Survival depends on recognizing the failure at the membrane, measuring the pressure at the wedge, and architecting the rescue at the bedside.”

Flash pulmonary edema (slide 46):

  • Precipitating causes: underlying cardiac causes, triggered by acute MI (myocardial infarction), severe ischemia or severe hypertension; chronic heart failure exacerbations caused by nonadherence, excess salt or rapid arrhythmias; noncardiac causes are differentiated by a normal wedge pressure.
  • Recognition: severe dyspnea, cyanosis, diaphoresis and pink frothy sputum; diffuse bilateral rales, rhonchi and expiratory wheezing; chest X-ray shows butterfly pattern edema and vascular blurring; key biomarkers elevated BNP (B-type natriuretic peptide), elevated wedge pressure, hypoxemia.
  • Acute emergency interventions: position the patient upright with legs dangling over the bed; oxygen or noninvasive ventilation to maintain oxygenation; IV furosemide for immediate venodilation and diuresis; IV nitrates to rapidly reduce afterload.
  • Pharmacologic strategy and transition: temporarily hold outpatient therapy during acute kidney injury; continue oral beta-blockers unless the patient presents in shock; cautious morphine: reduces anxiety but risks severe ventilatory drive reduction.

Pulmonary edema

Defining feature: acute dyspnea with bilateral crackles, pink frothy sputum and an S3, batwing opacities and Kerley B lines, a high B-type natriuretic peptide and a wedge pressure >18–20 mmHg (cardiogenic)

Definition
Fluid flooding the alveolar airspace when outward capillary hydrostatic pressure exceeds inward oncotic pressure (cardiogenic, the pressure problem, “the pump is failing”: wedge >18–20 mmHg) or the capillary barrier shatters (noncardiogenic, the permeability problem, “the pipe is leaking”: wedge ≤18 mmHg).
Who gets it
Cardiogenic: systolic dysfunction (ischemic, reduced ejection fraction), diastolic dysfunction (stiffening and aging, preserved ejection fraction), valvular lesions. Noncardiogenic: acute respiratory distress syndrome, sepsis, inhaled toxins, high altitude.
Risk factors
Flash edema triggers: acute myocardial infarction, severe ischemia, severe hypertension; chronic heart failure exacerbation from nonadherence, excess salt or rapid arrhythmias.
Classic signs & symptoms
Acute dyspnea, diaphoresis, tripoding; pink frothy sputum (late); cyanosis.
Physical exam findings
Bilateral rales/crackles and wheezing (diffuse rales, rhonchi, expiratory wheezing in flash edema); S3 gallop.
Diagnostics / tests
B-type natriuretic peptide (substantial elevation separates cardiogenic dyspnea from pulmonary mimics); transthoracic echo (structure, ejection fraction, valves); pulmonary artery catheter / right heart catheterization when the cause is uncertain (wedge >18–20 mmHg = cardiogenic). Chest X-ray: batwing opacities, peribronchial cuffing, Kerley B lines, bilateral effusions (butterfly pattern and vascular blurring in flash edema). Mimics: pulmonary embolism (clear lungs, normal natriuretic peptide initially), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease exacerbation (wheeze, hyperinflation, hypercapnia, usually normal natriuretic peptide).
First-line treatment
LMNOP: IV loop diuretics (furosemide, bumetanide, torsemide), morphine only with caution, IV nitrates, oxygen and upright positioning (legs dangling), positive airway pressure (noninvasive ventilation). Continue oral beta-blockers unless in shock; hold outpatient therapy during acute kidney injury. Long term: quadruple therapy (ARNI, beta-blocker, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist, SGLT-2 inhibitor) for reduced ejection fraction. Elderly (preserved ejection fraction): gentle, precise diuresis.
Complications
Alveolar flooding and hypoxemia; morphine-related respiratory (ventilatory drive) depression; over-diuresis hypotension in the elderly, recurrent flooding with under-diuresis; progression to stage D (marked symptoms at rest despite maximal therapy, frequent hospitalizations), which triggers referral for heart transplant, mechanical circulatory support (left ventricular assist device) or palliative hospice care.

4.12 · Pulmonary embolism: diagnosis

The Bayesian shift (slide 52): modern pulmonary embolism (PE) diagnosis has moved from a technique-oriented approach to a Bayesian analysis. The pre-test probability, derived from clinical scoring (e.g., Wells criteria) independently of any test, dictates which diagnostic test is chosen and how its results are interpreted to give a final post-test probability. The tests: D-dimer, CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA) or a ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) scan.

The long tail: chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) (slide 61; know the decision points):

  • Screen for persistent or worsening dyspnea after 3 months of anticoagulation for PE. No dyspnea → determine the length of anticoagulation based on the risk for recurrent venous thromboembolism. The VTE masterclass: clinical pathway summary (slide 62)
1. Suspect (the threat)Recognize Virchow’s triad. Beware the 25% of patients without dyspnea. Watch for S1Q3T3 on EKG.

Pulmonary embolism

Defining feature: sudden dyspnea (absent in about 25%), pleuritic pain with hemoptysis, or collapse in a patient with Virchow risk factors; Wells decides D-dimer or CT pulmonary angiography

Definition
Embolic occlusion of the pulmonary arteries, one continuum with deep venous thrombosis (venous thromboembolism), ranging from asymptomatic disease to hemodynamic collapse and death; massive embolism causes acute cor pulmonale.
Who gets it
Patients with Virchow’s triad drivers; an objective evaluation is still owed when no predisposition is found.
Risk factors
Hypercoagulability (malignancy, estrogen therapy, pregnancy, genetic thrombophilias, sepsis); venous stasis (prolonged immobilization such as flights over 8 hours, bed rest, paralysis, heart failure class III/IV); endothelial injury (recent surgery, central venous catheters, pacemaker wires, pelvic/lower limb trauma).
Classic signs & symptoms
Three syndromes: isolated dyspnea (sudden, minutes to hours, at rest or only with exertion); pleuritic pain and hemoptysis (distal pleural-based infarction); circulatory collapse (syncope, severe hypotension, obstructive shock = massive proximal occlusion). About 25% have no dyspnea.
Physical exam findings
Localized friction rub (infarction); in massive embolism, distended neck veins, tricuspid regurgitation murmur, tachycardia and pallor (acute cor pulmonale); hypotension or pulselessness.
Diagnostics / tests
Wells first (≤4 unlikely, >4 likely). Unlikely: PERC (all eight true = stop), else a highly sensitive D-dimer (negative excludes; positive → CT pulmonary angiography); outpatient tools, D-dimer of limited use in inpatients. Likely: straight to CT pulmonary angiography (first-line for almost all), or a ventilation/perfusion scan for renal dysfunction, severe contrast allergy or the critically ill (a completely negative scan excludes embolism; often nondiagnostic in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or severe heart failure). EKG: S1Q3T3 (right ventricular strain; highly specific, low sensitivity); chest film usually abnormal but nonspecific, classic Westermark, Hampton’s hump, Palla. Echo: risk stratification (D-shaped septum), not diagnosis.
First-line treatment
Anticoagulate all, immediately on diagnosis or on high suspicion while awaiting imaging; provoked = consider stopping at 3 months, unprovoked or persistent risk = indefinite. Massive (unstable): thrombolysis (systemic or catheter-directed); if thrombolytics are absolutely contraindicated (recent surgery, active bleeding) or a right heart clot is in transit: mechanical thrombectomy or surgical embolectomy. Submassive: thrombolysis highly controversial.
Complications
Right ventricular failure, septal shift and obstructive shock; death (massive: high mortality); pulmonary infarction; recurrence; chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (screen for dyspnea at 3 months; risk: unprovoked, diagnosis delayed >2 weeks, right ventricular dysfunction on the first CT, recurrent embolism; treated by pulmonary thromboendarterectomy at an expert center). Thrombolysis: major bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage.
