  5. Discuss the related anatomy and physiology of AV nodal and infranodal conduction abnormalities.
  6. Correlate ECG findings with clinical implications of ventricular arrhythmias and AV blocks.

The one-line frame for the whole lecture. Two families of rhythm. The ventricular rhythms are about a ventricular focus taking over (ventricular tachycardia, torsades de pointes), chaos (ventricular fibrillation) or nothing at all (asystole), and whether the patient has a pulse. The atrioventricular blocks are about P waves that get through slowly (first-degree), get through until one does not (second-degree) or never get through (third-degree), and the PR interval is what tells them apart.

Numbers: rates and intervals here are the electrocardiographic criteria the lecture teaches. On the quizzes, any value in a question stem comes with its normal range: PR interval 0.12 to 0.20 seconds, QRS under 0.12 seconds, heart rate 60 to 100 per minute. Nothing is calculated.

Ventricular tachycardia

Ventricular tachycardia (the deck also calls it V-tach) refers to any tachycardia which originates in the ventricles. It can present with or without a pulse, and it can be regular and monomorphic (every complex the same shape) or irregular and polymorphic. On the strip: rate 100 to 300 per minute, regular, wide QRS complexes, no P waves (any that are present are usually dissociated, not married to the QRS) and typically no PR interval. The deck’s two worked strips run at 140 and 190 per minute.

Polymorphic ventricular tachycardia: torsades de pointes

Polymorphic ventricular tachycardia is an irregular ventricular tachycardia at 100 to 300 per minute. The form that comes with a prolonged QT (Q-to-T) interval is called torsades de pointes, French for twisting of the points, from its unique look and shape: the complexes swing from positive to negative around the baseline. ★ It is usually a consequence of a prolonged QT interval; polymorphic ventricular tachycardia with a normal QT interval, for example from ischemia, is not torsades.

Ventricular fibrillation

Ventricular fibrillation is asynchronous, chaotic firing of multiple foci within the ventricles. The heart muscle cannot contract, so it quivers; with no ventricular contraction there is no cardiac output and no pulse. There are no measurable P waves, QRS complexes or T waves: rate 300 to 500 per minute, irregular, no PR, QRS not measurable. Low-amplitude fibrillation is called fine; large chaotic waves are coarse.

Asystole

Asystole is a lack of electrical activity with no pulse, also known as flat line. Untreated, ventricular fibrillation ends in it.

Pulseless electrical activity

Pulseless electrical activity is a condition in which there is organized electrical activity on the monitor but no palpable pulse because the heart is not pumping effectively. The deck describes it as no mechanical activity; ultrasound can show weak contractions. ★ It can look like any organized rhythm, even normal sinus; if there is no pulse, it is pulseless electrical activity. So the strip alone cannot make the diagnosis: the absence of a pulse does.

Ventricular standstill

During ventricular standstill there are atrial contractions but no ventricular contractions: obvious, upright P waves march out across the strip with no QRS complexes, no detectable pulse and no cardiac output. A patient can go through episodes of paroxysmal ventricular standstill, conducted beats and standstill alternating.

Three separations to hold. Monomorphic versus polymorphic: one shape, regular versus changing shape, irregular (rate and width cannot separate them). Tachycardia versus fibrillation: organized complexes versus chaos with no QRS (P waves and a ventricular origin are shared). Fine fibrillation versus asystole: small continuous waves versus a flat line.

13.3 · Paced rhythms (on the deck, read alongside the ventricular rhythms)

  • Atrial pacemakers take over for the sinoatrial node and depend on an intact and properly functioning conduction pathway. In an atrial paced rhythm a pacer spike is followed by a normal P-QRS-T cycle, so the QRS is narrow and normal.
  • Ventricular pacemakers pace the ventricles below the level of the atria, so a ventricular paced rhythm shows a (possible) P wave, a pacer spike, a wide QRS complex and a contralateral T wave, one going the opposite direction of the QRS. ★ A spike married to every QRS, a wide QRS and a contralateral T wave show the ventricle is captured.
  • Patients with a dual-chamber pacemaker, which is common, can have both an atrial and a ventricular pacemaker spike: a spike before the P wave and another before each wide QRS.

13.4 · Objective c — Recognizing the atrioventricular blocks

Atrioventricular junction heart blocks are a delay in, or a complete failure of, the conduction of an atrial impulse through the atrioventricular node. There are four: first-degree; second-degree type I (Wenckebach, also called Mobitz I); second-degree type II (Mobitz II); and third-degree (complete heart block).

First-degree atrioventricular block

A benign PR interval delay from slowed conduction through the atrioventricular node: a delayed conduction between the sinus node and the ventricles. ★ The hallmark is a sinus rhythm with a PR interval beyond 0.2 seconds, and all the P waves are conducted; nothing is dropped. Rate slow or normal, regular, P waves present (normal and married to the QRS), QRS narrow or wide.

Second-degree type I (Wenckebach, Mobitz I)

Caused by blockage of conduction in the atrioventricular node: nodal conduction slows a little more with each beat until one impulse is not conducted (the slide attributes this to the all-or-nothing property of the cardiac cell’s depolarization; see the note at the top of this section). The classic pattern is grouped beats until a non-conducted P wave drops a QRS complex, and then the cycle begins over: a predictable progressive prolongation of the PR interval until a P wave is not conducted. Rate slow or normal; irregular (from the dropped beat); P waves present, normal and married to the QRS except for the dropped beat; PR increasing; QRS narrow or wide.

Second-degree type II (Mobitz II)

Less common but more serious than type I; it is possible to have more than one dropped beat in a row, and it is almost always a sign of underlying disease of the conduction system. ★ The hallmark is a constant PR interval with some non-conducted P waves followed by dropped QRS complexes. Type II is identified when at least two consecutive atrial impulses are conducted with constant PR intervals before the dropped QRS. Rate slow or normal; irregular; P waves present, normal and married to the QRS except for the dropped beats; PR normal or wide, but always constant across the strip; QRS wide or narrow — usually, not always, wide from an accompanying bundle branch block.

Third-degree atrioventricular block (complete heart block)

Atrial and ventricular activity are totally independent: there is a total blockage, and no P waves are conducted through the atrioventricular junction. ★ The hallmark is regular P-P intervals unrelated to regular R-R intervals, with P waves that appear to march through the QRS-T complexes. The atrial rate is faster than the ventricular rate, and there are two independent pacemakers: the sinoatrial node, and either the atrioventricular junction or the Purkinje and bundle-branch fibers. Regular; no PR interval; QRS narrow or wide depending on the origin of the escape.

The cheat code: type I or type II? (slide 50) Locate the dropped beat (the non-conducted P wave). Compare the PR interval of the cycle before the dropped beat with the PR interval of the cycle after it. ★ If the PR intervals are different in duration, it is always second-degree type I; if they are equal, it is second-degree type II, except in 2:1 block, where the two beats cannot tell you the type. In type I the PR is longest just before the drop and resets shorter after it.

His two questions (a shortcut, not a slide): more P waves than QRS complexes suggests a block; then lengthening PR = type I, constant PR = type II, randomly changing PR = third-degree. A long PR with every P conducted is first-degree.

13.6 · Objective e — Nodal versus infranodal conduction

  • First-degree block sits in the atrioventricular node: slowed conduction, every impulse still gets through.
  • Second-degree type I sits in the atrioventricular node, where conduction slows beat by beat until one impulse fails; he described it as the node progressively fatiguing until it does not release an impulse, then resetting. The P waves march out on time; the node is what falls behind.
  • Second-degree type II is infranodal: low, below the atrioventricular node, at the His bundle, generally in the His-Purkinje system (slide 36). It reflects disease of the conduction system.
  • Third-degree block is a total blockage at the junction. The sinoatrial node keeps driving the atria; the ventricles are driven by an escape pacemaker below the block, in the atrioventricular junction (a narrow QRS) or the Purkinje and bundle-branch fibers (a wide QRS, as in slide 43’s ventricular escape).
  • Ventricular tachycardia comes from a ventricular focus. Its impulse often does not travel back up into the atria, so the atria usually carry on doing their own thing and any P waves are usually dissociated from the QRS complexes (in some ventricular tachycardia the impulse does conduct backward to the atria).
