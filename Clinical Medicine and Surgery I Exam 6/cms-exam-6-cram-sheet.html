★ Professor Emphasized — Who Gets Oxygen, Who Qualifies, Fire

TermWhat you need to know
★ IndicationsHypoxia; acute medical or surgical needs (perioperative, postoperative); respiratory conditions (carbon monoxide poisoning); special indications without hypoxia: air hunger (end of life) and cluster headache. “One of the big things.”
★ FireBenefit: correction of hypoxia, symptom relief, therapeutic benefit. Risk: fire hazard — “probably one of the biggest risks.” Also hypercapnia (carbon dioxide retention), mucosal irritation, vasoconstriction.
Myocardial infarctionVasoconstriction: not given unless saturation is below 91% (the slide’s value; current guidelines use 90%).
★ Home qualificationDocumented hypoxia by arterial blood gas or pulse oximetry at rest, with exertion and/or during sleep, performed at the time of need. Rest: saturation ≤ 88% on room air or partial pressure of oxygen ≤ 55 mm Hg. Exertion: ≤ 88% and improves with oxygen. Sleep: ≤ 88% with a drop of 5% or more.
Group I vs IIGroup I (qualifies): ≤ 55 mm Hg or ≤ 88% → recertify at 12 months. Group II (conditional): 56–59 mm Hg or 89% plus pulmonary hypertension, cor pulmonale (right heart failure) or polycythemia (hematocrit above 56%) → recertify at 3 months.

Delivery Devices

TermWhat you need to know
Varies or fixedNasal cannula, simple mask: fraction varies with respiratory rate, inspiratory flow, tidal volume. Partial rebreather, non-rebreather: varies with patient demand. Air entrainment (Venturi, air-entrainment nebulizer): fixed, does not fluctuate.
Nasal cannulaLow flow, 1–6 L/min, 24–44% (room air 21%; about +4% per liter: 1 = 24, 2 = 28, 3 = 32, 4 = 36, 5 = 40, 6 = 44). Maxes at 44% even above 6 L/min. Humidity above 4 L/min. Mild hypoxia; most commonly used.
Simple maskLow flow, 5–10 L/min, .35–.50 (5–6 L/min 35%, 6–7 40%, 7–8 50%). Mild to moderate respiratory distress.
Partial rebreatherSome exhaled gas gets into the reservoir bag; not commonly used. Its fraction differs between slides (60–80% vs .40–.70): never tested.
Non-rebreatherLow flow device, but with a high fraction: 10–15 L/min = 60–100%. Reservoir bag + one-way valve (no inhaling exhaled air). Flow set to keep the bag from collapsing. Severely hypoxic patients still ventilating well.
Venturi maskHigh flow, precise; ports change the fraction; 4–10 L/min → 24–50%; total flow above inspiratory demand. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (precision).
Air-entrainment nebulizerAerosol mask, open face mask, T-piece, tracheostomy collar. Can deliver higher but not recommended above .50.
Advanced airwaysBag-valve mask (resuscitator bag); oropharyngeal and nasopharyngeal airways; endotracheal tube, tracheostomy; tube changer and bougie; laryngeal mask airway.

Nebulizers, Response & Education

TermWhat you need to know
Why a nebulizerLiquid medication directly to the airways; medication chosen for the specific treatment; secretion clearance; inhalers are challenging in certain situations; severe exacerbations of respiratory illness.
Is it working?Clinical: symptoms, work of breathing, color and perfusion. Objective: saturation improves (target varies); blood gas shows a higher oxygen without a higher carbon dioxide and a corrected pH. Vital signs; watch for complications.
★ Home safetyHighly flammable, compressed gas. No smoking; “No Smoking” and “Oxygen in Use” signs. At least 10 feet from heat sources (pilot lights, electric appliances, candles). No petroleum products (lip balms, lotions, oils, grease). Smoke and carbon monoxide alarms tested monthly. Proper storage and disposal.

★ Professor Emphasized — Normals, Winter Formula, Anion Gap

TermWhat you need to know
★ Normal valuespH 7.35–7.45 (“burn this into your eyeballs”); carbon dioxide 36–44 mm Hg (midpoint 40); bicarbonate 22–26 mEq/L (midpoint 24). Acidemia below 7.35, alkalemia above 7.45.
★ Winter formulaMetabolic acidosis: expected carbon dioxide = 1.5 × bicarbonate + 8, ± 2. Higher than expected = added respiratory acidosis, can signal impending respiratory failure.
★ Anion gapSodium − (chloride + bicarbonate), normal 9–15. Above 15 = elevated-gap acidosis.
★ Treatment“Always going to be correct the underlying disorder.” Therapy aimed at the pH itself only when it is unsustainable: below 6.7 or above 7.6.
★ Frequent questionsObesity hypoventilation triad; paper bag = placebo; saline responsive is the common alkalosis; carbon dioxide rarely above 55 in compensation; urine chloride splits the alkaloses.

Reading the Gas & Compensation

TermWhat you need to know
Steps1. pH: acidosis or alkalosis. 2. Which value (carbon dioxide or bicarbonate) matches the pH. 3. Mixed? pH more or less than expected. 4. Compensation: respiratory disorder → expected bicarbonate; metabolic disorder → expected carbon dioxide. 5. Don’t neglect the patient.
RulesCompensation never returns pH to normal and never past it; respiratory cause → renal compensation; metabolic cause → respiratory compensation. Normal pH with abnormal values = mixed disorder.
Ratios (given in questions)Respiratory acidosis: bicarbonate +1 per 10 mm Hg acute, +3.5 chronic. Respiratory alkalosis: −2 per 10 acute, −5 chronic. Metabolic acidosis: carbon dioxide −1.3 per 1 mEq/L (use Winter). Metabolic alkalosis: +0.75 per 1 mEq/L, rarely above 55 (above = concurrent respiratory acidosis).
TimingRenal compensation begins 6–12 hours after onset, complete over days: small bicarbonate change = acute, large = chronic.
AlgorithmpH below 7.4 + carbon dioxide above 40 = respiratory acidosis (acute or chronic?); below 40 = compensating metabolic acidosis (check the gap). pH above 7.4 + carbon dioxide above 40 = compensating metabolic alkalosis; below 40 = respiratory alkalosis.

Respiratory Acidosis & Alkalosis

TermWhat you need to know
Respiratory acidosisHypoventilation → carbon dioxide above 40 → low pH, ± hypoxemia. Somnolence, confusion, altered mental status, asterixis, myoclonus; severe: raised cerebral blood flow and intracranial pressure, papilledema. Labs: low pH, high carbon dioxide, high bicarbonate, hypochloremia if chronic (ammonium chloride excretion).
Acute causesAirway obstruction; pneumonia, asthma, pulmonary edema, aspiration pneumonitis; central nervous system depression (recreational drugs, intracranial catastrophe, intravenous sedation); myasthenia gravis, Guillain-Barré syndrome; pneumothorax, flail chest.
Chronic causesChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (“first thing that comes to mind”), interstitial lung disease, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis; muscular dystrophy, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis; ★ obesity hypoventilation (Pickwickian) syndrome: obesity + sleep disordered breathing + chronic hypercapnia.
TreatUnderlying cause. Acute: restore ventilation. Chronic: careful oxygen titration; avoid rapid carbon dioxide reduction → post-hypercapnic metabolic alkalosis.
Respiratory alkalosisHyperventilation → low carbon dioxide → high pH. Light-headedness, anxiety, perioral numbness, paresthesias; severe: tetany (high pH binds calcium to albumin). Compensation: cells trade hydrogen ions for potassium, then kidneys excrete bicarbonate and keep chloride (low bicarbonate, hypokalemia, hyperchloremia).
Alkalosis causesPsychogenic, stroke, raised intracranial pressure; hypoxia (high altitude, severe anemia, ventilation-perfusion mismatch, hypotension); salicylates, caffeine, nicotine, catecholamines, thyroxine; pulmonary embolism, pneumonia, pulmonary edema, mechanical hyperventilation, interstitial lung disease; pregnancy, hyperthyroidism; septicemia, heat exposure, hepatic failure.
TreatUnderlying cause; usually self-limited. ★ Paper bag not necessary: does not correct carbon dioxide, may lower oxygen, placebo. Rapid correction of chronic alkalosis → post-hypocapnia metabolic acidosis.

Metabolic Alkalosis & Acidosis

TermWhat you need to know
Metabolic alkalosisHigh bicarbonate → high pH. No characteristic findings; orthostatic hypotension; hypokalemia → weakness, hyporeflexia. Labs: high pH and bicarbonate, raised carbon dioxide, low potassium, low chloride.
★ Urine chlorideBelow 25 mEq/L = saline responsive (more common; volume contraction): diuretics, post-hypercapnia, vomiting or nasogastric suction, antacids, volume contraction. Above 40 = saline unresponsive (euvolemic or hypervolemic) → check blood pressure.
Unresponsive groupsNormotensive: severe potassium depletion; hypoparathyroidism and hypercalcemia. Hypertensive: primary aldosteronism, exogenous alkali, mineralocorticoids, licorice (“a unicorn”).
Treat alkalosisUnderlying cause; saline responsive: isotonic saline and stop diuretics.
Metabolic acidosisLow bicarbonate → low pH, low carbon dioxide; hyperkalemia may be present. Signs of the cause + compensatory hyperventilation; Kussmaul respirations (increased tidal volume and tachypnea) in diabetic ketoacidosis. Normal gap = hyperchloremic; elevated gap = normochloremic.
Elevated gap: MUDPILESMethanol, metformin · uremia · diabetic ketoacidosis · paraldehyde · isoniazid, iron · lactic acidosis · ethylene glycol · salicylates, starvation.
Normal gap: USEDCARUreteral diversion · saline infusion · exogenous acid · diarrhea · carbonic anhydrase inhibitors · adrenal insufficiency · renal tubular acidosis (bicarbonate loss, chloride retention).
Gap pitfallsAlbumin makes most of the normal gap; hypoalbuminemia, sodium extremes and antibiotics distort it. A normal gap does not exclude acidosis and should not override clinical concern. Fewer unmeasured cations (calcium, magnesium, potassium) raise it.

Electrolytes & Oxygen

TermWhat you need to know
PotassiumAcidosis: hydrogen into cells, potassium out → hyperkalemia. Alkalosis: potassium into cells → hypokalemia.
OxygenAcidosis: lower hemoglobin affinity, curve right, more oxygen to tissues. Alkalosis: higher affinity, curve left, less oxygen to tissues.
Unsustainable pHBelow 6.7 or above 7.6: cardiac hypocontractility and irritability, seizures, cerebral edema, metabolic disturbances.
BuffersChemical buffering (extracellular, intracellular), respiratory (carbon dioxide, fast), renal (bicarbonate, hours to days). Water + carbon dioxide ↔ carbonic acid ↔ hydrogen ion + bicarbonate. Brainstem chemoreceptor: more carbon dioxide → lower cerebral pH → more breathing. Hypercapnia usually from under-excretion.

★ Professor Emphasized — Severity, Education, Classes

TermWhat you need to know
★ Severity from a descriptionTable for 12 years and older; severity goes to the most severe category in which any feature occurs. Intermittent = “rule of twos” (2 or fewer days/week, 2 or fewer nights/month). Focus on mild and moderate.
★ Persistent cut-offsMild: symptoms more than 2 days/week but not daily; 3–4 nights/month; 1-second volume above 80%. Moderate: daily; more than once/week but not nightly; 60–80%. Severe: all day; often 7/week; below 60%.
★ Patient educationWritten action plan; inhaler, spacer and nebulizer technique; peak flow zones; triggers; rinse the mouth after an inhaled steroid; vaccines. “Really important is the patient education.”
★ Classes, not brandsKnow the class: short-acting beta-2 agonist (rescue), inhaled corticosteroid (first-line controller), long-acting beta-2 agonist (never alone), leukotriene modifier (add-on).
Not testedEpidemiology numbers; emergency management (slides 97–105); age-normal ratio values; any question with conflicting rescue-inhaler guidance.

Definition, Types & Triggers

TermWhat you need to know
DefinitionChronic inflammatory airway disorder, reversible obstruction (some long-standing cases irreversible); trouble getting air out. Hyper-responsiveness: excessive constriction to a trigger a normal airway ignores.
CourseAny age, peak 3 years; may quiet in adolescence and return. Severity rarely changes: mild rarely becomes severe; severe usually severe at onset.
Death riskFrequent bronchodilator use; no or poorly taken inhaled steroid; previous near-fatal admission.
Extrinsic vs intrinsicExtrinsic: allergen-triggered, atopic, most common (atopic triad: rhinitis, asthma, eczema). Intrinsic: about 10%, adult onset, negative skin tests, normal immunoglobulin E, nasal polyps, may be aspirin sensitive.
Other typesOccupational (better weekends and vacations); catamenial (menstrual cycle); “cardiac” (decompensated heart failure); exercise-induced (starts within 3 minutes, peaks 10–15, gone by 60); aspirin-sensitive (1–5%, minutes to 12 hours).
TriggersDust mites (most common), pet dander, cockroaches, pollens, fungal spores; viruses; aspirin and beta blockers; exercise; cold air; pollution; smoke; stress; occupational agents.
Risk factorsAtopy; obesity (body mass index above 30); early viral infection. Allergen avoidance does not prevent asthma; early exposure may protect.

Diagnosis & Monitoring

TermWhat you need to know
ExamOften normal between attacks. Exacerbation: accessory muscles, leaning forward; wheeze may vanish (decreased breath sounds, prolonged expiration). Worse at night (peak 3–4 am).
SpirometryBefore and after a short-acting bronchodilator; reduced 1-second volume and ratio; 12% or more improvement supports asthma. Effort-dependent: coach. Repeat yearly.
Other testsProvocation (methacholine, histamine) if spirometry is not diagnostic, not below 65% predicted; exercise challenge for exercise-induced bronchospasm; allergy testing for triggers; exhaled nitric oxide = inflammation; chest x-ray normal or hyperinflated.
Peak flowHome baseline; morning before, afternoon after the bronchodilator; 20% change between them or day to day = poor control.
Action planGreen 80% or more of personal best; yellow 50–79% (add quick-relief, keep controller); red under 50%, very short of breath or quick-relief failing (medical alert). Danger signs: cannot walk or talk, blue lips or nails.
Visit sequenceInitial: diagnose, classify, start medicine and demonstrate, written plan, schedule follow-up. Follow-up: control, compliance, adjust, update plan.
DifferentialVocal fold dysfunction or paralysis, foreign body, laryngotracheal mass; chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis; conversion disorder, emotional laryngeal wheezing, factitious disorder; aspergillosis.
AspergillosisAllergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis: hypersensitivity to Aspergillus fumigatus, almost only in asthma or cystic fibrosis; vigorous immunoglobulin E and G response; exacerbations.

Treatment by Class

TermWhat you need to know
RescueEvery patient needs a short-acting beta-2 agonist (albuterol); 3–6 hours; before exercise prevents exercise-induced bronchospasm; rising use = poor control; tremor, palpitations.
Inhaled corticosteroidPreferred first-line for persistent asthma; twice daily; full effect takes months; hoarseness, cough, thrush → spacer, rinse and spit.
Systemic steroidExacerbations: short course, no taper. Long-term only if refractory, minimum dose, with calcium and vitamin D. Side effects: truncal obesity, osteoporosis, bruising, ulcers, depression, hypertension.
Long-acting beta-2 agonistUp to 12 hours; never alone (no anti-inflammatory effect); pair with inhaled steroid.
Other controllersCromolyn: prevents, does not relieve. Tiotropium: slow, weak, few side effects. Theophylline: narrow range; nausea, vomiting, arrhythmias, seizures, high glucose, low potassium. Leukotriene modifiers: add-on, helpful for exercise-induced. Monoclonal antibodies: specialist, spare steroids.
AvoidAspirin and non-selective anti-inflammatory drugs (ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac, indomethacin) in aspirin-sensitive asthma; selective ones (celecoxib, meloxicam) allowed. Beta blockers.
Immunotherapy & vaccinesImmunotherapy: allergic triggers, single allergen, controlled setting only. Influenza yearly and pneumococcal; caution with intranasal influenza vaccine.
Poor controlCheck compliance and technique first (most common reason); steroid resistance is very rare; refractory asthma → specialist.

★ Key Numbers

TermWhat you need to know
★ DiagnosisSpirometry before and after a bronchodilator: ratio of 1-second volume to forced vital capacity below 0.70; response under 12% = irreversible. Chronic bronchitis without obstruction is not this disease.
★ Airflow gradePost-bronchodilator 1-second volume: 1 mild ≥80%; 2 moderate 50–79%; 3 severe 30–49%; 4 very severe <30%.
★ Symptoms & riskAssessment test ≥10 or dyspnea grade ≥2 = more symptoms. High risk = 2 or more moderate exacerbations or 1 hospitalization.
★ Groups2020: A low/low, B high symptoms/low risk, C low symptoms/high risk, D high/high. 2023–2026: A, B, and E = any high risk. “S × R = group.”
★ StartA: one bronchodilator. B: long-acting beta agonist + long-acting antimuscarinic. E: that pair, add inhaled steroid if eosinophils ≥300.
★ SurvivalOnly smoking cessation, long-term oxygen (severe resting hypoxemia) and noninvasive ventilation (hypercapnic exacerbation) improve survival.

Subtypes, Causes & Presentation

TermWhat you need to know
DefinitionPersistent airflow obstruction + respiratory symptoms from inhaled particles; umbrella of chronic bronchitis (cough ≥3 months/year for 2 years) and emphysema (alveolar destruction).
Blue bloaterChronic bronchitis: sputum, rhonchi, cyanosis, edema; early hypoxemia and hypercapnia; normal diffusing capacity; bronchial wall thickening.
Pink pufferEmphysema: thin, pursed lips, barrel chest, distant sounds, hyperresonance; gas exchange late; reduced diffusing capacity; flat diaphragm, bullae.
CausesTobacco ~90% (primary); biomass smoke (low-income countries, never-smokers); coal, gold mining, welding; inactivity. Cessation is the key treatment.
Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiencyAge ≤45, nonsmoker, basilar panlobular emphysema, family history; level <11 micromol/L + deficient genotype. Smokers: centrilobular, upper lobe.
Early signDyspnea on exertion. Not always thin; not always productive. Pursed lips keep airways open. Screen: heart disease, lung cancer, osteoporosis, depression, reflux, anemia.
Exam extrasFlat diaphragm → lower intercostal retraction on inspiration; neck veins distend in expiration; cor pulmonale; syncope and falls (poor perfusion).

Tests

TermWhat you need to know
PatternsLow ratio = obstruction. Obstruction + low forced vital capacity = possible mixed (confirm with total lung capacity). Normal ratio + low forced vital capacity = possible restriction.
PhysiologyAir trapping (residual volume up), hyperinflation, dynamic hyperinflation with exertion; scooped expiratory limb.
Other testsBlood gas only if deteriorating (carbon dioxide >45, bicarbonate up = compensatory metabolic alkalosis); blood count (anemia, eosinophils); B-type natriuretic peptide for heart failure; chest film rules out other causes; computed tomography rarely needed.
ScoresBODE (body mass index, obstruction, dyspnea, exercise) → 4-year survival: 0–2 = 80%, 3–4 = 67%, 5–6 = 57%, 7–10 = 18%. 1-second volume ≥65 = 0, 50–64 = 1, 36–49 = 2, ≤35 = 3; walk ≥350 m = 0, 150–249 = 2, ≤149 = 3; dyspnea 0–1 = 0, 2 = 1, 3 = 2, 4 = 3; body mass index ≤21 = 1.

Management

TermWhat you need to know
Non-drugCessation (essential, all groups, with counseling); rehabilitation essential for B and E; activity; yearly influenza vaccine; pneumococcal at 65 (earlier if 1-second volume <40% or cardiac disease).
Five A's / FRAMESAsk, Advise, Assess, Assist, Arrange. Feedback, Responsibility, Advice, Menu, Empathy, Self-efficacy.
Drug cautionsLong-acting beta agonist: arrhythmia, worse atrial fibrillation. Antimuscarinic: dry mouth, anticholinergic burden in elders. Inhaled steroid: never alone, pneumonia, fractures. Albuterol: not excessively. Oral steroids: exacerbations only.
Older adultsFixed 0.70 ratio may overdiagnose; arthritis, tremor, cognition, low flow limit dry powder inhalers → spacer, soft mist, nebulizer; refer to rehabilitation actively; palliative care early.
OxygenTreat arterial oxygen <55 mmHg or saturation <88%. Rest ≤88% → long-term oxygen >15 hours/day; rest 89–93% → none; exertion ≤88% → with exertion; night only → sleep apnea workup.
Refer to pulmonologyUncertain diagnosis, early onset or deficiency, exacerbations despite optimal therapy, oxygen, noninvasive ventilation, surgery, symptoms out of proportion.

Exacerbations

TermWhat you need to know
DefinitionAcute worsening over 14 days or less; exclude pneumonia, embolism, acute heart failure, pneumothorax. >80% outpatient.
Admit / intensive careAdmit: marked symptoms, noninvasive ventilation, new edema, arrhythmia, comorbidity, no home support. Intensive care: unstable, major infection, invasive ventilation, sepsis, altered mental status, acidosis.
TreatBronchodilators, steroids, oxygen; antibiotics if more and purulent sputum (doxycycline, macrolide, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, amoxicillin-clavulanate; levofloxacin for pseudomonal concern); high-flow for hypoxia; noninvasive ventilation for hypercapnia.
DischargeAlbuterol no more than every 4 hours; stable 12–24 hours; walks across room; eats and sleeps; home arrangements. Recovery 4–6 weeks.
