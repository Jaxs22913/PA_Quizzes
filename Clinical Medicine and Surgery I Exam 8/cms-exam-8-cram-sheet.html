Slide conflict: slide 13 gives anemia of chronic disease a low serum iron and transferrin saturation with a normal or low TIBC; slide 27 gives its serum iron as “↑ or ↓”. TIBC also differs: normal or low on slide 13, ↓ on slide 27 (never above normal on either). Both agree on ferritin (normal or ↑): tell them apart by ferritin with TIBC, never by serum iron alone. Iron Deficiency Anemia

TermWhat you need to know
CausesChronic blood loss (gastrointestinal, menses; most common in adults); diet (vegan, tea, calcium antacids, proton pump inhibitors); malabsorption (gastric bypass, celiac sprue, food allergies); demand (pregnancy, growth in infancy or adolescence).
Who50% of anemia worldwide; ~1 million deaths a year (71% Africa and Asia). United States: men under 50 1%, women of childbearing age 10%, 9% of children 12–36 months (1/3 of them anemic); low-income families at risk.
SignsFatigue, pallor, exercise intolerance, dyspnea on exertion, smooth tongue, pica (common), koilonychia (spoon nails), cheilosis, Plummer-Vinson syndrome.
DifferentialAnemia of chronic disease, thalassemia, lead poisoning, zinc deficiency, sideroblastic anemia.
DiagnosisMicrocytosis (MCV <80), hypochromia (↓ MCH); ↓ ferritin, ↓ transferrin saturation, ↑ total iron-binding capacity; microcytic hypochromic smear. Also urinalysis, fecal occult blood (stool guaiac), consider prothrombin and partial thromboplastin times.
TreatThe cause: oral contraceptive (consider continuous-cycle) for menses; gastrointestinal bleed → colonoscopy or endoscopy for colorectal cancer; stop anticoagulants if severe (consult cardiology). Then iron replacement.
ParenteralIf intolerant or refractory; over 15 minutes to 1 hour. Ferric pyrophosphate citrate (Triferic) goes in the dialysate to replace the 5–7 mg lost per hemodialysis.

Anemia of Chronic Disease (Inflammation)

TermWhat you need to know
CauseAny inflammatory condition: infection, autoimmune, neoplasm. Interleukin-6 → hepcidin ↑ blocks iron absorption; inflammation inhibits renal erythropoietin; direct marrow suppression.
Who2nd most common anemia; any age; older adults at increased risk (~1 million over 65 in the United States).
PictureInitially resembles iron deficiency; symptoms like iron deficiency plus the causative condition.
DiagnosisSame labs as iron deficiency; erythrocyte sedimentation rate and/or C-reactive protein if inflammation not apparent; ferritin normal or increased; ferritin <30 ng/mL = coexistent iron deficiency.
TreatThe underlying condition; erythropoietin if indicated (hemoglobin <10); maybe parenteral iron; hematology usually not needed.

Sideroblastic Anemia & Lead Poisoning

TermWhat you need to know
WhoRare; about 2/3 males in early childhood or adolescence, 1/3 young to middle-aged females; any age.
PictureNothing specific beyond anemia; usually moderate, hematocrit 20–30%.
DiagnosisVariable microcytosis; dimorphic smear (microcytic, normal and macrocytic cells); ↑ serum iron, transferrin saturation, ferritin; zinc ↑ if from copper deficiency; ring sideroblasts on marrow aspirate = defining.
TreatRemove the agent; transfusion if severe; poor response to erythropoietin; occasional response to pyridoxine (50–200 mg/day); luspatercept for myelodysplastic syndrome with ring sideroblasts; hematology for diagnosis or transfusion.
Lead (plumbism)Microcytic hypochromic anemia with basophilic stippling (abnormal nucleic acid collections). Sources: industrial, gasoline sniffing, lodged bullets, lead cookware, paint chips, Flint water.
Lead effectsNeurologic most vulnerable (irritability, behavior change, peripheral neuropathy); abdominal colic, nausea, constipation; lead line in the gums.
Lead treatmentStop exposure; by lead level; severe with neurologic effects → chelation: succimer (Chemet), dimercaprol, ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA).

Macrocytic, Megaloblastic & Folate

TermWhat you need to know
MacrocyticMCV >100: megaloblastic (B12, folate) or non-megaloblastic (alcohol or drug, hypothyroidism, liver failure, marrow dysplasia; usually <115).
SmearMacro-ovalocytes and hypersegmented neutrophils (slides 39–40).
Folate causesDiet (impoverished, alcohol use disorder, elderly); pregnancy; proximal small bowel malabsorption; drugs: methotrexate, 5-fluorouracil, hydroxyurea, phenytoin, metformin.
Folate replacementFolic acid 1 mg orally or parenterally daily for 1–2 weeks, after checking for B12 deficiency.

Vitamin B12 & Pernicious Anemia

TermWhat you need to know
AbsorptionPeptic digestion frees B12 → intrinsic factor from parietal cells binds it → binds cubilin in the distal ileum → transcobalamin → stored in the liver (~3–4 years or more).
Pernicious anemiaMost frequent cause of B12 deficiency: autoimmune attack on parietal cells and/or intrinsic factor → gastric atrophy and increased gastric cancer risk.
ClinicalAnemia findings; glossitis, oral ulcers. Distinguishing: neurologic (dorsal and lateral cord demyelination): neuropsychiatric change, stocking-and-glove numbness, tingling, burning of the hands and feet, then unsteady gait and lost position sense; may not improve with B12.
ReplacementB12 1000 mcg subcutaneously or intramuscularly weekly for 3 weeks; or oral cobalamin 1000–2000 mcg daily.

Chronic Kidney Disease & Aplastic Anemia

TermWhat you need to know
Kidney: orderComplete blood count, reticulocytes, ferritin and transferrin saturation; assess other causes (slide 53).
Kidney: treatCorrect iron deficiency; oral or intravenous iron and an erythropoiesis-stimulating agent by stage, hemoglobin and risk; monitor hemoglobin, iron and blood pressure; nephrology for advanced disease.
Aplastic: whatPancytopenia from loss of hematopoietic stem cells (the defining feature).
Aplastic: whoHalf in the first three decades (peaks in childhood and at 20–25); male:female ~1:1; 0.6–6.1 per million.
Aplastic: causesAutoimmune; stem cell injury (cytotoxic drugs, chloramphenicol, ionizing radiation, benzene, pesticides); viruses; clonal (paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, myelodysplastic syndrome, acute myeloid leukemia); inherited (Fanconi anemia, childhood).
Aplastic: pictureRecurrent infections (neutropenia), bleeding, petechiae, purpura (thrombocytopenia), fatigue, pallor.
Aplastic: planBone marrow biopsy required; urgent hematology/oncology; treatment = bone marrow transplant.

Leukopenia & Neutropenia

TermWhat you need to know
CausesViral illness, medications or chemotherapy, autoimmune disease, nutrient deficits, marrow pathology.
WorkupRepeat complete blood count with differential, trend, peripheral smear, other cell lines.
Chronic careStop an offending drug; correct deficiencies; selected patients need G-CSF (granulocyte colony-stimulating factor).
TeachReport fever or rigors promptly; monitor counts.
PrognosisTransient viral leukopenia often resolves; persistent or severe neutropenia = more infection risk, may mean marrow disease.

Hemolytic Anemias & Autoimmune Hemolysis

TermWhat you need to know
DefinitionShortened red cell survival from premature destruction. Red cell ~6 µm vs capillaries ~2 µm.
Red cell defensesDeformable membrane and cytoskeleton (spectrin, ankyrin); strong redox environment; biconcave disc (~40% more surface area).
All hemolytic anemias↓ red cell life span, compensatory ↑ erythropoiesis, retention of breakdown products (iron); marrow hyperplasia and ↑ reticulocytes; extramedullary hematopoiesis (liver, spleen, lymph nodes) if severe and chronic.
RoutesExtravascular (more common; spleen and liver macrophages → unconjugated bilirubin); intravascular (free hemoglobin → haptoglobin; some into urine).
PresentationAnemia symptoms; severe: chest pain, high-output heart failure, shock. Differentiating: jaundice, splenomegaly, joint pain, hematuria.
AutoimmuneAntibody against the red cell membrane; primary (de novo, almost always IgG) or secondary; transfusion reaction. Coombs positive; venous thromboembolism risk.

G6PD Deficiency

TermWhat you need to know
WhatMost common genetic disorder of erythrocytes (200–400 million); X-linked recessive, so generally only males are affected; carrier females generally unaffected.
MechanismNo reduced glutathione → oxidative damage → Heinz bodies inside the cell; splenic macrophages bite out a chunk (bite cells); intravascular and extravascular hemolysis.
PrevalenceKurdish Jews 60–70%; Sardinians 4–35%; Nigerians 22%; Thai 17%; American Black people 11–12%; Japanese and Koreans 0–1%.
SymptomsNone until oxidative stress; then abdominal pain, jaundice, dark urine, splenomegaly.
Treat“Don’t eat that” (avoid the trigger); hydrate; transfuse if needed; splenectomy only for recurrent attacks.

Thalassemia

TermWhat you need to know
WhatMutations cut alpha or beta globin synthesis; most erythroblasts die in the marrow; globin imbalance → subunits precipitate → destruction in the spleen.
CourseHemolytic anemia, iron overload, splenomegaly, extramedullary hematopoiesis, jaundice, skeletal changes (pictured on slide 87).
TreatSupportive: transfusion only when absolutely necessary (worsens iron overload); folic acid; genetic counseling; preconception testing with a family history.

Sickle Cell Disease

TermWhat you need to know
ClinicalAnemia almost universal, hematocrit 15–30%; pain crises (microvascular occlusion: pain, tenderness, anxiety, tachycardia).
InfarctionSplenic infarct; aseptic necrosis of the femoral or humeral heads; retinopathy; kidney disease; leg ulcers; priapism; susceptibility to encapsulated bacteria.
StrokePediatric stroke 300× unaffected children; 25% have a stroke by age 45.
Acute chest syndromeLeading cause of death; often with a pain event; >10% adult mortality; infection, infarction, pulmonary or fat embolism; chest pain, fever, dyspnea, hypoxemia, pulmonary infiltrates on a chest X-ray.
ManagementHydroxyurea raises HbF and reduces vaso-occlusive events; vaccines and childhood penicillin prophylaxis; transfusion or exchange for selected severe events; red flags: fever, chest pain or hypoxemia, new neurologic deficit.

Hemochromatosis

TermWhat you need to know
WhatIron overload: excess deposition → oxidative stress, fibrosis, organ dysfunction; asymptomatic early. Liver, heart, joints, endocrine organs, skin.
SignsFatigue; skin bronzing (face, neck, groin, extensor forearms and lower legs); joint pain, 2nd and 3rd metacarpophalangeal joints and knees; cardiomyopathy.
PearlPituitary iron → hypogonadotropic hypogonadism: low libido, impotence, amenorrhea, small testes, gynecomastia, less body hair.
FamilyConsider HFE testing with elevated transferrin saturation or ferritin; offer first-degree relative screening.
TeachAvoid iron supplements and excess alcohol. Early treatment prevents organ damage; assess fibrosis, monitor cirrhosis for liver cancer.
