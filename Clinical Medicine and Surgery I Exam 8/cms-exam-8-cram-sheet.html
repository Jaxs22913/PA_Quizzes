RoutesExtravascular (more common; spleen and liver macrophages → unconjugated bilirubin); intravascular (free hemoglobin → haptoglobin; some into urine).
PresentationAnemia symptoms; severe: chest pain, high-output heart failure, shock. Differentiating: jaundice, splenomegaly, joint pain, hematuria.
AutoimmuneAntibody against the red cell membrane; primary (de novo, almost always IgG) or secondary; transfusion reaction. Coombs positive; venous thromboembolism risk.

Hematopoiesis & the Malignancy Families

TermWhat you need to know
ClonalityOne oncogenic mutation in a single somatic cell gives a clonal advantage; the clone expands and gathers more alterations (slide 6).
DefinitionCancers of blood-forming tissues or the lymphatic system from a monoclonal hematologic clone; leukemias, lymphomas, plasma cell disorders; cell of origin sets the type (lymphoid or myeloid) (slide 7).
Acute vs chronicAcute = immature progenitor white cells, rapid proliferation; chronic = mature malignant white cells. Crowding causes anemia, thrombocytopenia, most commonly leukocytosis (slide 14).
LymphomaLymphoid tissue (nodes, spleen, thymus); >100 subtypes; non-Hodgkin 90% (B and T cell), Hodgkin 10% (few Reed-Sternberg cells among immune cells); lymphadenopathy, night sweats, weight loss, fevers (slide 15).
MyelomaPlasma cells (terminally differentiated B cells); one monoclonal antibody, the M protein; CRAB: hypercalcemia, renal dysfunction, anemia, bone pain; infections (slide 16).
Epidemiology9.4% of new United States cancers (2025); ~1.8 million living with one; 9.1% of cancer deaths (slide 8).
Risk factorsRadiation, benzene; family history, trisomies; autoimmune disorders; older age (exceptions: ALL, some lymphomas) (slide 9).
PresentationNon-specific: fatigue (anemia), bleeding (thrombocytopenia), swollen nodes (lymphoma), bone pain (myeloma); incidental cytopenias or high protein (myeloma). Almost all need bone marrow biopsy and/or flow cytometry (slide 11).
TransplantHSCT = bone marrow transplant, after high-dose chemotherapy +/- radiation. Allogeneic = donor cells; autologous = own cells (“stem cell rescue”) (slide 12).

Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL)

TermWhat you need to know
WhatLymphoid progenitor/stem cell leukemia; very immature, rapidly proliferating; B-cell precursor (leukemic, marrow) or T-cell (rarer, lymphoma-like, thymus, mediastinal mass); lymphoblastic lymphoma subtype (slides 18–19, 22).
WhoBimodal: most common malignancy under 15 (5-year survival >90%); adult peak ~50, poor over 40. Slide 21: children under 5, adults over 50; male (slides 18, 21).
Risk factorsRadiation (atomic bomb, prior treatment); benzene, chemotherapy; EBV, HTLV-1 (Caribbean); Down syndrome, Fanconi anemia; male (slide 21).
GeneticsChildren: hyperdiploidy, favorable. Adults: BCR-ABL t(9;22) (Philadelphia). 1/3: no chromosome change, relatively favorable (slide 20).
SignsAnemia, bleeding (petechiae, purpura), infections, B symptoms, >50% lymphadenopathy/hepatomegaly/splenomegaly, bone or joint pain. White count often > 30K (30% neutropenic) (slide 22).
DiagnoseFlow cytometry (B or T) + bone marrow biopsy; imaging, labs, lumbar puncture. Lymphoid blasts ≥ 20% in marrow; diffuse marrow and blood involvement (aggressive lymphomas spare the marrow); CNS involvement = adverse (slides 23, 25).
DifferentialAML, CLL, aggressive lymphomas, hairy cell leukemia; lymphoblastic lymphoma (treated alike); atypical lymphocytosis of mononucleosis, pertussis (not clonal on flow) (slide 24).
TreatPh-: pediatric-like regimens under 40 (induction, consolidation, maintenance). Ph+: TKI + chemotherapy. Allogeneic transplant for adverse risk or relapse (slide 27).
OutcomeUnder 15: ~90% five-year survival, 2–5 years of chemotherapy; adults ~40%. Hematologist/oncologist, admission, life-long surveillance, trials (slides 28–29).

Acute Myeloid Leukemia (AML) & APL

TermWhat you need to know
WhatMyeloid progenitor/stem cell leukemia; Auer rods pathognomonic; mostly over 60; unusual in children outside neonates (slide 31).
Risk groupsFavorable, intermediate, poor. Secondary AML (from MDS, MPN, or after other cancer therapy) = poor outcomes (slides 32, 38).
Risk factorsPrior high-dose radiation; benzene, petroleum, alkylators; HIV; Down syndrome, Fanconi anemia; over 60; male (slide 34).
SignsAnemia, bleeding, infections, B symptoms; M4/M5: gum hyperplasia, extramedullary disease. White count usually high; > 200K rare; > 100K = emergent leukapheresis (slides 33, 35).
DiagnoseFlow cytometry (vs ALL) + bone marrow biopsy; Auer rods confirm myeloid; mutation sequencing. Myeloid blasts ≥ 20% (10% with certain mutations) (slides 36, 38).
DifferentialALL, CML, MDS, myeloproliferative disorders; pancytopenia of infection, aplastic anemia, nutritional deficiency, drugs (slide 37).
TreatFit: 7+3 (cytarabine + anthracycline). Unfit: hypomethylating agents +/- venetoclax (BCL-2 inhibitor). Allogeneic transplant for intermediate/high risk once in remission (slide 40).
OutcomeRelapse common; 48% at 1 year, 25–30% at 5; favorable risk ~2/3; over 70: 5%; adverse or secondary ~10% (slide 41).
APL (M3)Arrest at the promyelocyte; t(15;17) PML-RARA; DIC and bleeding, high early mortality; arsenic trioxide + ATRA; >90% cure if the presentation is survived (slide 43).

Myelodysplastic Syndrome (MDS)

TermWhat you need to know
WhatMyeloid malignancies defined by dysplasia (abnormal-appearing myeloid cells); from clonal hematopoiesis; hypercellular marrow, cells not functional (slides 45–46).
WhoOlder (median 70), men; often idiopathic; sometimes prior chemotherapy or radiation (slide 46).
PictureCytopenias, most often anemia; many asymptomatic, found incidentally; abnormal neutrophils; multinucleated red cells; higher risk: blasts < 20% (otherwise AML) (slide 47).
TreatLow risk + anemia: ESA (erythropoiesis-stimulating agents) + transfusions; neutropenia: myeloid growth factors (careful); higher risk: chemotherapy or hypomethylating agents; only cure = allogeneic transplant (slide 48).
OutcomeLower risk: median survival > 5 years. Very high risk: median overall survival 9 months, > 80% AML within 5 years. Low risk goal: quality of life (slide 49).

Chronic Myelogenous Leukemia (CML)

TermWhat you need to know
WhatStem cell leukemia making excess granulocytes (more mature than blasts); historically to acute leukemia in ~4–6 years; BCR-ABL / Philadelphia chromosome hallmark; now largely chronic (slide 56).
Mechanismt(9;22): BCR attaches to ABL1; the fusion enzyme drives proliferation, blocks apoptosis: the cell “can’t turn off” (slide 57).
Who15–20% of leukemias; median age 45–55; slightly more men; radiation bomb victims; no other risk factors (slide 58).
Signs~50% asymptomatic (leukocytosis on a blood count); fatigue; splenomegaly (abdominal swelling); B symptoms. White count often > 100K (to 500K), platelets high, anemia, very low blasts (slide 59).
DiagnoseBone marrow biopsy + PCR for BCR-ABL1; PCR in a patient with marked granulocytosis confirms and later monitors; marrow: granulocytic proliferation, myelocytes (slide 60).
DifferentialMyelofibrosis (CML: normal red cell shape, normal hematocrit; BCR/ABL1+). Reactive leukocytosis: no splenomegaly, white count usually < 50K, BCR/ABL1 negative (slide 61).
TreatTyrosine kinase inhibitors (6 agents) for chronic phase; stop after MMR for 2 years (50% recur, most recapture); allogeneic transplant for young healthy patients with TKI resistance or advanced disease (slides 63, 65).
OutcomeMost live normal lives on TKIs; accelerated or blast phase median survival < 12 months; hematologist; treatment for life unless sustained remission x 2 years; trials for advanced phase (slides 65–66).

Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia

TermWhat you need to know
WhatProliferative leukemia of mature B-cell lymphocytes in blood, marrow, nodes, spleen (same disease as small lymphocytic lymphoma); 1% of United States cancers. Mature T-cell form: T-cell prolymphocytic leukemia.
Genes80%: del 13q, del 11q, trisomy 12, del 17p.
WhoMedian age 70; 2× males; Eastern European and Jewish descent; no known occupational or environmental risk factors.
PictureOften asymptomatic, routine labs; painless firm mobile nodes (50–90%); B symptoms 5–10%; fatigue, bruising, infections; splenomegaly ~40%.

Burkitt Lymphoma

TermWhat you need to know

TermWhat you need to know
WhatB-cell lymphoma: few Reed-Sternberg cells (binucleated, neoplastic B cells) among many immune cells; contiguous, orderly spread; 10–15% of lymphomas.
WhoBimodal: 20s and >60; same-sex sibling 10×; Epstein-Barr virus 40–50%; HIV, HHV-6, tuberculosis.

TermWhat you need to know
WhatPlasma cell (terminally differentiated B cell) clone making one antibody, most commonly IgG; 10% of hematologic malignancies; spectrum MGUS → smoldering → myeloma.
MechanismOsteoclast activation → calcium release; kidney injury from light chain and calcium phosphate deposition; anemia from kidney and marrow.
WhoMost over 65; African Americans higher risk; family history; prior MGUS or smoldering myeloma; radiation, chemical, occupational.
PictureCRAB; infections; skull “punched out” lytic lesions.
