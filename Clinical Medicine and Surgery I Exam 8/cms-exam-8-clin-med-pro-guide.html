Iron deficiency anemia★ Professor emphasized1 not covered

★ Said: "You guys want to know this slide" (slide 15): microcytosis, hypochromia, low ferritin and saturation, high total iron-binding capacity; replace the iron and still correct the underlying cause. Slide emphasis: chronic blood loss is the most common adult cause; asterisked nail, lip and Plummer-Vinson findings; ferritin is an acute phase reactant; ferrous sulfate and its constipation.

Name of Condition
Iron deficiency anemia Slide 9
Definition
A microcytic, hypochromic anemia from too little iron; anemia is a reduction in healthy red blood cells or hemoglobin that lowers oxygen delivery to the tissues Slides 6, 15
Etiology (cause)
★ Chronic blood loss, the most common cause of iron deficiency anemia in adults (gastrointestinal loss and menses); dietary factors (vegan diet, tea drinkers, calcium antacids, medications, particularly proton pump inhibitors); malabsorption (gastric bypass surgery, celiac sprue, food allergies); increased demand (pregnancy, growth in infancy or adolescence) Slide 10
Epidemiology (who)
50% of anemia worldwide; almost 1 million deaths a year, 71% in Africa and Asia, only 1.4% in North America; higher in developing countries. United States: men under 50, 1%; women of childbearing age, 10% (menstrual losses); 9% of children 12 to 36 months are iron deficient and one-third of them develop anemia Slide 11
Risk Factors
Menstruation, gastrointestinal bleeding, vegan diet, tea, calcium antacids, proton pump inhibitors, gastric bypass, celiac sprue, pregnancy, infancy and adolescent growth; low-income families are particularly at risk in the United States Slides 10, 11
Pathology
Not covered in the lecture
Clinical Manifestation
Fatigue, pallor, decreased exercise tolerance, shortness of breath especially with exertion, smooth tongue, pica (common), ★ nail bed changes such as koilonychia (spoon-shaped nails), cheilosis, Plummer-Vinson syndrome Slide 12
Diagnosis
Complete blood count: microcytosis (mean corpuscular volume below 80 fL), hypochromia (low mean corpuscular hemoglobin). Iron panel: reduced ferritin, reduced transferrin saturation, increased total iron-binding capacity. Smear: microcytic, hypochromic cells. ★ Ferritin is an acute phase reactant: a normal or high ferritin does not exclude iron deficiency; consider an erythrocyte sedimentation rate and/or C-reactive protein. Also urinalysis, fecal occult blood test (stool guaiac), consider prothrombin and partial thromboplastin times. Differential: anemia of chronic disease, thalassemia, lead poisoning, zinc deficiency, sideroblastic anemia Slides 13–16
Treatment/Therapy
Treat the underlying cause: oral contraceptive (consider continuous-cycle) for menstrual loss; gastrointestinal bleed: gastroenterology consult for colonoscopy or endoscopy to evaluate for colorectal cancer; stop anticoagulants if anemia is severe (consult cardiology). Iron replacement: ★ ferrous sulfate 325 mg (66 mg elemental iron) once daily or every other day, with at least an as-needed laxative or stool softener for ★ constipation; food lowers side effects and absorption; alternate-day dosing is probably best; or ferrous sulfate 44 mg/5 mL (as elemental iron) once daily in the morning on an empty stomach with a full glass of orange juice. Response: hematocrit halfway to normal in 3 weeks, baseline by 2 months; continue 3 to 6 months after normal values to refill stores. Parenteral iron if intolerant or refractory (most need 1 to 1.5 g). Hematology referral if the diagnosis is not confirmed or there is no response to oral iron Slides 17–21
Mortality ★
Almost 1 million deaths a year worldwide. Prognosis: usually resolves once the cause and iron stores are corrected Slides 11, 106
Anemia of chronic disease★ Professor emphasized

★ Said: "Want to pay attention to this slide?" (slide 23): interleukin 6 raises hepcidin. Slide emphasis: treat the underlying condition is arrowed as most important; ferritin is an acute phase reactant.

Name of Condition
Anemia of chronic disease (anemia of chronic inflammation) Slides 22–23
Definition
Anemia caused by any inflammatory condition; initially resembles iron deficiency anemia Slide 23
Etiology (cause)
Any inflammatory condition: infection, autoimmune disease, neoplasm Slide 23
Epidemiology (who)
The second most common anemia; any age; about 1 million people older than 65 in the United States Slide 24
Risk Factors
Older age (more chronic inflammatory disease) Slide 24
Pathology
Multifactorial: inflammatory cytokines, in particular interleukin-6, upregulate hepcidin, which blocks iron absorption; systemic inflammation inhibits renal erythropoietin production; likely direct inflammatory marrow suppression Slide 23
Clinical Manifestation
Similar to iron deficiency anemia; resembles the causative condition Slide 24
Diagnosis
Same labs as iron deficiency; erythrocyte sedimentation rate and/or C-reactive protein if inflammation is not apparent. Ferritin normal or increased (a ferritin below 30 ng/mL, 67 pmol/L, means coexistent iron deficiency); total iron-binding capacity decreased (slide 27; normal or low on slide 13); reticulocytes usually decreased; mean corpuscular volume normal or low. Serum iron: low on slide 13, up or down on slide 27 Slides 13, 25, 27
Treatment/Therapy
★ Treat the underlying condition (most important); may benefit from erythropoietin (epoetin alfa, Procrit); FDA-approved for hemoglobin below 10 g/dL and anemia due to rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, hepatitis C, zidovudine therapy in HIV, myelosuppressive chemotherapy of solid malignancy, or chronic kidney disease; may need parenteral iron; hematology referral not usually needed Slide 26
Mortality ★
Prognosis: can persist while the underlying disease is active Slide 106
Sideroblastic anemia
Name of Condition
Sideroblastic anemia Slides 28–29
Definition
Anemia defined by ring sideroblasts in the bone marrow: iron-containing mitochondria forming a basophilic ring around the nucleus Slide 32
Etiology (cause)
Hereditary (usually X-linked); acquired (usually myelodysplastic syndrome); acquired and reversible (alcohol, drugs such as isoniazid and chlorambucil, copper deficiency, hypothermia, lead poisoning). Modern classification: dyserythropoiesis (hypohepcidinemia) or transfusion-dependence (myelodysplastic syndrome, thalassemia) Slides 29–30
Epidemiology (who)
Rare; about two-thirds of cases present in males in early childhood or adolescence, one-third in young to middle-aged females; can occur at any age Slide 30
Risk Factors
Alcohol, isoniazid, chlorambucil, copper deficiency, hypothermia, lead exposure, myelodysplastic syndrome Slide 29
Pathology
Excess total body iron; iron-laden mitochondria ring the nucleus Slide 32
Clinical Manifestation
No specific features other than those of anemia; usually moderate, hematocrit 20 to 30% Slide 31
Diagnosis
Variable microcytosis and hypochromia; dimorphic smear (microcytic, normal and macrocytic cells); increased serum iron, transferrin saturation and ferritin; increased zinc if caused by copper deficiency; bone marrow aspirate with ring sideroblasts (the defining feature) Slide 32
Treatment/Therapy
Remove the offending agent if acquired; red cell transfusion if severe; poor response to recombinant erythropoietin; occasional response to oral pyridoxine (50 to 200 mg/day); luspatercept for myelodysplastic syndrome with ring sideroblasts; hematology referral for diagnostic or transfusion support Slide 33
Mortality ★
Prognosis: variable; clonal forms may require continued follow-up Slide 106

Megaloblastic anemia: folate deficiency1 not covered
Name of Condition
Megaloblastic anemia (folate deficiency) Slides 37–39, 42
Definition
A macrocytic anemia (mean corpuscular volume above 100 fL) with megaloblasts (early red cell precursors), macro-ovalocytes and hypersegmented neutrophils; caused by vitamin B12 or folate deficiency Slides 38–39
Etiology (cause)
Folate: dietary (impoverished, indigent, alcohol use disorder, elderly); increased demand (pregnancy); impaired absorption (proximal small bowel); drugs (methotrexate, 5-fluorouracil, hydroxyurea, phenytoin, metformin) Slide 42
Epidemiology (who)
Not covered in the lecture
Risk Factors
Poverty, alcohol use disorder, older age, pregnancy, proximal small bowel disease, the drugs listed Slide 42
Pathology
Impaired DNA synthesis causes nuclear-cytoplasmic asynchrony and impaired hematopoiesis; granulocytes and megakaryocytes affected to a lesser degree Slide 39
Clinical Manifestation
Findings of all anemias: fatigue, pallor, headache; dyspnea and heart failure if severe Slide 45
Diagnosis
Complete blood count: red cells, hemoglobin, hematocrit down; mean corpuscular volume up; mean corpuscular hemoglobin and its concentration normal to slightly up; red cell distribution width up; reticulocytes vary. B12 and folate levels first (not very sensitive), smear; if borderline: homocysteine (needs both B12 and folate) and methylmalonic acid (elevated in B12 deficiency but NOT folate deficiency). Non-megaloblastic macrocytosis (alcohol or drug, hypothyroidism, liver failure, marrow dysplasia) usually stays below 115 fL Slides 38, 40–41, 47
Treatment/Therapy
Before replacing folate, evaluate for B12 deficiency (otherwise labs improve but permanent neurologic symptoms may worsen); folic acid 1 mg orally or parenterally daily for 1 to 2 weeks Slide 49
Mortality ★
Prognosis: counts improve with treatment Slide 106
Vitamin B12 deficiency and pernicious anemia★ Professor emphasized2 not covered

★ Said: "This slide is definitely one that you guys want to pay attention to" (slide 47): methylmalonic acid is for B12 only, not folate. Slide emphasis: pernicious anemia is the most frequent cause; burning of the hands and feet; homocysteine and methylmalonic acid are bold.

Name of Condition
Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) deficiency; pernicious anemia Slides 43–44
Definition
Megaloblastic, macrocytic anemia from too little B12; pernicious anemia is the autoimmune form against parietal cells and/or intrinsic factor Slides 43–44
Etiology (cause)
★ Pernicious anemia, the most frequent cause of B12 deficiency Slide 44
Epidemiology (who)
Not covered in the lecture
Risk Factors
Not covered in the lecture
Pathology
Absorption: peptic digestion frees B12; intrinsic factor from parietal cells binds it and protects it; the complex binds cubilin in the distal ileum; B12 binds transcobalamin and is stored in the liver (about 3 to 4 years or more). Pernicious anemia: autoimmune attack causes gastric mucosal atrophy and increased gastric cancer risk. B12 deficiency demyelinates the dorsal and lateral spinal cord tracts Slides 43–45
Clinical Manifestation
Anemia findings (fatigue, pallor, headache; dyspnea and heart failure if severe); glossitis, oral ulcers. Distinguishing: neurologic findings: neuropsychiatric changes (depression, irritability, insomnia, cognitive slowing, dementia); symmetric numbness, tingling, ★ burning to hands and feet (stocking-and-glove distribution); then unsteady gait and loss of position sense; may or may not improve with B12 Slides 45–46
Diagnosis
B12 and folate levels first (not very sensitive) and smear; if borderline: ★ homocysteine (needs both) and ★ methylmalonic acid (elevated in B12 but not folate deficiency). Pernicious anemia: anti-intrinsic factor antibodies (specific, not sensitive); anti-parietal cell antibodies (neither sensitive nor specific); gastric biopsy may show chronic atrophic gastritis; in reality a megaloblastic picture that corrects with B12 is the usual pathway (parenteral B12 trial) Slides 47–48, 50
Treatment/Therapy
B12 1000 mcg subcutaneously or intramuscularly weekly for 3 weeks, or oral cobalamin 1000 to 2000 mcg daily; evaluate for B12 deficiency before replacing folate Slide 49
Mortality ★
Prognosis: counts improve; delayed B12 treatment may leave neurologic deficits; increased risk of gastric cancer in pernicious anemia Slides 44, 106
Anemia of chronic kidney disease1 not covered
Name of Condition
Anemia of chronic kidney disease (anemia due to renal failure) Slides 51–52
Definition
A normocytic anemia of chronic kidney disease, primarily from decreased erythropoietin Slide 52
Etiology (cause)
Primarily, but not completely, decreased erythropoietin; inflammation and hepcidin restrict available iron Slides 52–53
Epidemiology (who)
Extremely common in chronic kidney disease; incidence rises as glomerular filtration rate declines; almost universal in dialysis patients Slide 52
Risk Factors
Declining glomerular filtration rate; dialysis Slide 52
Pathology
Inappropriately low erythropoietin response; iron-restricted erythropoiesis Slide 53
Clinical Manifestation
Not covered in the lecture
Diagnosis
Complete blood count: red cells, hemoglobin, hematocrit down; mean corpuscular volume, mean corpuscular hemoglobin and its concentration normal; red cell distribution width up; reticulocytes down. Order complete blood count, reticulocyte count, ferritin and transferrin saturation; assess other causes. A low transferrin saturation may mean iron-restricted erythropoiesis despite a normal or high ferritin Slides 52–53
Treatment/Therapy
Correct iron deficiency; consider oral or intravenous iron and an erythropoiesis-stimulating agent based on chronic kidney disease stage, hemoglobin and risk; ferric pyrophosphate citrate (Triferic) in the dialysate replaces the 5 to 7 mg of iron lost each hemodialysis. Follow-up: monitor hemoglobin, iron and blood pressure; nephrology for advanced disease, erythropoiesis-stimulating agent decisions or uncertainty Slides 21, 53, 106
Mortality ★
Prognosis: often chronic; may improve with iron or an erythropoiesis-stimulating agent Slide 106
Aplastic anemia
Name of Condition
Aplastic anemia Slides 54–55
Definition
Pancytopenia (loss of red cells, platelets and white cells) with loss of hematopoietic stem cells, the defining feature Slide 55
Etiology (cause)
Autoimmune; direct stem cell injury (drugs, chemicals, radiation); viral infections; genetic causes. Drugs and agents: cytotoxic or chemotherapeutic drugs (not aplastic anemia if an expected effect), chloramphenicol, ionizing radiation, toxins (benzene, pesticides). Clonal: paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, myelodysplastic syndrome, acute myeloid leukemia. Inherited: Fanconi anemia (diagnosed in childhood) Slides 56–57
Epidemiology (who)
Half of cases in the first three decades, with a small peak in childhood and a second between ages 20 and 25; male-to-female ratio about 1:1; 0.6 to 6.1 cases per million Slide 55
Risk Factors
Chloramphenicol, cytotoxic drugs, ionizing radiation, benzene, pesticides, viral infection, inherited conditions (Fanconi anemia) Slides 56–57
Pathology
Drugs, chemicals, viruses or mutations can alter the hematopoietic stem cells and predispose them to autoimmune destruction Slide 56
Clinical Manifestation
Manifestations of pancytopenia: recurrent infections (neutropenia); bleeding, petechiae, purpura (thrombocytopenia); fatigue, pallor Slide 58
Diagnosis
Bone marrow biopsy is required for diagnosis and to exclude other causes Slide 58
Treatment/Therapy
Urgent or emergent hematology/oncology consultation; bone marrow transplant; infection and bleeding precautions Slides 58, 106
Mortality ★
Five-year survival above 75% after bone marrow transplant from a suitable donor; most untreated patients die within one year from complications. Prognosis depends on severity and response to treatment Slides 55, 106
Leukopenia and neutropenia2 not covered
Name of Condition
Leukopenia and neutropenia Slide 59
Definition
Leukopenia is a low total white blood cell count; neutropenia is defined by the absolute neutrophil count Slide 59
Etiology (cause)
Viral illness, medications or chemotherapy, autoimmune disease, nutrient deficits, marrow pathology Slide 59
Epidemiology (who)
Not covered in the lecture
Risk Factors
Infection risk rises with severity, duration and impaired immune status Slide 60
Pathology
Not covered in the lecture
Clinical Manifestation
Profound neutropenia may have subtle findings; fever with an absolute neutrophil count below 500 is an emergency Slide 60
Diagnosis
Absolute neutrophil count = white count x (neutrophils + bands)% / 100. Mild 1,000 to 1,500; moderate 500 to 999; severe below 500 cells/microliter. Repeat complete blood count with differential, review the trend and smear, check other cell lines. Escalate to hematology for persistent or unexplained severe neutropenia, other cytopenias or an abnormal smear Slide 59
Treatment/Therapy
Fever with an absolute neutrophil count below 500 (or expected to fall below 500): prompt assessment, vitals, targeted examination, cultures, empiric antipseudomonal antibiotics without delay. Chronic: stop an offending drug, correct deficiencies, granulocyte colony-stimulating factor for selected patients. Education: report fever or rigors promptly; monitor counts Slide 60
Mortality ★
Prognosis: transient viral leukopenia often resolves after recovery; persistent or severe neutropenia carries greater infection risk and may indicate marrow disease Slide 60
Autoimmune hemolytic anemia★ Professor emphasized1 not covered

★ Slide emphasis: autoimmune hemolytic anemia carries an increased risk of venous thromboembolism (bold); hemolysis labs bilirubin, haptoglobin, lactate dehydrogenase, schistocytes and jaundice are bold.

Name of Condition
Autoimmune hemolytic anemia Slide 71
Definition
Hemolytic anemia (shortened red cell survival from premature destruction) caused by antibody against the red cell membrane Slides 61, 71
Etiology (cause)
Primary (de novo antibody production; almost always IgG) or secondary: viruses (human immunodeficiency virus, hepatitis, Epstein-Barr virus, SARS-CoV-2); autoimmune disorders (lupus, rheumatoid arthritis, scleroderma, ulcerative colitis); lymphoproliferative disorders (leukemia, lymphoma, multiple myeloma); pregnancy. Transfusion reaction is also immune-mediated Slides 65, 71
Epidemiology (who)
Not covered in the lecture
Risk Factors
The secondary causes listed Slide 71
Pathology
Extracorpuscular, immune-mediated hemolysis; hemolytic anemias share a shorter red cell life span, compensatory erythropoiesis (marrow hyperplasia, reticulocytosis) and retention of breakdown products Slides 63, 65
Clinical Manifestation
Anemia symptoms (fatigue, shortness of breath, chest pain, pallor; severe: high-output heart failure, shock); differentiating: ★ jaundice, splenomegaly, joint pain, hematuria; ★ increased risk of venous thromboembolism Slides 70, 73
Diagnosis
Hemolysis labs: complete blood count, reticulocyte count, ★ bilirubin (unconjugated greater than conjugated; jaundice above 2.0 mg/dL), ★ lactate dehydrogenase up, ★ haptoglobin decreased or absent (intravascular), urinalysis, smear (★ schistocytes). Direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test: positive when the washed red cells carry IgG or complement. Consider D-dimer and bilateral leg ultrasound for deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism Slides 66–69, 72–73
Treatment/Therapy
Consider immediate stabilization if hemoglobin below 6, a rapid decline, or instability; contact the blood bank. Rule out secondary causes. Glucocorticoids initially (e.g., prednisone 60 to 100 mg daily); rituximab (anti-CD20); other: immunosuppressants, splenectomy. Venous thromboembolism prophylaxis for inpatients, consider for outpatients Slides 73–74
Mortality ★
Prognosis: relapse may occur; outcome depends on cause and response Slide 107
G6PD deficiency★ Professor emphasized

★ Said: "Be familiar with Heinz bodies, what they are, what they represent" (slides 77-78); hemolysis is intravascular and extravascular. Slide emphasis: fava beans and acute hemolytic anemia are bold. Antimalarials: slide 79 lists chloroquine as a trigger, the slide 83 table as safe, and he said "don't just assume all anti-malarial medications carry that same risk", so no antimalarial is keyed.

Name of Condition
Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency Slide 76
Definition
The most common genetic disorder of erythrocytes: an enzyme deficiency that leaves red cells open to oxidative damage, causing ★ acute hemolytic anemia on oxidative stress Slides 76, 79
Etiology (cause)
X-linked recessive. Common triggers: ★ fava beans, naphthalene (mothballs), antimalarials (some; Prof. Ward said chloroquine does not carry the same risk), nitrofurantoin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (Bactrim), methylene blue, phenazopyridine, quinolones Slides 76, 79
Epidemiology (who)
200 to 400 million people. Prevalence: Kurdish Jews 60 to 70%; Sardinians 4 to 35%; Nigerians 22%; Thai people 17%; American Black people 11 to 12%; Brazilian Black people 8%; Greeks 6%; South China about 6%; India 3%; Japanese and Koreans 0 to 1% Slides 76, 80
Risk Factors
Male sex (X-linked recessive; carrier females have two red cell populations and are generally unaffected); exposure to the oxidant triggers Slides 76, 79
Pathology
G6PD is needed in the glutathione pathway; without reduced glutathione, oxidative stress forms Heinz bodies inside the red cell; splenic macrophages remove them by taking a chunk of the cell (bite cells); intravascular and extravascular hemolysis Slides 77–78
Clinical Manifestation
No symptoms until oxidative stress; drug-induced hemolysis is acute, of variable severity, after a lag of 2 to 3 days: abdominal pain, jaundice, dark urine, splenomegaly Slide 81
Diagnosis
Labs consistent with hemolytic anemia; during an attack the smear may show Heinz bodies and bite cells; confirmed by a low G6PD level roughly 8 weeks after an attack Slide 82
Treatment/Therapy
Avoid the trigger ("don't eat that"); hydrate; transfuse if needed; splenectomy only for recurrent attacks. Education: avoid oxidative triggers; seek care for jaundice or dark urine Slides 82, 107
Mortality ★
Prognosis: usually favorable with trigger avoidance Slide 107
Thalassemia★ Professor emphasized

★ Said: "Did I tell you guys to pay attention to slide 88?" (thalassemia labs). Slide emphasis: the globin imbalance line is bold; iron overload is bold and underlined.

Name of Condition
Thalassemia (alpha and beta) Slides 84–85
Definition
A hemoglobinopathy from mutations causing deficient synthesis of alpha or beta globins Slide 85
Etiology (cause)
Inherited mutations of the globin genes (4 alpha, 2 beta); the phenotype depends on how many are mutated, from silent carrier (one alpha gene lost) to incompatible with life Slide 89
Epidemiology (who)
Alpha thalassemia: Southern China, Malaysia, Thailand, Mediterranean region; mild forms in African origin. Asian ancestry often has the alpha thalassemia 1 trait (both deletions on one side, higher risk of hydrops fetalis); African ancestry more often the alpha thalassemia 2 trait (one deletion on each side) Slide 86
Risk Factors
Family history (preconception testing and counseling available) Slide 88
Pathology
★ Imbalance in expression leads to accumulation and precipitation of globin subunits, causing red cell destruction in the spleen; most erythroblasts die in the marrow Slide 85
Clinical Manifestation
Variable by mutation: hemolytic anemia, ★ iron overload, splenomegaly, extramedullary hematopoiesis, jaundice, skeletal changes Slide 87
Diagnosis
Generally low hemoglobin, mean corpuscular volume, mean corpuscular hemoglobin and reticulocyte index (marrow failure); Coombs negative (slide 87); slide 13 adds more microcytosis for the hemoglobin level with a normal or high red cell count, and a reticulocytosis, against the low reticulocyte index of slide 87. Complete blood count, iron studies, reticulocyte count; hemoglobin electrophoresis; gene testing Slides 13, 87–88
Treatment/Therapy
Supportive: transfusion when absolutely necessary (can worsen iron overload); folic acid; genetic counseling. Hematology for severe forms; iron monitoring Slides 89, 107
Mortality ★
Prognosis: trait often mild; severe forms need lifelong monitoring; the most severe genotype is incompatible with life Slides 89, 107
Sickle cell anemia
Name of Condition
Sickle cell anemia (sickle cell disease) Slide 90
Definition
A hemoglobinopathy in which hemoglobin S (two alpha and two mutant beta globins) polymerizes under low oxygen tension and forms a sickle shape Slide 90
Etiology (cause)
Mutation of a beta globin gene Slide 90
Epidemiology (who)
Distribution follows the malaria belt; heterozygous carriers (HbAS) are 90% less likely to have severe malaria Slide 91
Risk Factors
Parents carrying the mutation (preconception screening); reactive lung disease increases the risk of acute chest syndrome Slides 92, 94
Pathology
Polymerized HbS sickles red cells, causing microvascular occlusion and infarction of any tissue with vascular beds; symptoms begin around 5 months of age as fetal hemoglobin gives way to HbS; fetal hemoglobin of 10 to 20% seems to lessen severity Slides 90, 92–93, 97
Clinical Manifestation
Anemia almost universal (hematocrit 15 to 30%); pain crisis (acute pain, tenderness, anxiety, tachycardia); splenic infarct, aseptic necrosis of the femoral or humeral heads, retinopathy, kidney disease; leg ulcers; pediatric stroke (300 times unaffected children; 25% have a stroke by 45); priapism; susceptibility to encapsulated bacteria. Infants: failure to thrive, pain, anemia, splenomegaly, chronic infections, swelling of the extremities. Acute chest syndrome: chest pain, fever, shortness of breath, hypoxemia, new infiltrates on chest X-ray Slides 92–95
Diagnosis
Four screening periods: preconception, prenatal (chorionic villus sampling), neonatal (all 50 states; missed cases usually present within two years), post-natal Slide 92
Treatment/Therapy
Hydroxyurea raises fetal hemoglobin and reduces vaso-occlusive events; vaccinations, indicated childhood penicillin prophylaxis, infection education; urgent assessment for fever, chest pain or hypoxemia, new neurologic deficit; transfusion or exchange for selected severe events (watch alloimmunization and iron loading); hematology follow-up, organ surveillance, consideration of curative therapies Slides 98, 107
Mortality ★
Acute chest syndrome is the leading cause of death, with more than 10% mortality in adults. Prognosis: lifelong disease; prevention reduces complications Slides 94, 107

Hemochromatosis (primary and secondary)
Name of Condition
Hemochromatosis (iron overload), primary and secondary Slides 99–100
Definition
Excess iron deposition throughout the body Slide 100
Etiology (cause)
Primary: autosomal recessive mutation of genes that control iron absorption. Secondary: excessive iron supplementation, chronic liver disease, anemia (parenteral iron overload; transfusion-related overload in children) Slides 100, 105
Epidemiology (who)
HFE hemochromatosis usually presents in adults Slide 105
Risk Factors
Family history (first-degree relatives); iron supplements and excessive alcohol Slide 104
Pathology
Iron deposition raises oxidative stress, causing fibrosis and organ dysfunction; liver, heart, joints, endocrine organs and skin most affected; pituitary iron causes hypogonadotropic hypogonadism Slides 100, 102
Clinical Manifestation
Asymptomatic early; fatigue; skin bronzing (face, neck, groin, extensor forearms and lower legs); joint pain, especially the second and third metacarpophalangeal joints and knees; cardiomyopathy, chest pain, dyspnea on exertion; decreased libido, impotence, amenorrhea, small testes, gynecomastia, reduced body hair Slides 100–102
Diagnosis
Iron panel: ferritin below 300 micrograms/L and transferrin saturation below 45% means no iron overload (ferritin is an acute phase reactant: read the whole picture). Liver biopsy with Prussian blue: primary shows hepatic iron sparing the Kupffer cells until later; secondary (parenteral overload) shows iron predominantly in Kupffer cells. Consider HFE testing with elevated transferrin saturation or ferritin; offer first-degree relative screening Slides 103–104
Treatment/Therapy
Primary: therapeutic phlebotomy with ferritin-guided follow-up. Secondary: treat the cause; consider chelation when phlebotomy is unsuitable. Avoid iron supplements and excessive alcohol; individualize dietary advice Slides 104, 107
Mortality ★
Early treatment can prevent organ damage; assess fibrosis and monitor cirrhosis for liver cancer; established cirrhosis may persist Slides 104, 107
