Asthma and Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease Drugs Compared by Class

Every respiratory drug class side by side: quick relief or long-term controller, how it works, what it causes and what to teach. Smoking cessation agents are at the end. No doses.

Lecture 11 — Drugs for Pulmonary Infections, Asthma and Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease 15 rows

Read the second column first. The quick-relief drugs are the short-acting beta-2 agonists, the anticholinergics and the systemic corticosteroids; everything else is a long-term controller taken daily whether or not the patient feels well. Two never-alone rules: a long-acting beta-2 agonist is never used alone in asthma (hence the combination inhalers), and the mast cell stabilizers must be taken before the exposure. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease by group (slide 102): group A a short-acting bronchodilator as needed; group B a long-acting muscarinic antagonist or long-acting beta-2 agonist; group C a long-acting muscarinic antagonist; group D a long-acting muscarinic antagonist, or both long-acting bronchodilators with severe breathlessness, or an inhaled glucocorticoid with a long-acting beta-2 agonist when asthma overlaps; every group avoids smoking and gets influenza and pneumococcal vaccines. Current guidelines differ: the 2023 report merged groups C and D into group E. Doses are not tested and are left out.
