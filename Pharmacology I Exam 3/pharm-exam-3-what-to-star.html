“They can cause hypercalcemia, but they can reduce calcium stones from forming. I’ll talk about that again in a second, because it’s a little oxymoronic; some students get a little confused by that.”

Lecture 9 — Diuretics and Heart Failure Drugs · 14:40

He flagged this as a point students trip over, so know the contrast: thiazides raise serum calcium slightly yet prevent calcium stones because less calcium stays in the urine (see 17:22 to 18:14).

Lecture 9 — Diuretics and Heart Failure Drugs · 19:45

A nephrologist’s line that he uses as his memory hook for the metolazone exception. The clinical use he described: adding metolazone to a loop diuretic infusion for a volume-overloaded patient whose kidneys are failing.

“I like to use the acronym PDQ for my class 1A antiarrhythmics. I think pretty darn quick… but those are going to be the three drugs that fit into this category here.”

Lecture 10 — Antiarrhythmic Drugs · 11:08

Acetylation means a lupus-like syndrome Lecture 10

“Anytime you see acetylation, know it’s probably going to be linked back to this lupus-like syndrome. That’s kind of a big, very notable sort of side effect associated with that.”

Lecture 10 — Antiarrhythmic Drugs · 18:54

Slide 13: procainamide’s lupus-like syndrome (25-50%, more often in slow acetylators). Slide 12: its active acetylated metabolite NAPA (N-acetylprocainamide) has class III activity, and fast acetylators may make more of it. He tied it back to hydralazine, the other acetylated drug.

The beta blocker is the bouncer Lecture 10

“You could have a patient who’s in AFib (atrial fibrillation) and the atria is just sitting there just quivering away, and it’s sending a whole bunch of signals to the ventricles… But the beta blocker is serving as sort of like a bouncer, right? So they only want to let so many people into the club at a steady rate.”

Lecture 10 — Antiarrhythmic Drugs · 29:19

Slide 25: class II slows conduction, decreases automaticity, prolongs atrioventricular conduction and decreases phase 4 depolarization. That is rate control: the ventricles get a steady rate while the atria may keep fibrillating. Class IV does the same through calcium (slide 37).

Sotalol: an -olol that is class II plus class III Lecture 10

“If you see sotalol, you’re like, wait a second, it’s an -olol. That should be a beta blocker… Sotalol is a kind of unique beta blocker, because it’s a non-selective beta blocker, but it also can block some potassium channel efflux. So you get both a class two and a class three effect here.”

Lecture 10 — Antiarrhythmic Drugs · 44:09

Slides 35 and 36: beta-blocking activity plus potassium channel block; decreases phase 4 depolarization and lengthens the action potential; for atrial flutter and fibrillation and ventricular tachyarrhythmia; torsades de pointes and bronchospasm.

Adenosine: turn it off and on again Lecture 10

“Imagine if your computer starts going on the fritz. What do you do? You turn it off and you turn it back on again… This is what adenosine does, but for the heart. It causes a rapid hyperpolarization of the cells. And if you’re checking a continuous EKG, you will actually see your patient flatline for just a couple seconds… a very short half-life, only about 10 seconds.”

Lecture 10 — Antiarrhythmic Drugs · 46:40

Slides 41 and 42: activates atrial potassium channels and blocks calcium channels, hyperpolarizes cells, may stop the heart for 2-3 seconds; half-life about 10 seconds; for paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia; flushing (20%), shortness of breath, burning in the chest. The fast push followed by a saline flush is his addition.

Torsades de pointes: always magnesium sulfate Lecture 10

“Magnesium sulfate, this one is going to be used specifically for treatment of torsades… This is a great pimping question from preceptors: it’s a treatment of choice for torsades. It’s always magnesium sulfate.”

Lecture 10 — Antiarrhythmic Drugs · 52:00

Slide 45: intravenous magnesium sulfate for torsades de pointes; bradycardia, flushing, headache and respiratory paralysis. This site leaves the gram amount out.

Standing rules: star these wherever they appear 11

STANDING RULE: know what every drug does to potassium Lecture 9

“One of the big things I told you with ACE inhibitors: you’ve got to know what these medications are doing to your potassium. You need to know whether they’re going to be increasing it or decreasing it, because you can kill somebody very easily with potassium.”

Lecture 9 — Diuretics and Heart Failure Drugs · 6:05

For every drug in this lecture, know whether it raises or lowers potassium. Loops, thiazides and carbonic anhydrase inhibitors lower it; potassium-sparing diuretics, aldosterone antagonists and ACE inhibitors raise it (see the potassium chart).

STANDING RULE: mortality benefit versus symptom relief Lecture 9

“If I say something is good for mortality purposes, you know that versus things that are just good for symptomatic care. If it’s good for mortality reasons, that means you want the patient on it no matter what. If it’s just good for symptom management, then they may not need to be on it all the time.”

Lecture 9 — Diuretics and Heart Failure Drugs · 40:55

The organizing rule of the heart failure half. Symptom-only: diuretics, digoxin, milrinone, dobutamine. Survival: ACE inhibitors, the three named beta blockers, aldosterone antagonists (advanced), sacubitril-valsartan, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors.

ACE inhibitors or ARBs are mandatory Lecture 9

“ACE inhibitors and/or ARBs absolutely require… they decrease preload and afterload, they decrease sympathetic activation, and biggest thing, they decrease left ventricular remodeling, dilation, hypertrophy… so ACEs or ARBs have to be mandatory for these patients.”

Lecture 9 — Diuretics and Heart Failure Drugs · 42:08

Survival-benefit drugs: the patient is on one irrespective of symptoms. Their problems: acute decrease in renal function, hypotension, high potassium, cough, angioedema (switch to an angiotensin receptor blocker for the cough or angioedema).

STANDING RULE: classes II and IV work only above the ventricles Lecture 10

“This list is not meant to fully memorize. What I want you to take are sort of the trends… if you look at class two and class four, these are good for treating supraventricular arrhythmias, or things that originate above the ventricles… They don’t really do anything for ventricular arrhythmias… Class one as a whole, and class three, based off how they work, they can treat both atrial arrhythmias and ventricular arrhythmias.”

Lecture 10 — Antiarrhythmic Drugs · 5:55

Slide 6 is read for its trend: class II, class IV and the miscellaneous agents are listed for supraventricular tachycardia; class I (lidocaine aside, ventricular only) and class III cover both. Slide 39 says to avoid verapamil and diltiazem in ventricular arrhythmias. Slides 26 and 27 still list ventricular beats for propranolol, so no question here claims beta blockers are supraventricular only.

STANDING RULE: rhythm control versus rate control Lecture 10

“Here’s where I get into the discussion of rate control versus rhythm control… the class ones that we’re looking at so far are going to be what we consider rhythm control. That means that they will maintain normal sinus rhythm in a patient as opposed to just preventing the symptoms… [of the beta blockers, at 28:37] this is what we call rate control.”

Lecture 10 — Antiarrhythmic Drugs · 12:06

His organizing frame (the slides do not use the words). Rhythm control puts and keeps the patient in normal sinus rhythm: class I, amiodarone, dofetilide. Rate control limits how many beats reach the ventricles: class II, class IV, digoxin. Slide words to match: quinidine “maintain rhythm” (slide 10), dofetilide “normal rhythm in atrial fibrillation” (slide 34), digoxin “slow ventricular rate” (slide 44).

STANDING RULE: the broad nosocomial regimen (“there will be a question like this”) Lecture 11

“If I put an answer choice and I say, hey, you’re suspecting a patient has ventilator associated pneumonia, which of these regimens is going to be sufficient for empiric treatment?… There will be a question like this on the test and you’re going to get a bunch of drug names and your job is to be able to identify what classes they come from and then is that an appropriate regimen?”

Lecture 11 — Drugs for Pulmonary Infections, Asthma and Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease · 22:56

Slide 23: ONE antipseudomonal beta-lactam (cephalosporin, carbapenem or penicillin/beta-lactamase inhibitor) PLUS an antipseudomonal fluoroquinolone or an aminoglycoside PLUS vancomycin or linezolid. He also said it at 18:41 and called it “at least one question” (24:24). His failing examples: two beta-lactams together (“They do the same thing”, 22:45) and two drugs for methicillin-resistant S. aureus with one gram-negative drug (23:59).

Airway or circulation in trouble: epinephrine now Lecture 11

“Epinephrine works for everything. Not every allergic reaction requires epinephrine though. But if we have suspicion, they could be developing anaphylaxis, any kind of cardiovascular compromise, any sort of airway compromise, absolutely use the epinephrine right away.”

Lecture 11 — Drugs for Pulmonary Infections, Asthma and Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease · 48:41

Slides 51 and 52: alpha effects (vasoconstriction, decreased vascular permeability) and beta effects (bronchial smooth muscle relaxation); usually intramuscular or subcutaneous, intravenous if the patient is coding.

STANDING RULE: quick relief or controller, for every asthma drug Lecture 11

“I want you to make clear the difference between drugs that are used for quick relief of symptoms and those which are used for control of symptoms or prevention of symptoms… Because those are easy test questions I can ask. I say which one of these is good for quick relief? Which one of these is good as a controller med?”

Lecture 11 — Drugs for Pulmonary Infections, Asthma and Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease · 54:21

Slide 67. Quick relief: short-acting beta-2 agonists, anticholinergics, systemic corticosteroids. Long-term controllers: long-acting beta-2 agonists, inhaled corticosteroids, leukotriene modifiers, mast cell stabilizers, anti-immunoglobulin E antibody, methylxanthines (theophylline).

STANDING RULE: never a long-acting beta-2 agonist alone in asthma Lecture 11

“One really critical point here is that for control of asthma long-term control you never ever ever ever ever ever use a long acting beta agonist by itself they’ve actually found the studies that that increased risk for death.”

Lecture 11 — Drugs for Pulmonary Infections, Asthma and Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease · 1:04:05

Slide 70: long-acting beta-2 agonists (formoterol, salmeterol): “Do NOT use alone for asthma.” They are paired with an inhaled corticosteroid (slide 74). He noted chronic obstructive pulmonary disease is different.

Systemic corticosteroids: taper after more than 5 days Lecture 11

“Remember, if they’re on it for more than five days, you have to taper. So if the patient got admitted and they’re on it for a week or more, then you’d have to taper down the dose. If it’s just a five-day course, no problem.”

Lecture 11 — Drugs for Pulmonary Infections, Asthma and Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease · 1:22:13

Slide 89: adrenal suppression, “General rule: taper if duration is > 5 days.” He affirmed the slide, so it is asked once; many references taper only after longer courses (guide note).

STANDING RULE: chronic obstructive pulmonary disease is not asthma Lecture 11

“I want you to compare and contrast that to the asthma management. Because this is a less of an inflammatory condition, we’re gonna see that our anti-inflammatories actually fit all the way down at the bottom of the list… So for them, they could be on a long-acting beta agonist, that’s fine. That’s different from asthma, asthma can’t do a SABA or a LABA by itself. Here you can.”

Lecture 11 — Drugs for Pulmonary Infections, Asthma and Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease · 1:39:46

Slide 102: short-acting bronchodilator as needed (A); a long-acting muscarinic antagonist OR long-acting beta-2 agonist alone (B); a long-acting muscarinic antagonist (C); the two together, and a glucocorticoid combination only with asthma features (D). No biologics (1:42:11). Compare slide 70: in asthma a long-acting beta-2 agonist is never used alone.

Specific facts flagged as testable 62

Wherever salt goes, water follows Lecture 9

“Diuretics are helping to block the reabsorption of sodium and chloride in the renal tubule, which will then cause water to flow with it. This is the general rule I’ll use: wherever salt goes, water wants to follow.”

Lecture 9 — Diuretics and Heart Failure Drugs · 0:30

The one-line mechanism for every diuretic: block sodium and chloride reabsorption somewhere along the nephron and water leaves with it. Where it is blocked sets the strength and the side effects.

The kidneys fight back (diuretic braking) Lecture 9

“They have this kind of diuretic braking action where you’ll see activation of the renin-angiotensin system, an increase in ADH release… the kidneys don’t like losing all that salt and volume, so they’re going to be fighting you, and that’s why you see synergy between ACE inhibitors and diuretics and calcium channel blockers.”

Lecture 9 — Diuretics and Heart Failure Drugs · 1:26

Volume loss triggers renin-angiotensin, aldosterone and antidiuretic hormone, which limit the diuretic. Drugs that blunt those systems pair well with diuretics. Slides 5, 15 and 26 list the same reflexes.

Where sodium is reabsorbed: ascending limb, distal tubule, collecting duct Lecture 9

“On the ascending portion this is where you can see a lot of sodium reabsorption occurring here, so this is going to be a really powerful place where we could block sodium reabsorption… the distal tubule is also an important place for sodium reabsorption here; this is where things like your thiazide diuretics… that’s where these are going to be working… and then you’ll get into the collecting duct, and this is where you’re going to see two major hormones working here, aldosterone and ADH.”

Lecture 9 — Diuretics and Heart Failure Drugs · 3:25

Match the site to the class: loops in the thick ascending limb, thiazides in the distal tubule, potassium-sparing drugs and aldosterone antagonists in the collecting duct. Note: at 3:19 the transcript has him calling the descending limb “only impermeable to water”; that is a slip of the tongue or a transcription error. The descending limb is where water leaves the lumen (slide 9), and the thick ascending limb is the water-impermeable segment.

Drugs go in descending order of potency; loops are the most potent Lecture 9

“We’re going to go in descending order of potency. We’re going to start with the most potent, the most bang for our buck in terms of urine formation… loop diuretics is going to cause the biggest increases in urine outflow.”

Lecture 9 — Diuretics and Heart Failure Drugs · 4:32

Potency order: loops, then thiazides, then the weak classes (potassium-sparing, aldosterone antagonists, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors). The more potent the drug, the more pronounced its side effects.

Loops cost potassium, calcium and magnesium Lecture 9

“You’re going to be seeing calcium and magnesium excretion too. These are important because these are side effects… if I’m increasing potassium excretion, well, I could see hypokalemia, I could see hypocalcemia or hypomagnesemia as a result.”

Lecture 9 — Diuretics and Heart Failure Drugs · 5:43

A loop diuretic wastes potassium, calcium and magnesium; each is a testable adverse effect, and low potassium and low magnesium also raise digoxin toxicity and arrhythmia risk.

Loops still work when kidney function is poor Lecture 9

“Notably, the loop diuretics still retain efficacy even when patients have really, really poor kidney function. Even though their GFR is less than like 30, the loops will still work… but just because the person’s making urine does not mean their kidneys are functioning all that well.”

Lecture 9 — Diuretics and Heart Failure Drugs · 8:48

Loops work below a creatinine clearance of 30 mL/min (slide 16); thiazides generally do not (metolazone excepted). Making urine is not proof of working kidneys.

Loop diuretic indications: anywhere fluid needs to come off Lecture 9

“Anytime we need to get fluid off of the patient, loops are great. We use it for pulmonary edema, nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis, hypercalcemia… heart failure, renal failure. You can use it in hypertension; it’s not going to be good for chronic hypertension, but if it’s related to having too much blood volume it could work.”

Lecture 9 — Diuretics and Heart Failure Drugs · 9:27

Slide 17 lists the loop indications: pulmonary edema, nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis (ascites), hypercalcemia (with saline), heart failure, renal failure, hypertension (volume-related, not chronic).

Loop adverse effects, including ototoxicity and seizures Lecture 9

“You can see these reflexive mechanisms due to volume depletion, hypokalemia, hyperglycemia, the alkalosis, the gout from the hyperuricemia… even ototoxicity with really high chronic dosing… hearing damage… you can lose too much sodium and cause hyponatremia that can lead to seizures… and an increase in your BUN.”

Lecture 9 — Diuretics and Heart Failure Drugs · 9:56

Slide 18's list: volume depletion, hypokalemia, hyperglycemia, contraction alkalosis, gout, ototoxicity, hyponatremia with seizures, allergic reaction, rise in blood urea nitrogen. Other diuretics share these but milder.

Other diuretics share the side effects, just less; the exception is potassium Lecture 9

“These are going to be very similar things to what you see with other diuretics, it’s just they’re going to be less pronounced because they’re not going to be as potent at getting rid of salt and water… loops are the biggest players here, they’re going to be the most drastic… one notable exception I will highlight: keep in mind what effects these drugs have on things like potassium, because some will, you’ll find later on, actually increase potassium, some will decrease it.”

Lecture 9 — Diuretics and Heart Failure Drugs · 10:40

Learn the shared adverse-effect set once (loops worst) and then the exceptions, above all potassium and calcium.

Loop interactions Lecture 9

“NSAIDs… block our blood pressure response; with an aminoglycoside, which also causes ototoxicity, that could be potentiating; lithium will not be cleared as well, which can lead to toxicities; and with digoxin, arrhythmia risk is going to be the big thing due to the electrolyte disturbances.”

Lecture 9 — Diuretics and Heart Failure Drugs · 11:35

Slide 19: NSAIDs blunt the response, aminoglycosides add ototoxicity, warfarin competes for protein binding, lithium clearance falls, digoxin plus low potassium and magnesium causes arrhythmias.

Thiazides: calcium is held back, so fewer calcium stones Lecture 9

“It will decrease renal calcium excretion, so it’s able to keep calcium from being excreted into the renal tubules, and that might be useful for patients with a history of kidney stones, nephrolithiasis due to calcium oxalate crystals.”

Lecture 9 — Diuretics and Heart Failure Drugs · 13:14

Thiazides are the opposite of loops on calcium: they reduce urinary calcium (stone prevention) and can raise serum calcium a little.

Who responds best to thiazides in hypertension Lecture 9

“Who does this work well in? If you have like elderly patients, obese, African American patients, who they all tend to be more sodium retentive… if you can get rid of that extra sodium you’re going to be able to help to decrease some of this kind of what we call water-logging.”

Lecture 9 — Diuretics and Heart Failure Drugs · 14:46

Best responders: elderly patients, African American patients and sodium-retentive states (slide 25). This site does not key “obese”, which is not an established marker of thiazide response.

Thiazides in chronic hypertension: long-lasting, cheap, add-on Lecture 9

“Thiazides have a more long-lasting action than a loop diuretic, so for chronic hypertension you’re going to see far more thiazides than loops. It doesn’t mean they’re first line anymore, but they can be a useful second or third line add-on… well tolerated, cheap, old medications.”

Lecture 9 — Diuretics and Heart Failure Drugs · 16:33

Chronic hypertension: thiazides, not loops. Low-dose thiazides are well tolerated (slide 25). Current hypertension guidelines still list thiazide-type diuretics among the first-line choices; his remark is about their place relative to newer agents.

The calcium paradox explained Lecture 9

“It’s going to be causing the patient to suck up more calcium out of the renal tubule so there’s less calcium there available to crystallize out. So yes, it could cause the patient’s serum calcium levels to go up a little bit, but it’s a pretty minor bump… it sounds a little counterintuitive but it actually does work.”

Lecture 9 — Diuretics and Heart Failure Drugs · 17:39

Low urinary calcium, slightly higher serum calcium: the reason thiazides help calcium stones and cause mild hypercalcemia.

Thiazides fail at low clearance, except metolazone Lecture 9

“You can find the thiazides actually become ineffective when your creatinine clearance goes down too low. Once you get down below like 30 or 40 most thiazides stop working, with the exception of metolazone, which will still retain the efficacy even at very low creatinine clearances.”

Lecture 9 — Diuretics and Heart Failure Drugs · 18:25

Slide 28 says the same. Key the metolazone exception; the blanket statement about other thiazides is not treated as absolute because newer evidence shows chlorthalidone can work at low clearance.

Hyperkalemia danger and the salt-substitute tip Lecture 9

“Hyperkalemia can be seen here, so that’s one major difference from your other diuretics. If you’re using these with ACE inhibitors or ARBs that could be a problem… or if the patient was on potassium supplements. A little tip: if someone is on a salt substitute, that’s usually potassium chloride… they’ve got to be cautious there.”

Lecture 9 — Diuretics and Heart Failure Drugs · 23:11
