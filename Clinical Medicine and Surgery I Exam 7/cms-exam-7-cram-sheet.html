REM vs Non-REM Sleep

TermWhat you need to know

TermWhat you need to know

TermWhat you need to know
DefinitionRight ventricular enlargement, hypertrophy or dilation caused exclusively by a primary lung or pulmonary vascular disorder; raised mean pulmonary artery pressure with a normal wedge. Left heart failure (Group 2) is not cor pulmonale.

The Pleura & Pleurisy

TermWhat you need to know
PressureIntrapleural pressure is always negative (about −4 mm Hg: 756 vs 760); the chest wall pulls out, the lung recoils in, and the suction keeps the lung inflated.
PleurisyInflammation of the parietal pleura; a symptom, not a diagnosis. Causes: idiopathic, trauma, infection, lupus or rheumatoid arthritis, pneumothorax, pulmonary embolism, malignancy, drugs (methotrexate, amiodarone, hydralazine).
PictureWell-localized sharp pain worse with breathing, coughing, movement, sneezing; central diaphragm → ipsilateral shoulder or neck pain.
ExamTachypnea, limited expansion; pleural friction rub (grating, both phases) that disappears when the breath is held (a pericardial rub does not).
Rule out firstMyocardial infarction, pericarditis, aortic dissection, pneumonia, pneumothorax, pulmonary embolism (“big scary things”); then rib or vertebral fracture, costochondritis, zoster.
TreatThe underlying condition + nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs.

Spontaneous Pneumothorax: Picture

TermWhat you need to know
CauseRupture of apical blebs (<1 cm, in the visceral pleura) or bullae (>1 cm).
HistoryOften at rest; acute dyspnea + ipsilateral pleuritic chest pain (severe, stabbing, worse with deep breaths); may be asymptomatic if small; cough less common.
ExamTachypnea, tachycardia, decreased excursion, decreased/absent fremitus, hyperresonance, diminished/absent breath and voice sounds on that side.
SecondarySame picture but worse (less reserve); infection adds cough, fever, chills.
DifferentialMyocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism, aortic dissection, pericarditis, pleurisy, muscle strain, costochondritis.

Pneumothorax Imaging

TermWhat you need to know
Upright chest filmInitial test: the visceral pleural line with no markings beyond it.
Supine filmDeep sulcus sign: a dark, deep costophrenic angle from air collecting laterally.
UltrasoundBedside, for the unstable patient: absent lung sliding; does not size it.

The Procedures Toolbox

TermWhat you need to know
GoalsEvacuate the space; close the leak; prevent recurrence.
Catheter aspirationLarge-gauge needle, catheter, 3-way stopcock, syringe; 2nd space midclavicular; over 2 liters out → consider a persistent air leak; film afterward.
Heimlich valveOne-way flutter valve on a chest tube; small, portable, lets the patient walk.
Chest tubeDrains air or fluid; pneumothorax or effusion; 4th or 5th space midaxillary.
PleurodesisInflames the pleura so the layers stick (no space for air or fluid): mechanical (surgical abrasion) or chemical (talc or drugs via chest tube or thoracoscope). Most common use: malignant effusion.
SurgeryThoracoscopic surgery has largely replaced open thoracotomy; thoracotomy stays for complicated cases.

After the Pneumothorax: Education

TermWhat you need to know
RecurrenceApproaches 50% within 3 years (slide 59); one-third of primary cases within 1–2 years, >60% after a second episode, >75% on the same side (slide 60).
LifestyleStop smoking (up to 100-fold risk); delay air travel 7–14 days after a film confirms resolution; avoid scuba diving; hold positive pressure during an active leak.
ReferSurgical evaluation for an air leak >4 days; pilots and divers early; genetics for a family history.

Traumatic & Tension Pneumothorax

TermWhat you need to know
TraumaticGunshot, stab, blunt trauma with rib fractures, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, mechanical ventilation: ask “Have you had any trauma?”
Closed vs openClosed: no wound, air from the lung. Open: chest-wall wound lets air in on inhalation (sucking chest wound).
TensionOne-way valve; hypotension, trachea away, neck veins up, breath sounds gone; clinical diagnosis.
ActChest tube now; if none, needle at the 2nd space midclavicular, then a chest tube. “This needle buys moments.” Never aspirate.

Hemothorax

TermWhat you need to know
CauseTrauma most common (heart, great vessels, lung, intercostal or internal thoracic arteries); neoplasms.
DefineFluid hematocrit >50% of the blood’s; strictly exudative. Hemopneumothorax: straight line, no meniscus.
TreatChest tube to evacuate and quantify; >200 mL/hr → thoracotomy.
RiskRetained blood → empyema, fibrothorax, trapped lung. An effusion that grows on anticoagulation needs a tap.

Pneumomediastinum

TermWhat you need to know
CauseAlveoli rupture next to the mediastinum (high pressure), esophageal perforation, or air tracking from neck or abdomen; after violent coughing, vomiting, inhaled drugs (Valsalva).
PicturePleuritic chest or neck pain, sore throat, dyspnea, cough.
ExamSubcutaneous emphysema in the neck; Hamman crunch: substernal crackling with each heartbeat (“snap, crackle, and pop”).
TreatAirway; treat the cause; conservative (oxygen, rest, analgesics); severe → emergent cardiothoracic surgery.

Pleural Effusion: Picture

TermWhat you need to know
MechanismsAltered permeability (inflammation, malignancy, pulmonary embolism); ↓ oncotic pressure (nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis); ↑ hydrostatic pressure (heart failure); blocked lymphatics (malignancy, trauma); peritoneal fluid across the diaphragm (cirrhosis, dialysis).
Who>1.5 million a year in the United States; heart failure the leading cause; malignant effusions can come years after a cancer diagnosis; asbestos exposure.
SymptomsNone if small; dyspnea, dry cough, pleuritic pain referred to the shoulder or upper abdomen.
CluesEdema, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea → heart failure; fever, purulent sputum → pneumonia; hemoptysis → malignancy; night sweats, weight loss → tuberculosis.

Effusion Workup

TermWhat you need to know
ImagingUpright film (75 mL posterior, 175 mL lateral angle); decubitus film (effusion side down) now replaced by ultrasound, which also guides the tap; contrast CT finds 10 mL and associated disease.
LoculationFluid trapped in compartments by adhesions between the pleurae (empyema).
ThoracentesisDiagnostic (50–100 mL) for a new effusion with no clinical cause; therapeutic relieves symptoms.
Send forProtein, lactate dehydrogenase, white count with differential, Gram stain, cytology (malignancy), culture; then Light’s criteria.

Exudates: Chylothorax & Empyema

TermWhat you need to know
ChylothoraxThoracic duct disruption (trauma, thoracic surgery most common; mediastinal tumors) → milky fluid, high triglycerides. Treat: chest tube or intermittent taps without over-draining (nutrition), octreotide, cut long-chain triglycerides.
EmpyemaPus in the pleural space; pneumonia with a parapneumonic effusion is the usual precursor; suspect it when pneumonia does not resolve. Treat: drainage + antibiotics promptly.

Transudates & Treating an Effusion

TermWhat you need to know
TransudatesCongestive heart failure >90%; cirrhosis with ascites, nephrotic syndrome, peritoneal dialysis.

★ Professor Emphasized — Acid-Base Disorders II

TermWhat you need to know
★ Compensation“Tattoo on the inside of your eyeballs”: compensation will never fully correct the pH in a simple disorder.
★ Normal pHA normal pH with abnormal values does not mean there is no mixed problem (three of the six slide examples have a normal pH).
★ One lungOnly one respiratory disorder at a time; several metabolic disorders can coexist.
★ Big gapA big anion gap is always metabolic; respiratory disorders do not cause large gaps (slide 26: >20 suggests a primary metabolic disorder).
★ Winter formulaExpected carbon dioxide = 1.5 × bicarbonate + 8, ± 2. Above = concurrent respiratory acidosis; below = concurrent respiratory alkalosis.
★ Gap vs bicarbonateChange in gap (from 10) vs fall in bicarbonate (from 25): equal = simple; gap bigger = + metabolic alkalosis (only with a high gap); bicarbonate bigger = check chloride; = gap + chloride = high-gap + normal-gap acidosis.
★ AlbuminAdd 2.5 for every 1 g/dL below 4.5; hypoalbuminemia lowers the gap, so the gap must be corrected (slides 8, 48).
★ Acute vs chronicRespiratory acidosis: bicarbonate up 1 (acute) vs 3.5 (chronic) per 10 mm Hg of carbon dioxide.
★ Treat the cause“Golden rule”: find and treat the underlying condition; always circle back to the patient (slide 27). Stop respiratory depressants (narcotics, benzodiazepines); careful oxygen in chronic retainers; do not overcorrect an alkalosis.
★ CalculateShe expects the calculations (gap, corrected gap, Winter, compensation), not the nomogram (slide 25); lab values come with reference ranges and the albumin.
Lighter touchRenal physiology detail (slides 20–21); plasma cell dyscrasias and bromide; licorice; the less common normal-gap causes.

Definitions, Buffers & Electrolytes

TermWhat you need to know
DefinitionsSimple (primary) disorder = one primary disturbance + its compensation; mixed = more than one primary disturbance at once (slide 4).
PotassiumAlkalosis: hydrogen ions out of cells, potassium in → hypokalemia. Acidosis: hydrogen ions in, potassium out → hyperkalemia (slide 5).
OxygenAlkalosis: hemoglobin holds oxygen tighter → less delivered to tissues. Acidosis: lower affinity → more delivered (slide 5).
AcidsVolatile (carbonic acid, from fats and carbohydrates) → lungs. Nonvolatile (proteins, ingested sulfates and phosphates) → kidneys (slide 13).
BuffersRenal/respiratory = most important overall; carbon dioxide adjusts quickly, bicarbonate slowly (hours to days). Intracellular buffers (proteins, phosphate, hemoglobin) do 60–70%; bone (slides 14, 19).
KidneyCannot excrete free hydrogen ions: buffered by ammonium and titratable acids (major one: hydrogen phosphate); urinary bicarbonate should be 0; urine pH can fall to 4.0 (slides 20–21).
LungsBrain stem senses cerebral pH; more carbon dioxide → faster breathing. Hyperventilation ↓ carbon dioxide; hypoventilation ↑ it (slides 18, 22).

Stepwise Method

TermWhat you need to know
1Arterial blood gas and electrolytes together; compare the two bicarbonates to verify accuracy (slide 56).
2pH: acidosis or alkalosis? Carbon dioxide and bicarbonate: respiratory or metabolic?
3Calculate the anion gap; correct for hypoalbuminemia.
4Estimate the compensation: appropriate? Excess or inadequate = mixed disorder (slides 26, 57).
5Which mixed disorders? pH, carbon dioxide, bicarbonate; change in gap, bicarbonate and chloride.
6Does it match the clinical picture? If there is a second disorder, find and treat both causes (slides 56–57, 63).

Respiratory Acidosis

TermWhat you need to know
What↑ carbon dioxide from decreased alveolar ventilation, NEVER increased production (slide 31).
Causes (slide 32)Central nervous system depression (sedatives, central event); neuromuscular and musculoskeletal disorders (myopathies, neuropathies, kyphoscoliosis, Guillain-Barré syndrome, myasthenia gravis, botulism, polymyositis); hemothorax, pneumothorax; flail chest; acute airway obstruction (upper airway, laryngospasm, bronchospasm); severe pneumonia or pulmonary edema; ventilator dysfunction; obesity-hypoventilation syndrome; pleural effusion; chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, acute respiratory distress syndrome. Acute or chronicBicarbonate up 1 per 10 mm Hg (acute) vs 3.5 after 24–48 hours (chronic). Slide 35: carbon dioxide 80 → compare the measured bicarbonate with each expected value.
TreatUnderlying condition; stop respiratory depressants (narcotics, benzodiazepines). Carbon dioxide >80 with oxygen <40: ventilate (airway, ventilator), clear secretions, oxygen (slide 36).
ChronicSame, with careful oxygen: a rapidly falling carbon dioxide can trigger alkalosis complications (arrhythmias, reduced cerebral perfusion, seizures) (slide 37).

Metabolic Alkalosis
