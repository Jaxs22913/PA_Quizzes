The Four EEG WaveformsClassification · Lecture 39 · slides 21–26

WaveFrequency (cycles/second)Where and when
BetaGreater than 14 (as high as 80)Parietal and frontal regions; asynchronous, low voltage; awake, alert, actively processing information
Alpha8–13, rhythmicalHealthy adults awake, quiet and resting; most intense occipital; disappear in deep sleep; replaced by beta when attention is directed to a task
Theta4–7Parietal and temporal regions in children; emotional stress in some adults (disappointment, frustration); degenerative brain disorders
DeltaLess than 3.5Voltage 2–4 times other waves; strictly cortical; very deep sleep, infancy, serious organic brain disease, cortex cut off from the thalamus
Memory aid

Fastest to slowest the waves spell BAT-D: Busy, At rest, Tired, Deep.

B Beta: busy, alert, actively processing (>14)A Alpha: awake but at rest, quiet (8–13)T Theta: tired (light sleep), children, stress, brain disorders (4–7)D Delta: deep sleep, infancy, organic brain disease (<3.5)

Where it breaks: “Tired” covers only theta’s sleep use; its children, stress and brain-disorder settings must be learned separately.

REM vs Non-REM Sleep

TermWhat you need to know
Non-REM (slow-wave)Deep, extremely restful; increased parasympathetic tone; blood pressure, heart rate, breathing and metabolic rate 10–30% lower; dreams less vivid and often not remembered (not consolidated). Stages N1, N2 (the majority), N3.
REMNot restful; vivid dreams with muscle activity; muscle tone very depressed (atonia except eyes and diaphragm); heart rate and breathing irregular; brain metabolism up to 20% higher; harder to arouse than non-REM, yet spontaneous morning waking happens in REM.
Paradoxical / desynchronizedREM’s EEG looks like wakefulness (paradoxical sleep); neurons fire without synchrony (desynchronized sleep).
Very sleepyREM episodes are short or absent; they lengthen as the person becomes rested.

Why We Sleep & the Sleep-Wake Cycle

TermWhat you need to know
Sleep restrictionOver a few days: worse cognitive and physical performance, productivity and health.
Two effect typesOn the nervous system and on other body systems; the exact functions remain a mystery.
FunctionsNeural maturation; learning and memory; targeted erasure of synapses; cognition; clearing metabolic waste of the awake brain; conserving metabolic energy.
Waking upSleep centers off → mesencephalic and upper pontile reticular activating nuclei released → cortex excited → positive feedback keeps wakefulness going.
Falling asleepHours later the activating neurons fatigue, the feedback loop fades and sleep-promoting centers take over. No definitive explanation exists.

Obstructive Sleep Apnea

TermWhat you need to know
Who and whyMost common sleep-disordered breathing; rises with age; men > women; post- > premenopausal; upper airway narrowing by soft tissue or structure.
Risk factorsObesity; big tongue, small or set-back jaw; neck 17 in (men) / 16 in (women); big tonsils; age; male; family history; alcohol or sedatives; hypothyroidism.
HistoryDaytime sleepiness, unrefreshing sleep, witnessed apnea, snorting or gasping, loud habitual snoring, morning headache, nocturia, poorly controlled hypertension, poor memory, low libido, night sweats.
ExamObesity, macroglossia, micrognathia, retrognathia, large neck, enlarged tonsils, crowded posterior oropharynx.
TestsThyroid-stimulating hormone if hypothyroid; split-night (2 hours, then CPAP titration) or all-night polysomnogram (mild to moderate at baseline); titration study if the diagnosis is known.
DifferentialCentral sleep apnea, nocturnal dyspnea (heart failure), swallowing disorder, panic attacks, nocturnal asthma, reflux, nocturnal seizures, narcolepsy, periodic limb movements.

Treating Obstructive Sleep Apnea

TermWhat you need to know
Most effectiveContinuous positive airway pressure (CPAP).
Other devicesAutotitrating PAP only without significant comorbidities; positional therapy if supine-only; oral appliance not for severe disease or no teeth; nasal expiratory resistance device.
DrugsModafinil or armodafinil: headache, anxiety, dizziness, nausea, hypersensitivity, many interactions; caution with cardiovascular, hepatic, psychiatric disease; lower doses in the elderly. Nasal steroids for allergic congestion.
SurgeryRadiofrequency ablation (mild to moderate, CPAP-intolerant); palatal implants (mild); maxillomandibular advancement; tracheostomy is a cure; bariatric surgery may help.
Lifestyle and referralDiet and exercise for weight loss; avoid alcohol and sedatives; refer to a sleep physician; counseling.
Older adultsLower mortality (they compensate) but drowsiness and nocturia, which improve with CPAP.

TermWhat you need to know

Central Sleep Apnea

TermWhat you need to know
MechanismBrainstem stops signaling: high carbon dioxide sensitivity → hyperventilation alternating with apnea; no effort (obstructive: effort against a collapsed airway).
Risk factorsHeart failure (Cheyne-Stokes; circulation delay to the carotid chemoreceptors), stroke, opioids, hypoxia, high altitude, CPAP use.
PresentationInsomnia, awakenings, nocturnal dyspnea, nonrestorative sleep, sleepiness; less snoring.
DiagnosisPolysomnography (home not sufficient): repetitive apneas without thoracic-abdominal excursion.
TreatmentTreat the cause (optimize heart failure therapy, reduce opioids, descend) → CPAP → adaptive servo-ventilation if CPAP fails, not in reduced ejection fraction.

Obesity Hypoventilation Syndrome

TermWhat you need to know
DefinitionAwake alveolar hypoventilation (raised awake carbon dioxide) in an obese person, not explained by anything else (Pickwickian syndrome).
Risk factorsCentral obesity, reduced lung function, weak inspiratory muscles, severe obstructive sleep apnea; male sex is NOT a risk factor; 50s and 60s.
Presentation90% have obstructive apnea symptoms (70% severe); 10% sleep hypoventilation (older women, fewer witnessed apneas); many present late with hypercapnic respiratory failure or right heart failure.
ComplicationsPulmonary hypertension, right-sided heart failure, sleepiness, hypertension, heart failure, insulin resistance; refer to a sleep specialist.
PrognosisHigh mortality; cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death; more hospital and intensive care use; worse with significant awake hypoxemia at diagnosis.

Interstitial Lung Disease: the Common Ground

TermWhat you need to know
What it is200+ disorders of scarring and/or inflammation of the parenchyma (alveoli, ducts, respiratory bronchioles); not malignant, not infectious; stiff, restrictive lungs → shortness of breath.
HowInflammatory cells in alveolar walls → alveolitis → fibroblasts → collagen = fibrosis (permanent).
Two groupsInflammation and fibrosis vs granulomatous; each known or unknown cause. Known: inorganic and organic dusts, fumes, gases. Unknown: sarcoidosis, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, connective tissue disease.
Drug causesMethotrexate, amiodarone, nitrofurantoin, bleomycin, chemotherapy, radiation (and the long slide 9 list).
SymptomsProgressive exertional dyspnea or persistent nonproductive cough; hemoptysis, wheeze, chest pain, tachypnea, anorexia; later dyspnea at rest.
ExamSmall lung volumes, bibasilar dry rales (inspiratory crackles), clubbing, right heart failure late; may be normal early.

Idiopathic Interstitial Pneumonia & Pulmonary Fibrosis

TermWhat you need to know
SubtypesIdiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (most common), desquamative, nonspecific, cryptogenic organizing, respiratory bronchiolitis-associated, lymphoid, acute interstitial.
Smoking67–75% of idiopathic and familial fibrosis patients have smoked; smoking is the cause in the majority; cessation is critical.
PictureInsidious dry cough, exertional dyspnea, fatigue; clubbing, bibasilar inspiratory crackles, reduced chest expansion.
TestsRestrictive pattern; CT diffuse patchy fibrosis with pleural-based honeycombing; lower-field markings; biopsy may be needed (lavage, transbronchial, surgical).
TreatmentControversial: nothing improves survival vs no treatment; steroids only for cryptogenic organizing, lymphoid and nonspecific subtypes.

Sarcoidosis

TermWhat you need to know
WhoMulti-organ, noncaseating granulomas, lungs and lymphatics most; North American Black and Northern European white people; women; third and fourth decades.
PictureCough, insidious dyspnea, chest discomfort, malaise, fever, weight loss, nontender lymphadenopathy; erythema nodosum (painful rashes, lower legs), parotid enlargement, lupus pernio (painful skin sores, face), hepatosplenomegaly.
TestsFilm: symmetric bilateral hilar and right paratracheal adenopathy; angiotensin-converting enzyme up in 40–80%, hypercalcemia (5%), hypercalciuria, eosinophilia, high sedimentation rate; biopsy confirms (transbronchial or fine-needle node).
TreatmentCorticosteroids (90% respond) for the listed indications; methotrexate, azathioprine, infliximab if intolerant or refractory.
Follow-upYearly: exam, pulmonary function, chest film, electrocardiogram, chemistry, eye exam.

Hypersensitivity Pneumonitis

TermWhat you need to know
WhatNon-atopic, non-asthmatic; inhaled organic dust: moldy hay (farmer’s lung), bird excreta and feathers (bird fancier’s), cheese maker’s lung, moldy sugarcane, infested grain.
PictureMalaise, chills, fever, cough, dyspnea, nausea hours after exposure; may mimic paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (worse after work at home); crackles, tachypnea, tachycardia, cyanosis.
TestsSerum antibodies to the agent = definitive; white count up with left shift; hypoxia; restrictive; small nodules sparing apices and bases; biopsy if subacute or chronic; noncaseating granulomas.
TreatmentIdentify and avoid the agent; steroids if severe; supportive; transplant. Reversible if caught early.

Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome

TermWhat you need to know
CausesDirect: aspiration, diffuse pneumonia, contusion, toxic gas, near-drowning, emboli. Systemic: sepsis (about a third of cases), pancreatitis, shock, trauma, overdoses, transfusions, amiodarone, nitrofurantoin.
PictureDyspnea 12–48 hours after the insult; hypoxemia refractory to oxygen; tachypnea, retractions, crackles; multiorgan failure.
ImagingBilateral infiltrates becoming confluent; air bronchograms (about 80%); normal heart size, few effusions; echocardiogram excludes cardiogenic edema (cardiomegaly, batwing opacities).
ManagementTreat the cause; low tidal volume; positive end-expiratory pressure for arterial oxygen above 60; early prone positioning (moderate to severe); conservative fluids; transfuse at 7 g/dL; steroids within 14 days (conditional); avoid oscillatory ventilation and routine pulmonary artery catheters; neuromuscular blockade controversial; ECMO (extracorporeal membrane oxygenation) salvage.
PrognosisMortality about 30% (20% trauma to 80% end-stage liver disease); lung function recovers over 6–12 months; residual diffusion deficits, weakness, fatigue.
