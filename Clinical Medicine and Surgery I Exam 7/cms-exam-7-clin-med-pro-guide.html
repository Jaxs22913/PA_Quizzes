Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA)★ Professor emphasized

★ Diagnosis “as simple as possible, five or more plus a symptom or 15 or more”; the sleep study is the gold standard and the home kit checks only obstructive apnea; “patient education is like the most important thing that we can do” (CPAP works but patients dislike it); severe disease needs CPAP, not an oral appliance; postmenopausal women carry more risk, “an important thing.”

Name of Condition
Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) Slides 4–5
Definition
Sleep-disordered breathing from upper airway narrowing: obstructive apneas (no airflow despite continued respiratory effort; airflow down 90% or more for at least 10 seconds) and hypopneas (slowed, shallow breathing in episodes of 10 seconds or longer) Slides 3, 5
Etiology (cause)
Narrowing of the upper airway by excessive soft tissue or structural abnormalities Slide 5
Epidemiology (who)
The most common form of sleep-disordered breathing; prevalence increases with age; sleep-disordered breathing is more common after menopause than before Slide 5
Risk Factors
Obesity, macroglossia, micrognathia, retrognathia, neck circumference 17 inches or more (men) or 16 (women), enlarged tonsils, increasing age, male sex, family history, alcohol or sedatives, hypothyroidism Slide 6
Pathology
Upper airway collapse during sleep while effort continues; untreated: increased sympathetic tone, bradycardia, blood pressure variability, arrhythmias, raised C-reactive protein, cortisol and proinflammatory cytokines Slides 3, 21–22, 24
Clinical Manifestation
Daytime sleepiness, unrefreshing sleep, witnessed apnea, snorting or gasping awakenings, loud habitual snoring, morning headache, nocturia, poorly controlled hypertension, poor concentration, low libido, night sweats; exam: obesity, large neck, enlarged tonsils, crowded posterior oropharynx Slide 7
Diagnosis
Split-night or all-night polysomnogram (gold standard); 5 or more predominantly obstructive events per hour plus a symptom, or 15 or more events, not explained by another condition; apnea-hypopnea and respiratory disturbance indices; thyroid-stimulating hormone if hypothyroidism is suspected; home kits assess only obstructive apnea and may miss mild disease, so use a polysomnogram with significant cardiopulmonary disease, neuromuscular weakness, awake hypoventilation or hypoxemia, stroke, long-term opioids, severe insomnia or a suspected non-obstructive sleep disorder; STOP-Bang (snoring, tired, observed, pressure, body mass index over 35, age over 50, neck 16 inches, gender male) preoperatively, not for community screening Slides 8–9, 16–17
Treatment/Therapy
Continuous positive airway pressure (most effective); autotitrating pressure without significant comorbidities; positional therapy; oral appliance (not severe disease, not without teeth); nasal expiratory device; stimulants (modafinil or armodafinil; caution with cardiovascular, hepatic, psychiatric disease; lower doses in the elderly); nasal steroids; surgery (tracheostomy is a cure); weight loss; avoid alcohol and sedatives; refer to a sleep physician Slides 11–13, 15
Mortality ★
Untreated: 2.5 times the driving accidents, 3 to 6 times the risk of sudden cardiac death at night, high heart failure mortality, 3 times the risk of hypertension; pulmonary hypertension, stroke, insulin resistance and diabetes, arrhythmias, ischemic heart disease, fatty liver (2 to 3 times), reflux (75 to 100%); lower mortality in the elderly, who compensate Slides 18–22
Central sleep apnea (CSA)★ Professor emphasized1 not covered

★ “I really want everyone to understand these risk factors are very different” (heart failure, stroke, opioids, high altitude, CPAP use), “because the treatment for central sleep apnea is really taking care of their risk factors”; CPAP first, then ASV, never with poor left ventricular function; home testing is not effective here.

Name of Condition
Central sleep apnea (CSA) Slide 23
Definition
Sleep-disordered breathing in which the brainstem stops sending signals to breathe: no airflow and no respiratory effort Slides 3, 24
Etiology (cause)
Increased sensitivity to carbon dioxide causing unstable breathing, hyperventilation alternating with apnea; in heart failure, Cheyne-Stokes breathing from a prolonged circulation delay to the carotid chemoreceptors Slide 24
Epidemiology (who)
Less common than obstructive sleep apnea Slide 24
Risk Factors
Congestive heart failure, stroke, opioid medications, hypoxia, high altitude, use of continuous positive airway pressure Slide 24
Pathology
Airflow stops because respiratory effort is absent or decreased (in obstructive apnea the airway collapses while effort continues) Slides 3, 24
Clinical Manifestation
Insomnia, frequent awakenings, nocturnal dyspnea, nonrestorative sleep, daytime sleepiness; snoring less common than in obstructive apnea; sounds very similar to obstructive apnea Slide 25
Diagnosis
Polysomnography (home testing not sufficient): repetitive apneas without thoracic-abdominal excursion Slide 26
Treatment/Therapy
Treat the cause (optimize heart failure therapy, reduce opioids, descend from altitude); continuous positive airway pressure first; adaptive servo-ventilation if it fails, avoided especially in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction Slide 26
Mortality ★
Not covered in the lecture
Obesity hypoventilation syndrome (Pickwickian syndrome)★ Professor emphasized

★ “Diagnosis of exclusion is kind of the big thing”; “the gender does not matter here”; weight loss plus BiPAP or CPAP, “first line, first line, first line”; cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death.

Name of Condition
Obesity hypoventilation syndrome (OHS), Pickwickian syndrome Slide 28
Definition
Awake alveolar hypoventilation (raised awake arterial carbon dioxide) in an obese person that cannot be attributed to another condition; a diagnosis of exclusion Slides 28, 32
Etiology (cause)
Obesity with reduced lung function and reduced inspiratory muscle strength; severe obstructive sleep apnea may be present (70% have severe obstructive symptoms) Slides 29–30
Epidemiology (who)
More likely as body mass index rises; patients may present in their 50s and 60s; male sex is not a risk factor; 90% have coexisting obstructive apnea symptoms (70% severe), 10% coexisting sleep hypoventilation (older women) Slides 28–30
Risk Factors
Central obesity (high waist-to-hip ratio), reduced lung function from obesity, reduced inspiratory muscle strength, severe obstructive sleep apnea Slide 29
Pathology
Awake hypoventilation with hypercapnia; late disease: hypoxemic hypercapnic respiratory failure and right-sided heart failure from pulmonary hypertension Slides 28, 30
Clinical Manifestation
Nonspecific: obese (body mass index over 30) and hypersomnolent, loud snoring, choking, fatigue, poor memory, small oropharynx, thick neck, dyspnea on exertion, facial plethora, elevated jugular venous pressure, hepatomegaly, pedal edema Slides 30–31
Diagnosis
Screen when awake saturation is 94% or less or the overnight lowest is below 80%, or with unexplained dyspnea, right heart failure, facial plethora or a raised bicarbonate (over 27: get an arterial blood gas). Diagnose: body mass index over 30, awake arterial carbon dioxide over 45 mm Hg, other causes excluded; labs (metabolic panel, blood count, thyroid, pulmonary function, chest film, toxicology); gold standard polysomnography with continuous nocturnal carbon dioxide monitoring Slides 31–32
Treatment/Therapy
Multidisciplinary; first line weight loss plus continuous positive airway pressure (with obstructive apnea) or bilevel positive airway pressure (with sleep hypoventilation) and lifestyle change; second line tracheostomy with nocturnal ventilation, bariatric surgery, medications after diet and exercise fail; refer to a sleep specialist Slides 33–34
Mortality ★
High morbidity and mortality; progresses untreated; cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death; more hospital and intensive care use; poor prognosis with significant awake hypoxemia at diagnosis Slide 35

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)★ Professor emphasized1 not covered

★ He called idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis "kind of the most common thing"; the four over-65 criteria allow a diagnosis without a surgical lung biopsy.

Name of Condition
Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), usual interstitial pneumonia Slide 35
Definition
The most common idiopathic (fibrosing) interstitial pneumonia: pulmonary fibrosis with no associated cause Slides 32, 35
Etiology (cause)
Unknown; smoking is the cause in the majority of cases Slides 35, 37
Epidemiology (who)
★ The most common subtype of idiopathic interstitial pneumonia, itself the most common diagnosis among patients with interstitial lung disease; 67 to 75% of patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and familial lung fibrosis have smoked; the four criteria that make a surgical lung biopsy unnecessary apply over 65 Slides 31–32, 35
Risk Factors
Smoking (67 to 75% of patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and familial lung fibrosis have smoked; smoking is the cause in the majority of cases) Slides 31, 37
Pathology
As in all fibrosis: inflammatory cells gather in the alveolar walls, alveolitis drives fibroblast production and collagen causes the fibrosis; CT shows diffuse, patchy fibrosis with pleural-based honeycombing Slides 6, 34
Clinical Manifestation
Insidious dry cough, exertional dyspnea, fatigue and malaise; clubbing, bibasilar inspiratory crackles, tachypnea, reduced chest expansion Slide 33
Diagnosis
Restrictive pattern (normal or increased ratio, decreased lung volumes); chest film with progressive fibrosis over several areas; CT with diffuse patchy fibrosis and pleural-based honeycombing; lavage or biopsy as needed. Over 65 with idiopathic disease and crackles, restrictive physiology, progressive fibrosis over years and honeycombing on high-resolution CT: 90% confidence, no surgical lung biopsy needed Slides 34–35
Treatment/Therapy
Controversial: nothing has improved survival or quality of life compared with no treatment; smoking cessation is critical Slide 37
Mortality ★

Sarcoidosis★ Professor emphasized

★ "Sarcoid, you could see questions about"; lupus pernio with a dry cough is a red flag; know the lab clues and the stages.

Name of Condition
Sarcoidosis Slide 39
Definition
A multi-organ disease of idiopathic cause with noncaseating granulomatous inflammation, most often of the lungs and lymphatic system Slide 39
Etiology (cause)
Unknown; environmental triggers (viral, bacterial, mycobacterial, inhaled agents, mold) in a genetically predisposed person Slides 39–40
Epidemiology (who)
Granulomatous lung inflammation in about 90%; higher in North American Black and Northern European white people; more often women; onset in the third and fourth decades Slides 39–40
Risk Factors
Genetic predisposition plus environmental exposure Slide 39
Pathology
Noncaseating granulomas (T lymphocytes, macrophages, epithelioid cells) in lungs, nodes, eyes, skin, liver, spleen, salivary glands, heart, nervous system Slides 20, 39
Clinical Manifestation
Cough, insidious dyspnea, chest discomfort, malaise, fever, weakness, weight loss, nontender lymphadenopathy; erythema nodosum, parotid enlargement, hepatosplenomegaly, crackles, ★ lupus pernio (painful skin sores on the face) Slides 42–43
Diagnosis
Chest film: symmetric bilateral hilar and right paratracheal adenopathy, stages I to IV; ★ elevated sedimentation rate and angiotensin-converting enzyme (40 to 80%), hypercalcemia (5%), hypercalciuria, eosinophilia, leukopenia; transbronchial or fine-needle node biopsy with noncaseating granulomas confirms; biopsy is essential whenever findings suggest an alternative diagnosis such as lymphoma Slides 25, 44–45
Treatment/Therapy
About 90% respond to corticosteroids (indications: disabling constitutional symptoms, hypercalcemia, iritis, uveitis, arthritis, central nervous system or cardiac involvement, granulomatous hepatitis, skin lesions other than erythema nodosum, progressive pulmonary lesions); methotrexate, azathioprine or infliximab if intolerant or refractory; yearly exam, pulmonary function, chest film, electrocardiogram, chemistry and eye exam Slides 46–48
Mortality ★
Best with hilar adenopathy alone; parenchymal involvement is worse; 20% with lung involvement develop irreversible impairment (fibrosis, bronchiectasis, cavitation) Slide 48

Name of Condition
Asbestosis Slide 55
Definition
Interstitial fibrosis of the terminal bronchioles, alveoli and interstitium from inhaled asbestos fibers that cannot be removed Slide 56
Etiology (cause)
Asbestos fibers from insulation, demolition, shipyards and construction Slide 55
Epidemiology (who)
Exposure over 10 to 20 years; patients usually seek attention about 15 years after exposure Slide 55
Risk Factors
Asbestos exposure; cigarette smoking increases radiographic changes and markedly increases lung carcinoma Slides 55–56
Pathology
Chronic inflammation and macrophage invasion form fibrosis; fibers can reach the pleura and cause mesothelioma; ★ ferruginous bodies in tissue suggest significant exposure Slides 55–56
Clinical Manifestation
Dyspnea on exertion, rarely cough; fine bibasilar crackles and clubbing with progression Slide 57

Hypersensitivity pneumonitis (extrinsic allergic alveolitis) Slide 65
Definition
A non-atopic, non-asthmatic inflammatory lung disease caused by inhaled organic material Slide 65
Etiology (cause)

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)★ Professor emphasized

★ The most narration time of the lecture: a feared complication of sepsis, trauma, aspiration and more.

Name of Condition
Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) Slide 72
Definition
Acute non-cardiogenic lung injury: within 7 days of a known insult, bilateral opacities not explained by effusion, atelectasis or nodules, failure not fully explained by heart failure or fluid overload, arterial oxygen to inspired oxygen ratio under 300 mm Hg Slide 73
Etiology (cause)
Direct: aspiration of gastric contents, diffuse pneumonia, lung contusion, toxic gas, near-drowning, high altitude, radiation, upper airway obstruction, fat, air, amniotic fluid or thrombus emboli. Systemic: sepsis, severe pancreatitis, shock, nonthoracic trauma, overdoses (opioids, aspirin, phenothiazine, tricyclic antidepressants, cocaine), multiple transfusions, disseminated intravascular coagulation, cardiopulmonary bypass, protamine, chemotherapy, amiodarone, nitrofurantoin Slide 72
Epidemiology (who)
About one-third of cases first present with sepsis Slide 72
Risk Factors
The direct and systemic insults above Slide 72
Pathology
Cytokine damage to capillary endothelium and alveolar epithelium; increased permeability, surfactant loss, alveolar collapse and refractory hypoxemia; hyperinflammatory phenotype (high interleukin-6, soluble tumor necrosis factor receptor) with multiorgan failure Slides 72–73
Clinical Manifestation
Profound dyspnea 12 to 48 hours after the insult; tachypnea, labored breathing, intercostal retractions, crackles; hypoxemia refractory to supplemental oxygen; multiple organ failure Slide 73
Diagnosis
Bilateral infiltrates becoming confluent, air bronchograms in about 80%, normal heart size with few effusions; echocardiography excludes cardiogenic edema; severity by arterial oxygen to inspired oxygen ratio (mild 200 to 300, moderate 100 to 200, severe under 100 mm Hg); differential: cardiogenic edema, diffuse alveolar hemorrhage, severe bilateral pneumonia, autoimmune lung disease Slide 73
Treatment/Therapy
Treat the precipitant; low tidal volume ventilation; titrate positive end-expiratory pressure (arterial oxygen above 60 mm Hg); early prone positioning in moderate to severe disease; conservative fluids; transfuse at hemoglobin 7 g/dL; corticosteroids conditionally within 14 days; sedatives, analgesics, antipyretics; avoid oscillatory ventilation and routine pulmonary artery catheters; neuromuscular blockade controversial; extracorporeal membrane oxygenation as salvage Slide 74
Mortality ★
About 30% mortality with low tidal volume ventilation; 20% after trauma to 80% with end-stage liver disease; lung function improves over 6 to 12 months; residual diffusion deficits, weakness and fatigue Slides 72, 74

Cor pulmonale (acute and chronic)★ Professor emphasized

★ “COPD [chronic obstructive pulmonary disease] is the absolute leading cause”; “that alveolar hypoxia is the most important driver of that pulmonary hypertension when we get into our COPD patients”; “the visual hallmark of bad right heart pressure overload is that septal flattening … kind of turns the left ventricle into a D shape.”

Name of Condition
Cor pulmonale, acute and chronic Slides 4, 9
Definition
Alteration in the structure and function of the right ventricle (enlargement, hypertrophy or dilation) caused exclusively by a primary disorder of the respiratory system or pulmonary vasculature: an elevated mean pulmonary artery pressure with a normal pulmonary capillary wedge pressure. Right ventricular failure from left heart failure (Group 2 pulmonary hypertension) is NOT cor pulmonale. Acute: sudden right ventricular dilation and dysfunction over minutes to hours; chronic: concentric hypertrophy, then late dilation, over months to years Slides 4, 9, 18
Etiology (cause)
Parenchymal (primarily chronic): ★ chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, the absolute leading cause; interstitial lung disease (fibrotic destruction of lung architecture); sleep-disordered breathing (chronic nocturnal alveolar hypoventilation). Vascular (acute and chronic): acute pulmonary thromboembolism (sudden macroscopic occlusion); primary pulmonary arterial hypertension (idiopathic vascular remodeling, Group 1); chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (unresolved clot burden, Group 4). Acute cor pulmonale: massive pulmonary embolism. Chronic: chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, interstitial lung disease, pulmonary arterial hypertension Slides 5, 9
Epidemiology (who)
Present in more than 50% of patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease; left ventricular failure is still the most common cause of right ventricular failure overall Slides 5, 18
Risk Factors
The underlying lung or pulmonary vascular disease; global alveolar hypoxia and hypercapnia (the chronic obstructive pulmonary disease “Blue Bloater”); loss of the vascular bed (emphysema, fibrosis, embolism); polycythemia from chronic hypoxia, which raises blood viscosity Slides 6–7
Pathology
★ Alveolar hypoxia is the single most important driver of pulmonary hypertension in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: global alveolar hypoxia and hypercapnia trigger diffuse, system-wide vasoconstriction (unlike the targeted reflex that shunts blood away from one hypoxic area), a massive rise in pulmonary vascular resistance and a relentless rise in right ventricular afterload. Vascular bed obliteration (emphysema, fibrosis, embolism) makes the resistance anatomical, not just functional, and polycythemia adds viscosity. The right ventricle is a thin-walled, high-compliance, low-pressure chamber, highly sensitive to sudden afterload changes. Acute: wall tension rises instantly (dilation, stretch, microinfarction), the septum bows leftward into the left ventricle, and left ventricular filling and output fall (hypotension, collapse). Chronic: compensatory hypertrophy until exhaustion, then decompensation, with raised diastolic pressures transmitted back into the systemic veins Slides 6–8, 10–11
Clinical Manifestation
Acute: severe sudden dyspnea, sweating, rapid pulse, pallor, syncope; distended neck veins, tricuspid regurgitation murmur (stretched annulus), tachycardia. Chronic: unproductive cough, progressive exertional dyspnea, the “Blue Bloater” phenotype; right ventricular heave (parasternal lift along the left sternal border), intensified P2, systolic murmur of tricuspid regurgitation; tender hepatomegaly, ascites, dependent peripheral pitting edema Slides 9–11
Diagnosis
Exclude elevated left-sided filling pressures first. A stepwise funnel: chest X-ray (interstitial markings or hyperinflation) and pulmonary function tests (obstructive: reduced forced expiratory volume in 1 second over forced vital capacity, the most common; restrictive: reduced total lung capacity) find lung disease (Group 3); a ventilation/perfusion scan or computed tomography pulmonary angiography excludes segmental defects (Group 4); right heart catheterization and echo showing a raised mean pulmonary artery pressure with a wedge pressure ≤15 mmHg leave primary vascular disease (Group 1). Chest film: flattened diaphragms (hyperinflation), enlarged central pulmonary arteries; the fields may look deceptively clear in pulmonary arterial hypertension or embolism. Electrocardiogram: peaked P waves (right atrial enlargement), tall R wave in V1 (right ventricular hypertrophy), severe right axis deviation in chronic disease; abnormal in 70% of acute pulmonary embolism. Echocardiography: right ventricular enlargement, estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure and ★ systolic septal flattening, the D-shaped left ventricle Slides 4, 12–14
Treatment/Therapy
The prime directive: treat the underlying lung disease. Continuous supplemental oxygen in Group 3 (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, interstitial lung disease): “oxygen is a drug” that reverses alveolar hypoxic vasoconstriction. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: bronchodilators and aggressive treatment of respiratory infections. Acute embolism: thrombolytics and anticoagulation (expand blood volume if hypotensive). Pulmonary arterial hypertension: targeted therapy (phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors, endothelin receptor antagonists, prostacyclins). Fluids: careful diuresis (loop diuretics, spironolactone) for retention, never aggressive: the damaged right ventricle relies on elevated preload, and over-diuresis starves the left ventricle Slides 15–16
Mortality ★
Acute: decreased left ventricular filling and immediate cardiovascular collapse (shock). Chronic: progressive volume overload, hypoxemia and right heart failure. Over-diuresis causes a disastrous drop in cardiac output; hypokalemia can trigger lethal arrhythmias Slides 9, 16

Pulmonary hypertension (World Health Organization Groups 1 to 5) Slide 19
Definition
Raised pressure in the pulmonary circulation that loads the right ventricle, classified by the World Health Organization by mechanism: pre-capillary Group 1 (pulmonary arterial hypertension; obliterative/proliferative), Group 3 (lung disease/hypoxia) and Group 4 (chronic thromboembolic; obstructive); post-capillary Group 2 (left heart disease; passive); Group 5 (unclear/multifactorial; complex) Slide 19
Etiology (cause)
Group 1: idiopathic, connective tissue disease, human immunodeficiency virus, schistosomiasis. Group 2: obstruction to pulmonary venous outflow (heart failure with reduced or preserved ejection fraction, mitral stenosis). Group 3: chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, interstitial lung disease, sleep apnea. Group 4: chronic thromboembolic occlusion. Group 5: hematologic, systemic and metabolic disorders (for example, sarcoidosis) Slide 19
Epidemiology (who)
Not covered in the lecture
Risk Factors
Chronic hypoxia (a potent pulmonary vasoconstrictor); chronic lung disease; previous pulmonary embolism (chronic thromboembolic disease); left heart disease Slides 19, 27
Pathology
Pressure destroys the pump in three stages: compensatory right ventricular hypertrophy against rising pulmonary vascular resistance; dilation and failure (the Starling mechanism fails, end-diastolic volume rises); ventricular interdependence (the dilated right ventricle bows the septum into the left ventricle), which impairs left ventricular diastolic filling and output and causes exertional syncope despite normal left ventricular contractility Slide 20
Clinical Manifestation
Exertional dyspnea and fatigue, often the only early symptom and frequently misattributed to deconditioning (World Health Organization class I to II); dizziness, lightheadedness and marked limitation of activity (class III); syncope or presyncope, an ominous sign of a fixed low cardiac output (class IV); end-stage abdominal bloating, early satiety and edema, present even at rest. Exam: elevated jugular venous pressure with large a waves (early) or regurgitant v waves (tricuspid insufficiency); parasternal lift (right ventricular heave); accentuated P2; right ventricular S3 gallop; hepatojugular reflux (sustained pressure on the liver raises the venous pressure by more than 1 cm); pitting edema to the hips and sacrum when severe; cool extremities; lungs typically clear, unlike the orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea and crackles of left heart failure Slides 21–23
Diagnosis
Echocardiography is the most useful initial (screening) test: right ventricular size and function, pulmonary pressures and left-sided valvular or myocardial mimics. A ventilation/perfusion scan is essential to exclude chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (do not rely on computed tomography angiography alone). ★ Right heart catheterization is the mandatory confirmatory test: it directly measures mean pulmonary artery pressure, wedge pressure and pulmonary vascular resistance to confirm pre-capillary pulmonary hypertension (a normal or low wedge ≤15 mmHg with a raised mean pulmonary artery pressure; above 15 mmHg points to left heart failure). Chest film: enlarged central pulmonary arteries with peripheral pruning Slides 23–24
Treatment/Therapy
Careful diuresis (intravenous loop diuretics venodilate before they diurese; avoid aggressive over-diuresis, which drops preload and crashes output); oxygen to keep PaO2 (arterial oxygen pressure) above 60 mmHg, especially during sleep or exertion; avoid strict bedrest: supervised, steady aerobic rehabilitation; annual influenza and pneumococcal immunizations. Drug classes: endothelin receptor antagonists (ambrisentan, bosentan, macitentan), ★ phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil), prostacyclins (epoprostenol, treprostinil, iloprost, selexipag; the most potent vasodilators, essential for severe disease). Vasoreactive: high-dose oral calcium channel blockers. Not vasoreactive: low or intermediate risk (class II/III) = oral combination therapy (endothelin receptor antagonist plus phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitor; early combination beats sequential monotherapy); high risk (class IV or rapid decline) = continuous intravenous or subcutaneous prostanoids (epoprostenol). Refractory disease (or pulmonary veno-occlusive disease): lung transplantation, the only definitive option, with intravenous support as a bridge. Early palliative care discussions and advance directives Slides 26–29
Mortality ★
Estimated 1-year mortality by risk: low (<5%: no right ventricular failure signs, slow progression, no syncope, class I or II, 6-minute walk >440 m); intermediate (5 to 10%: no right ventricular failure signs, moderate progression, occasional syncope, class III, 165 to 440 m); high (>10%: ★ clinical signs of right ventricular failure present, rapid progression, repeated syncope, class IV, <165 m). Cardiogenic pulmonary edema (the pressure problem) Slide 33
Definition
Fluid flooding the alveolar airspace when the outward capillary hydrostatic pressure exceeds the inward plasma oncotic pressure across an intact but overwhelmed capillary membrane: the pressure problem, “the pump is failing,” with ★ a wedge pressure above 18 to 20 mmHg Slides 32–33, 38
Etiology (cause)
Systolic dysfunction (ischemic loss of functioning myocardium; heart failure with reduced ejection fraction); diastolic dysfunction (myocardial stiffening and aging; heart failure with preserved ejection fraction); valvular lesions (chronic aortic or mitral regurgitation, severe stenosis) Slide 34
Epidemiology (who)
Adults: usually ischemia-driven loss of pump function (reduced ejection fraction). The elderly: age-related myocardial stiffening (preserved ejection fraction) Slide 42
Risk Factors
Heart failure (reduced or preserved ejection fraction), myocardial ischemia, valvular disease; flash edema triggers: acute myocardial infarction, severe ischemia, severe hypertension, and in chronic heart failure nonadherence, excess salt or rapid arrhythmias Slides 34, 46
Pathology
Elevated capillary hydrostatic pressure is the engine; plasma oncotic pressure is the failing counterbalance; the semipermeable membrane stays intact but is overwhelmed, so fluid floods the alveoli. The balloon-wedged catheter “looks forward” through the capillary bed to the left atrial pressure. The elderly heart (preserved ejection fraction) has an elevated end-diastolic pressure, extreme sensitivity to preload and a narrow therapeutic margin Slides 32, 38, 42
Clinical Manifestation
Acute dyspnea, diaphoresis and tripoding (catecholamine surge, extreme work of breathing); bilateral rales/crackles and wheezing (fluid-filled airways, peribronchial cuffing); S3 gallop (an overloaded, failing ventricle); pink, frothy sputum (alveolar fluid shift with microvascular hemorrhage) Slide 35
Diagnosis

Transthoracic echo: the best test for structure (ventricular size, ejection fraction, valves). Pulmonary artery catheter/right heart catheterization: the ultimate hemodynamic arbiter when the cause is uncertain (★ wedge above 18 to 20 mmHg confirms cardiogenic hydrostatic failure). Chest X-ray: batwing (central perihilar) opacities, peribronchial cuffing, Kerley B lines (fluid in the interlobular septa), bilateral pleural effusions. Long term: quadruple therapy (angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitor such as sacubitril-valsartan, beta-blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors). Adults (reduced ejection fraction) generally tolerate aggressive diuresis; the elderly (preserved ejection fraction) need gentle, precise diuresis. Stage D: immediate evaluation for heart transplant, mechanical circulatory support (left ventricular assist device) or palliative hospice care Slides 39–42, 44
Mortality ★
Quadruple therapy gives the greatest mortality reduction in reduced ejection fraction. Elderly: over-diuresis causes precipitous hypotension, under-diuresis recurrent drowning. Stage D (marked symptoms at rest despite maximal medical therapy, frequent hospitalizations) is the break point that triggers referral for advanced therapies Slides 41–42, 44

Pulmonary embolism (PE), part of venous thromboembolism Slide 48
Definition
Sudden macroscopic occlusion of the pulmonary vasculature by thromboembolism. Deep venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism are one continuum of the same disease (venous thromboembolism), ranging from asymptomatic disease to hemodynamic collapse and death; massive embolism causes acute cor pulmonale Slides 5, 9, 48
Etiology (cause)

Epidemiology (who)
Not covered in the lecture
Risk Factors
Hypercoagulability: malignancy, estrogen therapy, pregnancy, genetic thrombophilias, sepsis. Venous stasis: prolonged immobilization (flights over 8 hours), bed rest, paralysis, heart failure (New York Heart Association class III/IV). Endothelial injury: recent surgery, central venous catheters, pacemaker wires, pelvic or lower limb trauma. Not finding a known predisposition should never stop an objective evaluation when the presentation fits Slide 49
Pathology
Massive embolism causes acute right ventricular pressure overload; the septum bows into the left ventricle, left ventricular end-diastolic volume and filling fall, and cardiac output crashes (circulatory collapse). Distal, pleural-based occlusion causes pulmonary infarction Slides 50, 56
Clinical Manifestation
Three syndromes: isolated dyspnea (sudden, over minutes to hours, at rest or only with exertion); pleuritic pain and hemoptysis (distal pleural-based infarction, often a localized friction rub); circulatory collapse (syncope, severe hypotension, obstructive shock = massive proximal occlusion). ★ The dyspnea trap: about 25% of patients with proven pulmonary embolism completely lack dyspnea. Diagnosis
Bayesian: the pre-test probability, set independently of any test, chooses the test and how to read it. ★ Wells score: +3 signs and symptoms of deep venous thrombosis; +3 pulmonary embolism the leading diagnosis; +1.5 heart rate >100; +1.5 immobilization >3 days or surgery in the previous 4 weeks; +1.5 previous deep venous thrombosis or pulmonary embolism; +1 hemoptysis; +1 malignancy (on treatment, treated in the last 6 months, or palliative); ≤4 = unlikely, >4 = likely. Unlikely: ★ the Pulmonary Embolism Rule-out Criteria (very low gestalt, <15%): age <50, heart rate <100, oxygen saturation >94%, no hemoptysis, no exogenous estrogen, no prior venous thromboembolism, no surgery or trauma, no unilateral leg swelling; all true = stop the workup; any failed = a highly sensitive D-dimer (a negative enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay D-dimer safely excludes; positive = computed tomography pulmonary angiography). Both are outpatient rule-out tools; the D-dimer is of limited use in inpatients (universally high false positives). Likely: skip the D-dimer and go straight to ★ computed tomography pulmonary angiography, the gold standard (first-line for almost all; contrast, radiation, overdiagnosis of subsegmental emboli), or a ventilation/perfusion scan if contrast is contraindicated (renal dysfunction, severe contrast allergy, critically ill; often nondiagnostic with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or severe heart failure; a completely negative scan excludes embolism as surely as a negative pulmonary angiogram). Electrocardiogram: ★ S1Q3T3 (deep S wave in lead I, Q wave and inverted T wave in lead III) suggests severe right ventricular strain, not purely embolism; highly specific, low sensitivity. Chest film: usually abnormal but nonspecific (atelectasis, effusion); ★ classic signs Westermark (focal peripheral oligemia), Hampton’s hump (wedge-shaped pleural-based opacity, infarction) and Palla (enlarged right descending pulmonary artery) are highly suggestive but infrequent. ★ Transthoracic echo has poor sensitivity (about 50%) but very high specificity for right ventricular strain (the D-shaped septum): risk stratification, not diagnosis Slides 51–57
Treatment/Therapy
Stratify: low risk (normotensive, no right ventricular dysfunction, negative biomarkers) = standard anticoagulation; ★ submassive/intermediate risk (normotensive, systolic >90, but right ventricular dysfunction on echo or computed tomography pulmonary angiography, or myocardial necrosis with raised troponin or B-type natriuretic peptide) = high risk of sudden deterioration; massive/high risk (sustained hypotension with systolic <90 for more than 15 minutes, pulselessness or profound bradycardia) = immediate advanced intervention. Massive (unstable): systemic or catheter-directed thrombolysis; with absolute contraindications to thrombolytics (recent surgery, active bleeding) or a right heart clot in transit: mechanical thrombectomy or surgical embolectomy. Submassive: thrombolysis highly controversial (major bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage). Screen for chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension if dyspnea persists at 3 months Slides 58–62
Mortality ★
Massive: high mortality; submassive: high risk of sudden deterioration; low risk: excellent prognosis. The long tail is chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension Slides 58, 61

Name of Condition
Pulmonary nodule (a solitary nodule on a chest X-ray is a coin lesion) Slide 12
Definition
A nodule seen on a chest X-ray or chest computed tomography; a solitary nodule on a chest X-ray is sometimes called a coin lesion. Most are benign, but some are the start of a cancer Slides 4, 12
Etiology (cause)
Most are benign: a granuloma (inflammatory) or a hamartoma (connective tissue growth); some are the start of lung cancer Slides 4, 12
Epidemiology (who)
Found incidentally when a chest X-ray or chest computed tomography is done for another reason; most are benign Slide 12
Risk Factors
For malignancy: a patient at high risk of lung cancer (smoking is implicated in 85 to 90% of lung cancers), whose nodule is worked up to exclude malignancy Slides 3, 12, 16
Pathology
Concerning: ★ greater than 6 mm, upper lobes or hilar structures, ★ spiculated (spikes, deviations or lobulations in the border), more than 3 nodules, enlarging over time, contrast enhancing on computed tomography, cavitated or nonsolid, a patient at high risk; ground-glass (stippled) or eccentric calcification. Definition

Transudates: ★ congestive heart failure in over 90%, cirrhosis with ascites, nephrotic syndrome, peritoneal dialysis Slides 81–82, 102–103, 111
Epidemiology (who)
Over 1.5 million patients a year in the United States; up to 62% of critically ill patients in intensive care; up to 30% have several causes at once; ★ congestive heart failure is the leading cause Slide 83
Risk Factors
Heart failure; cancer (malignant effusions can occur years after the diagnosis); hepatic disease; trauma; asbestos exposure; infection risk factors (diabetes, alcoholism, immunosuppression, poor dental hygiene); drugs (amiodarone, methotrexate, beta blockers) Slides 83–84
Pathology
Normally about 10 mL of fluid enters from parietal pleura capillaries and leaves by parietal pleura lymphatics, inflow equal to reabsorption; an effusion forms when that balance fails. Adhesions between the visceral and parietal pleurae form loculations (inflammatory causes such as empyema) Slides 4–5, 81, 94
Clinical Manifestation
Asymptomatic if small; dyspnea, dry cough and pleuritic chest pain (sharp, worse on deep inspiration, referred to the same-side shoulder or upper abdomen). Clues: edema, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (heart failure); fever, purulent sputum (pneumonia); hemoptysis (malignancy); fever, night sweats, hemoptysis, weight loss (tuberculosis). Exam: no findings under 300 mL; asymmetrical expansion; trachea shifted away over 1000 mL; decreased tactile fremitus; dullness; diminished or absent breath sounds, bronchial toward the top; friction rub Slides 85–88
Diagnosis
Upright chest radiograph: 75 mL blunts the posterior and 175 mL the lateral costophrenic angle; the lateral decubitus film (effusion side down) has been replaced by ultrasound, which also guides thoracentesis; contrast chest computed tomography finds as little as 10 mL. Diagnostic thoracentesis (50 to 100 mL) for a new effusion with no clinical cause: protein, lactate dehydrogenase, white cell count with differential, Gram stain, cytology, culture; appearance alone is not diagnostic. ★ Light’s criteria: an exudate if at least one of pleural to serum protein over 0.5, pleural to serum lactate dehydrogenase over 0.6, or pleural lactate dehydrogenase over two-thirds of the upper limit of normal serum lactate dehydrogenase Slides 90–91, 93, 95–96, 98–99, 101
Treatment/Therapy
Treat the underlying disorder (diuretics first for heart-failure transudates); diagnostic or therapeutic thoracentesis (relieves dyspnea and tests whether the lung expands); chest tube; pleurodesis for recurrent malignant effusions (lung, breast, ovarian) as palliative care, for an expandable lung; an indwelling tunneled pleural catheter, placed as an outpatient and drained at home, for short prognoses, a non-expandable lung or rapidly recurring fluid Slides 97, 113–114

Definition
A viral myalgia of the striated intercostal muscles Slide 116
Etiology (cause)
Often coxsackie B virus (an RNA (ribonucleic acid) enterovirus), also echoviruses, typically after an upper respiratory or gastrointestinal infection Slides 116, 119
Epidemiology (who)
Adults (pleuritic pain) and children (abdominal pain); a summer peak; spread by fecal-oral contamination and contaminated water Slides 117, 119
Risk Factors
Exposure to coxsackievirus or echovirus by the fecal-oral route or contaminated water Slide 119
Pathology
The virus targets the striated intercostal muscles, causing inflammation or necrosis and frequent elevations of serum creatine kinase Slide 116
Clinical Manifestation
Acute onset of fever and pleuritic pain (adults) or abdominal pain (children); paroxysms of severe, knife-like pain lasting 15 to 30 minutes with diaphoresis and tachypnea; splinting of the chest during attacks; tender involved muscles Slide 117
Diagnosis
Elevated serum creatine kinase; a chest radiograph, usually normal, rules out other causes of chest pain Slides 116, 118
Treatment/Therapy
Supportive: nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and heat to the affected muscles; prevention by hand hygiene and pool chlorination Slides 118–119
Mortality ★
Symptoms resolve but may recur a few days later; a self-limited, benign course without long-term sequelae; orchitis and aseptic meningitis in under 10% Slides 118–119
