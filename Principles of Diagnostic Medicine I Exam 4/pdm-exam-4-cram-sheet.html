Landmarks You Can Feel (Lecture 17)

TermWhat you need to know

Jugular notchDeep to it is the TRACHEA.

The Normal Chest Film

TermWhat you need to know
First lineChest radiographs are the most common radiologic study (radiographs = 75% of images ordered) and almost always FIRST for a pulmonary or cardiac complaint; pain and shortness of breath.
★ Posteroanterior vs anteroposteriorPOSTEROANTERIOR = ambulatory, standing. ANTEROPOSTERIOR = supine, HEART MAGNIFIED: do not call it enlarged on that view alone.
★ Aortic knobThe rounded bulge at the top of the left mediastinal border (the aortic arch); pulmonary artery just below, then the left hilum.
★ Costophrenic anglesSharp Vs where each hemidiaphragm meets the chest wall: "nice and sharp" is normal. Gastric bubble under the left hemidiaphragm.
★ Heart sizeNo more than HALF the posteroanterior span of the chest; one third right of the midline, two thirds left.
★ HilaBisect the chest; mostly pulmonary arteries and veins (plus fat, nodes, bronchial walls); LEFT slightly HIGHER; concave, similar size and density.
★ TracheaSmooth radiopaque border, radiolucent inside, MIDLINE.
The rest of the readLungs radiolucent; retrosternal and retrocardiac spaces; only pulmonary arteries and veins as vascular markings; each rib one at a time, both sides; spinous processes midline. Lateral film: thoracic spine has a posterior arch.

Upper Airway Films

TermWhat you need to know
IndicationsMuffled voice; stridor (wheeze heard from the mouth without a stethoscope); suspected sinus infection; anterior neck pain.
Water's viewMaxillary and frontal sinuses, 45 degrees to the orbitomeatal line. For sinusitis: not really needed, but can confirm it and possibly REDUCE ANTIBIOTIC use.
★ Lateral soft-tissue neckTrachea = the dark, patent, radiolucent air column. The esophagus sits in the RETROTRACHEAL space and shows NO air: collapsed = NORMAL when not eating.
Bones on the neck filmSlight ANTERIOR curve (cervical lordosis). The body below C7 is partly hidden by the FIRST RIB.
How to read itSuperior to inferior, left to right; airways dark; sinuses air-filled; bones continuous; always symmetry.

Is It the Film or the Patient?

TermWhat you need to know
Good exposureVessels to at least the PERIPHERAL THIRD; paraspinous margins; left hemidiaphragm visible behind the heart.
OverexposedDARK: thoracic spine, mediastinum, retrocardiac areas and nasogastric/endotracheal tubes show through.
UnderexposedWHITE: small vessels, NO spine and NO mediastinal anatomy.
InspirationDomes to the TENTH posterior rib standing (eighth to tenth seated or lying). Few ribs above the diaphragm = poor effort.
★ RotationSpinous processes to the clavicle borders. Do NOT use the tracheal shadow (a tube looks the same rotated).
The checklistTrachea midline or deviated; lungs shadowing or lucency; vessels enlarged; HILA masses or lymphadenopathy; mediastinum width or mass; PLEURA effusion, thickening, calcification; BONES lesions or fractures; soft tissues: don't miss a mastectomy.

Choosing the Study

TermWhat you need to know
Computed tomography first?ONLY for a suspected pulmonary embolism: the computed tomography ANGIOGRAM. Everything else starts with the radiograph.
Radiograph indicationsChest pain, dyspnea, cough over 6 WEEKS unexplained, unexplained weight loss in any chronic SMOKER, hemoptysis, adventitious sounds, support hardware (lines, tubes, pacemaker).
Computed tomographyMost common reason: an INCONCLUSIVE radiograph with a continuing problem. Also embolism, trauma, chronic obstructive pulmonary and interstitial lung disease, BONE (rib and spine fractures), cancer screening. Head and neck: retropharyngeal abscess or foreign body the radiograph missed.
Contrast is forVASCULAR pathology (embolism, dissection, aneurysm), focal lesions, the -itis conditions (magnetic resonance superior for vasculitis), METASTASES to lymph nodes.
UltrasoundAlmost exclusively TRAUMA: collapsed lung or fluid (extended focused assessment with sonography for trauma); procedures; NO contraindications. Ribs cast shadows.
★ Ventilation/perfusionFor pulmonary embolism when the angiogram can't be done (CONTRAST ALLERGY); INFERIOR to the angiogram. Ventilation: INHALED technetium-99m. Perfusion: INJECTED technetium-99m macroaggregated albumin. Result: low, medium or high probability.
Positron emission tomographyHigher resolution and sensitivity than single-photon, more cost; usually secondary to the radiograph; mostly for METASTASES; now done with computed tomography for a much CLEARER image.
Magnetic resonanceKing of soft tissue, but the lungs' anatomy makes computed tomography first. Used for a mass INCONCLUSIVE on computed tomography, patients who can't have it, vasculopathy (second choice); SUPERIOR to the angiogram for INTRAMURAL HEMATOMA. Inflammation: dark T1, bright T2.
Who reads itThe radiologist gives the official read; the clinician's read is often just for the answer.

Contraindications and Cautions

TermWhat you need to know
★ RadiographsNO true contraindications. Lifetime limit 100 millisieverts = about 10,000 chest radiographs or 25 chest computed tomography scans.
★ Pregnancy test"Always, always, always": beta human chorionic gonadotropin (urine or blood) on every female of childbearing age. Pregnancy is NOT an absolute contraindication; emergent imaging is done, even with contrast.
Lead shieldingEverything not being filmed, especially thyroid, ovaries, testes, breasts, fetus.
★ Contrast cautionsAnaphylaxis to medicines; ★ SHELLFISH anaphylaxis not yet tested for iodine allergy; iodine anaphylaxis or angioedema; HIGH CREATININE or blood urea nitrogen (renally cleared, NEPHROTOXIC); suspected THYROID dysfunction.
Magnetic resonance precautionsMetal implants that are NOT titanium; gadolinium allergy (rare); claustrophobia.

Reading Computed Tomography

TermWhat you need to know
PlanesTRANSVERSE (axial) = cross-section; FRONTAL (coronal) = both lungs, apex to base; SAGITTAL = one side in profile. A slice, not a stacked shadow.
AttenuationHIGH (bright): bone (very high), FRESH blood, contrast. LOW (dark): air and gas, fluid, coagulated blood. NORMAL: heart and muscle.
Angiogram pearlsDESCENDING AORTA always on the LEFT of the patient's vertebral column; patient's left = YOUR right; RIGHT ventricle nearest the sternum; LEFT ventricle has the thickest wall; for an embolism the PULMONARY ARTERIES matter most.
ClotA dark filling defect inside a bright, contrast-filled pulmonary artery.
VasculogramAlmost always a computed tomography angiogram: embolism, pulmonary hypertension, vasculitis, scleroderma.

The Five Patterns

TermWhat you need to know
PneumoniaCONSOLIDATION (infiltrate): white, filled-in lung where it should be dark. Don't sort bacterial from viral on the film.
★ Pleural effusionBLUNTED costophrenic angles; on computed tomography a gray collection compressing the lung.
★ Pulmonary edemaCEPHALIZATION of vessels, KERLEY B LINES (short horizontal lines at the outer lower lungs from thickened INTERLOBULAR SEPTA), pleural effusion, INCREASED cardiothoracic ratio.
PneumothoraxJET BLACK air with the COMPRESSED lung beside it.
★ Interstitial lung diseaseDiffuse abnormal patterns in the INTERSTITIUM, not the air sacs. Computed tomography: reticular (net-like), nodular (dots), HONEYCOMBING (cysts 3 to 10 millimeters), volume loss, traction bronchiectasis, GROUND-GLASS (hazy, pale), Kerley B lines.

The Equipment and What Each Test Measures (Lecture 20)

TermWhat you need to know
The framePulmonary function tests measure FLOW (how air moves), VOLUME (how much the lungs hold) and GAS TRANSFER (how well gas crosses into the blood).
★ Whole-body plethysmographyThe glass booth. Measures the total lung capacity, the functional residual capacity, the RESIDUAL VOLUME ("the key thing") and airway resistance.
SpirometerAirflow and some lung volumes (handheld or desktop).
Peak flow meterPeak expiratory flow; correlates with the forced expiratory volume in one second; bedside or home, as in asthma.
Blood gas analyzer · pulse oximeterGas exchange and oxygenation from a blood sample (pH, carbon dioxide and oxygen partial pressures, bicarbonate) · oxygen saturation and pulse rate from a fingertip clip (home use shown).
Accurate testingProperly calibrated equipment (the calibration syringe), clean components and correct patient technique.

The Four Volumes and Four Capacities

TermWhat you need to know
★ The tracing"See it in your sleep." A volume is one compartment; a capacity is a SUM of volumes.
Tidal volumeEach normal restful breath, about 500 mL.
Inspiratory reserve volumeExtra breathed IN on top of the tidal volume (3000 to 3300 mL).
Expiratory reserve volumeExtra breathed OUT after a normal breath out.
Residual volumeAir left after maximal expiration; can NEVER be breathed out (1200 mL).
Inspiratory capacityTidal + inspiratory reserve.
Functional residual capacityExpiratory reserve + residual: air left at the end of a quiet breath out, muscles relaxed (2200 to 2400 mL).
Vital capacityTidal + inspiratory reserve + expiratory reserve: everything EXCEPT the residual volume (4500 to 5000 mL).
Total lung capacityAll four volumes: the maximum the lungs hold (5700 to 6200 mL).

Spirometry and the Forced Values

TermWhat you need to know
How it is doneInhale maximally, then exhale forcefully, as fast as possible, for at least 6 SECONDS. Repeated 3 times; the HIGHEST volumes and flows are recorded (the slide; the recording said "average").
Forced vital capacityTotal volume forcefully and rapidly exhaled after a maximal breath; reached in about 6 seconds.
Forced expiratory volume in one secondAir blown out in the FIRST SECOND.
★ The ratioForced expiratory volume in one second over forced vital capacity: the % of the forced vital capacity out in the first second. Normal more than 80%; below 70% = obstruction.
★ The three most important valuesThe forced vital capacity, the forced expiratory volume in one second and their ratio.
Peak expiratory flow rateMaximum velocity of forced expiration: steepest slope of the volume-time curve, top of the flow-volume loop.
Forced expiratory flow 25% to 75%Average flow over the middle half of the forced vital capacity.
Normal volume-time curveSteep rise, then a plateau. Volume at 1 second = forced expiratory volume in one second; plateau = forced vital capacity.
★ Normal flow-volume loopExpiratory limb above the axis, inspiratory below; quick rise to a sharp peak, then a STRAIGHT fall to the volume axis.
Percent of predictedForced expiratory volume in one second and forced vital capacity 80 to 100%; total lung capacity 80 to 120%; diffusing capacity more than 60%. Ranges are given on the exam.

Why Order Them

TermWhat you need to know
Diagnosis (most common)A pulmonary symptom: chronic shortness of breath, cough, unexplained hypercapnia or hypoxemia (smokers, occupational exposures).
NOT a specific diagnosisThey show the pathophysiological derangement and help rule out parts of the differential.
Prognosis · surgical riskPrognosis of known disease · surgical risk before lung resection or with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.
ScreeningEarly, asymptomatic complications of non-pulmonary disease (autoimmune disease) or of medication (long-term AMIODARONE).

Obstructive Against Restrictive

TermWhat you need to know
Three categoriesObstructive, restrictive and pulmonary vascular (gas transfer impairment). NOT mutually exclusive (sarcoidosis can be all three).
ObstructiveIncreased airway resistance; trouble getting air OUT. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, ★ BRONCHIECTASIS (permanent airway dilation), cystic fibrosis.
RestrictiveDecrease in VOLUME; trouble getting air IN. Poor compliance (interstitial lung disease, chest wall: obesity, kyphosis, scoliosis) or neuromuscular weakness (amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, muscular dystrophy).
Gas transfer impairmentNormal mechanics; the alveolar-capillary membrane or pulmonary blood flow. Pulmonary hypertension, chronic thromboembolic disease, early emphysema, early interstitial lung disease.
★ The result tableObstruction: ratio DECREASED, below 70%. Restriction: forced vital capacity DECREASED, ratio normal or increased, 70% or more.
★ Obstructive volume-time curveA slow rise and a long, late plateau (severe: no plateau, forced vital capacity reduced).
★ Restrictive volume-time curveThe normal shape at a LOWER volume: the air comes out easily, there is less of it.
★ "A scoop in the loop"Obstruction: the expiratory limb is concave (COVING) after the peak; severe = low peak, long flat tail.
★ "A witch's hat"Restriction: narrow, tall, sharp point; everything smaller; peak normal or mildly reduced, no coving.
On the filmHyperinflated, flat diaphragms = obstruction. Small-volume lungs with reticular opacities = restriction (confirm with a low total lung capacity).

Reading a Report

TermWhat you need to know
★ Step 1The RATIO first. 70% is your marker.
Ratio lowAssess the forced vital capacity: normal = obstruction; low = obstruction or MIXED (severe obstruction lowers it too); check the total lung capacity.
Ratio normal or highAssess the forced vital capacity: low = possible restriction (confirm with lung volumes); normal = normal mechanics.
Mixed defectLow ratio AND low total lung capacity.
Case 1 (no audio)Smoker: ratio 52% (low), forced vital capacity 61%, total lung capacity 120% (not low) = OBSTRUCTION, scooped loop; little change after albuterol.
Case 2 (no audio)Animal trainer, crackles, saturation 88%: ratio 94%, forced vital capacity 39%, total lung capacity 44% = RESTRICTION; diffusing capacity LOW (38%): the membrane is affected.
Case 3 (no audio)Spinal cord injury, clear lungs: ratio normal, forced vital capacity 71%, total lung capacity 67% = RESTRICTION; diffusing capacity NORMAL (97%): the membrane is intact.

Each Standard Test's Job, and Oxygenation

TermWhat you need to know
Spirometry with the flow-volume loopDiagnoses OBSTRUCTIVE disease.
Lung volumesDiagnose RESTRICTIVE disease (the total lung capacity confirms restriction).
Diffusing capacity for carbon monoxideIntegrity of the alveolar-capillary membrane: diagnoses PULMONARY VASCULAR disease. Normal spirometry and volumes with a suspected gas transfer problem: order this next.
What the tests examineAirways, parenchyma, pulmonary vasculature, the bellows (diaphragm and chest wall) and the neural control of ventilation.
Specialized testsArterial blood gas; exercise oximetry; six-minute walk; peak flow; maximal inspiratory and expiratory pressures (diaphragmatic strength).
Six-minute walk testWalk as far as possible in 6 minutes: monitors response to treatment AND prognosis.
Exercise oximetryOxygenation at rest and with modest activity; quick and cheap.
OxygenationPulse oximeter (fingertip) and the blood gas analyzer; the arterial blood gas is a SPECIALIZED test, not a standard one.
