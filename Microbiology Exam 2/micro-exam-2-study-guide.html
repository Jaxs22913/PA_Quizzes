7.1 · Objective 1 — The four immunopathologies

ImmunopathologyWhat it isDirection
Allergy / hypersensitivityAn exaggerated, misdirected expression of immune responses to an allergen (antigen). Uses the same mechanisms as protective immunity. Four types: I, II, III are B cell mediated; IV is T cell mediatedOverreaction
AutoimmunityAbnormal responses to self antigensTypically overreaction
ImmunodeficiencyDeficiency or loss of immunityUnderreaction
CancerBoth a cause and an effect of immune dysfunctionUnderreaction

Cancer and immune dysfunction. Cancer is both a cause and an effect: immune failure permits it, and the tumor then suppresses immunity. Immune surveillance normally keeps cancer in check, and successful tumors evade and actively manipulate the response once established.

Four immunopathologies. The four categories are allergy, autoimmunity, immunodeficiency and cancer; allergy and autoimmunity are overreactions, while immunodeficiency and cancer are underreactions.

The hygiene hypothesis. Many allergens resemble parasitic antigens, and helminth infection (about 1 billion people heavily and persistently infected) produces exactly the allergic profile — CD4 type 2 helper T responses, high immunoglobulin E, more eosinophils and mast cells — yet people with chronic worm infections tend to have less allergic disease, for two proposed reasons: nonspecific immunoglobulin E competes for the Fc receptors on mast cells, basophils and activated eosinophils, and regulatory T cells suppress T cell responses in chronic parasitic infection. Where helminths were removed (Western Europe, North America) by better sanitation and treatment, the idea is that this arm of the immune system is underused and less successfully regulated, so it may overreact to harmless things.

Predisposition. A generalized predisposition to allergy is familial — not a predisposition to a specific allergy. Allergy is affected by age, infection and geographic area; atopic allergies may be lifelong, outgrown, or develop later in life. The four groups are defined by the effector mechanism, and the reaction can differ with the mechanism of exposure.

Allergens and the type I mechanism

Allergens are immunogenic: proteins, or lower molecular weight haptens. They typically enter through epithelial portals — respiratory, gastrointestinal, skin — and the organ where the allergy shows may or may not be the portal of entry.

DoseWhat happens
Sensitizing dose (first contact)Specific B cells form immunoglobulin E, which attaches to mast cells and basophils. Generally no signs or symptoms.
Provocative dose (later contact)The same allergen binds the immunoglobulin E on the mast cell (each cell binds 10,000–40,000 immunoglobulin E); mast cells degranulate, releasing inflammatory cytokines.

General targets are the skin, upper respiratory tract, gastrointestinal tract and conjunctiva (rashes, itching, redness, rhinitis, sneezing, diarrhea, tears); systemic targets are smooth muscle, mucous glands and nervous tissue (vascular dilation and constriction changing blood pressure and respiration).

ManifestationMechanism

Anaphylaxis

Cutaneous anaphylaxis is the wheal and flare skin reaction — the one used in allergy diagnosis. Systemic anaphylaxis is sudden respiratory and circulatory disruption that can be fatal in minutes. Allergen and route vary: bee stings, antibiotics and serum by injection; foods such as peanuts by mouth. The allergen reaches the bloodstream and activates connective tissue mast cells around blood vessels throughout the body. Fluid leaves the blood → drastic fall in blood pressure → tissue swelling → organ damage → death by asphyxiation from constricted airways; 500–1000 deaths a year in the United States.

What epinephrine does in anaphylaxis (five actions): stimulates reformation of endothelial tight junctions; relaxes bronchial smooth muscle; prevents or decreases upper airway mucosal edema; stimulates the heart; binds receptors on immune cells to suppress histamine release.

Late phase reaction. Immunoglobulin E reactions have an immediate response (the wheal and flare, from mast cell degranulation) followed about 6 hours later by a late phase reaction mediated by synthesized products such as leukotrienes. It attracts more cells, including eosinophils, whose cytokines enhance inflammation and make the tissue more sensitive next time. In asthma, measured as forced expiratory volume in 1 second, the immediate response is under 1 hour and the late phase response over 6 hours.

Arthus reaction. It is localized dermal injury from inflamed blood vessels, a vasculitis typically following a second injection of vaccine at a site, and normally self limiting.

Contact dermatitis follows the same two-dose logic as type I, but with T cells: first contact is the sensitizing dose; later contact is the reactive dose, with tissue damage from macrophage cytokines and cytotoxic T cells.

Desensitization (allergen-specific immunotherapy). The most common form is subcutaneous immunotherapy: small amounts of allergen by injection. The injected allergen stimulates high levels of allergen-specific immunoglobulin G that blocks the allergen from reaching the immunoglobulin E, so mast cells do not degranulate. It improves reactions in about 80% of those who complete it.

“Of course it’s not that simple”: desensitization also activates regulatory T cells, which inhibit T cell activation of B cells and release anti-inflammatory cytokines, and induces mediators that damp T cell proliferation and mast cell and eosinophil activity. Sublingual immunotherapy places allergen under the tongue as a tablet or liquid; it is used more elsewhere than in the United States (which has tablets for pollen and dust mite). A 4-year trial of a peanut dose equal to 1/75 of a kernel was effective and safe, protecting against accidental exposure; it has also worked for kiwi, hazelnut, milk and peach.

7.4 · Objective 4 — The mechanism of transfusion reactions

A transfusion reaction is type II. Donor and recipient are matched for the ABO blood group antigens because red blood cells carry no major histocompatibility complex molecules — ABO is what the immune system sees. You carry antibodies in your serum against the ABO antigens you lack. Transfusions are temporary, and an ABO incompatibility can be fatal through complement activation and cell lysis.

StepDetail
Cross-matchRecipient’s serum against donor’s red cells. Not the reverse: even whole blood carries too little donor antibody to coat the recipient’s cells densely enough to react
ConsequencesRed cell destruction → blocked glomeruli, fever, jaundice. Even incomplete complement activation leaves antibody-coated cells for natural killer cells and macrophages
Rh factor and hemolytic disease of the newborn (erythroblastosis fetalis). Rh antigen is on Rh-positive cells only. An Rh-negative person makes no anti-Rh antibody unless sensitized — by a bad transfusion or an incompatible pregnancy (Rh-negative mother, Rh-positive fetus). The first pregnancy is normally fine; it sensitizes. Later pregnancies may be hemolytic. Prevention is passive immunization with antibody against the Rh antigen (Rhogam), which prevents sensitization of the mother. Give it at 26–28 weeks, within 72 hours after birth, and after prenatal invasive tests, abdominal injury or accidental exposure.

7.6 · Objective 6 — The etiology of autoimmune conditions

In autoimmunity the immune system has lost tolerance to autoantigens and forms autoantibodies and sensitized T cells against them, destroying self tissue. It involves all hypersensitivity types except type I. There are genetic and gender predispositions: autoimmunities run in families, and women are more likely than men to have them. 7.8 · Objective 8 — The mechanisms of carcinogenesis

Cancer cells carry genetic alterations that disrupt the normal cell division cycle. Possible causes: errors in mitosis, genetic damage, activation of oncogenes, or retroviruses. Tumors may be benign (nonspreading, self-contained) or malignant (spreading from the tissue of origin to other sites). Immune surveillance keeps cancer “in check.”

Transformation: a cancer arises from a single cell that has accumulated multiple mutations; oncogenes are activated by radiation, chemicals or oncogenic viruses (papillomavirus and cervical carcinoma, hepatitis B virus and liver cancer, among others).

Necessary characteristics of cancer cells
They stimulate their own growth · ignore growth-inhibiting signals · avoid death by apoptosis · develop a blood supply (angiogenesis) · leave their site of origin to invade other tissues (metastasis) · replicate constantly · evade and outrun the immune response
Tumor antigens (more than 1000 identified)Where found
Tumor specificOn tumor cells, not on normal cells. Derived from viral proteins, mutated cellular proteins or amino acid recombinations
Tumor associatedOn tumor cells and on normal cells in smaller amounts
How successful tumors evade immunity. A variant tumor cell can cleave the stress molecule that natural killer and gamma-delta T cells recognize from its surface; the soluble molecule then binds the lymphocytes’ receptor, and the tumor cell escapes killing. Tumors also secrete transforming growth factor beta, suppressing immunity and recruiting regulatory T cells, which make more of it plus interleukin 10 and suppress the CD8 and CD4 type 1 helper cells specific for tumor antigens. In many cancers, the more regulatory T cells in a tumor, the worse the prognosis.

Tumor escape from natural killer cells. When tumor cells cleave a stress molecule that natural killer cells recognize and release it in soluble form, the tumor cell escapes being killed: the soluble molecule binds the killing receptor on natural killer and gamma-delta T cells.

10.6 · Objective 1 — Clostridium botulinum and botulism

Botulism is an intoxication associated with inadequate food preservation. C. botulinum is a spore-forming anaerobe of soil and water. Foodborne botulism is most prevalent with low-acid, home-canned foods (green beans, corn), but meats, fish and dairy can carry it, and rarely commercial foods (the 2007 Castleberry chili sauce outbreak). Pathogenesis: spores are on the food when it is gathered; if reliable temperature and pressure are not achieved, the air is evacuated but the spores remain; anaerobic conditions favor germination and growth; the toxin, botulin, is released and carried to neuromuscular junctions, where it blocks the release of acetylcholine, needed for muscle contraction.

11.1 · Objective 1 — Why novel therapies: resistance and stewardship

What drives the need for novel therapies is antimicrobial and antibiotic resistance, fed by:

  • Over-use of antibiotics in agriculture, in plants and in animals;
  • clinically inappropriate prescriptions, which create the need for antimicrobial stewardship: education of clinicians and of patients;
  • antibiotic prophylaxis, used to reduce infection risk “when possible” (11.3 lists the circumstances where it is justified);
  • patient non-compliance, such as stopping a course early or leaving against medical advice.

Antibiotic resistance is a type of drug resistance that renders the antibiotic ineffective in killing or controlling bacterial growth (slide 6). It belongs to the bacteria, not to the patient: an allergy is the patient reacting to the drug; resistance is the drug no longer working on the organism.

Resistance follows use. There are over 100 antibiotics on the market, and resistance is still becoming more prevalent. Slide 8 tracks three of them from discovery to widespread clinical use to the first resistance detected: penicillin, vancomycin and azithromycin each met resistant bacteria within a few years to about two decades of coming into wide use. (The exact years differ between sources; the pattern is the point.)

The “superbugs” (slide 16): methicillin-resistant and methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA, MSSA), vancomycin-resistant and vancomycin-intermediate Staphylococcus aureus (VRSA, VISA), toxigenic Escherichia coli, Clostridioides difficile, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae, vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus and Pseudomonas. The slide’s notes add the ESKAPE pathogens, which top the lists of organisms causing nosocomial (hospital-acquired) infections.

11.2 · Objective 1 — How antibiotics are chosen: testing, antibiograms, empiric therapy

Disk diffusion (Kirby-Bauer). White paper disks treated with antibiotics are placed on an agar plate inoculated with the organism. The antibiotic diffuses out, and the circular zone of inhibition around each disk shows how effective, or how ineffective, that antimicrobial is against the organism: a wide clear ring means growth was stopped; growth up to the disk means the drug had little effect.

Antibiograms are profiles of antimicrobial susceptibility, by percentage, used to guide empiric therapy. On the slide’s example, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus is far less often susceptible than methicillin-susceptible strains to ciprofloxacin and erythromycin, while both are fully susceptible to vancomycin. The limitations:

  • the minimum inhibitory concentration is not included;
  • the patient’s risk factors are not considered;
  • there are no data on synergy when antimicrobials are used in combination;
  • the data may apply only to a single hospital within a health system.

Empiric therapy means antibiotics prescribed before cultures are confirmed or other diagnostic results are available. It is common in human and veterinary medicine, especially for critically ill or emergent patients, is based on prior clinical experience and/or antibiograms, and is then adjusted once results arrive (for example, from intravenous to oral). “Why empirical therapy? Because it works!” (Klastersky, 2009).

11.4 · Objective 2 — Fecal microbiota transplant

Fecal microbiota transplant gives a patient screened donor stool to realign their gastrointestinal microflora, “the power of poop”.

  • It is old. It has been used for centuries in human and animal medicine; old Chinese texts give recipes for “yellow soup” (Ge Hong in the 4th century and Li Shizhen in the 16th are the sources usually cited).
  • Its researched use is Clostridioides difficile infection, about 500,000 cases a year and about 30,000 deaths in the United States alone. Preventing recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection is its established use (see 11.7 on the slide’s other conditions).
  • How it is given: by nasogastric tube, colonoscopy or enema, using screened donor stool.
  • Stool banks make it more accessible. Donor screening is similar to blood bank screening, to reduce the risk from blood-borne and other pathogens: human immunodeficiency virus, hepatitis A, B and C, syphilis, Clostridioides difficile, and the parasites Giardia and Cryptosporidium.
  • Veterinarians call it “transfaunation”, and it works for animals too.

11.5 · Objective 3 — Phage therapy

Bacteriophages (“phages”) are very small viruses that specifically attack bacteria and no other types of cells. Like all viruses they carry deoxyribonucleic acid or ribonucleic acid, and they replicate by a lytic or a lysogenic life cycle. For therapy you need lytic phages with deoxyribonucleic acid: phages that destroy the bacterium rather than settling into its genome. “The enemy of my enemy is my friend”: the bacteria’s own enemies, put to work for the patient.

Why it is an option now. Phages exhibit high host specificity, so they could attack highly antibiotic-resistant clinical strains. The idea is not new: it was explored in eastern Europe in the 1920s and 1930s and abandoned in the West with the emergence of antibiotic therapy (penicillin became clinically available in the 1940s). One obstacle today is translating the original publications, which are in non-English journals.

  • Wide distribution in the body after systemic administration.
  • Potential to reduce the inflammatory response.
  • Could eventually be more cost effective than antibiotics.
  • Greater efficacy: phages target specific bacterial cells and then die off when their host is no longer present.
  • Not a magic bullet: the optimal dose, route, frequency and duration still need to be precisely defined.
  • Clinicians must know the causative agent, because host specificity is so high; identifying it can be time-consuming and cost-prohibitive in resource-limited settings.
  • Gene transfer: what if the phage transfers deoxyribonucleic acid to its host cell?
  • Not covered by public health insurance in some countries (a few European countries are encouraging research).
  • No phage product is yet approved as a medicine (the slide: bacterial viruses “are not currently recognized as medicinal products”; in the United States the Food and Drug Administration handles them as investigational biologics), so regulatory clearance and unknown safety issues are concerns; investigational drug status and emergency use authorization are the useful routes around it.

Descriptive comes first. Before you undertake any analytic study you have to understand the descriptive components. The basic triad of descriptive epidemiology is person, place and time:

  • Person: age, gender, socio-economic status (education, occupation, income), marital status, ethnicity/race/genetic profile, behavior and habits.
  • Person characteristics. They include age, gender, socioeconomic status (education, occupation, income), marital status, ethnicity or genetic profile, and behavior.
  • Place: geographically restricted or widespread (outbreak, epidemic, pandemic)? off-shore (a tsunami)? climate effects (temperature, humidity); urban, suburban-squatter or rural; relation to environmental exposure (water, food supply); multiple clusters or one?
  • Time: changing or stable? clustered (epidemic) or evenly distributed (endemic)? Time trends: point source (localized and stationary), propagated (reflects a spreading infection; may be extended), seasonal, and secular (occurrence over a prolonged period, usually years).
  • Secular trend. A secular trend is the occurrence of disease over a prolonged period, usually years.

The basic triad of analytic epidemiology is the three phenomena it assesses: host, environment and agent.

AgentsHost factorsEnvironment
Biological (microorganisms); physical (temperature, radiation, trauma); chemical (acids, alkalis, poisons, tobacco); environmental (nutrients in the diet, allergens); psychological experiences Genetic endowment; immunologic status; personal characteristics; personal behavior; definitive versus intermediate host (in vector-borne diseases) Living conditions (housing, crowding, water supply, refuse, sewage); atmosphere and climate; modes of communication, the phenomena that bring host and agent together, such as a vector, vehicle or reservoir

John Snow’s cholera investigations show both kinds.

  • Descriptive (the outbreak in the Golden Square area of London): he marked where people with cholera lived and worked on a map, now called a spot map, and marked the water pumps. More cases were found near Pump A than near B or C; residents said they preferred Pump A; few cases occurred two blocks to the east, where a brewery with its own deep well let employees take their water (and a daily allotment of malt liquor). Households with cholera all drew from Pump A, the common factor. Officials were notified, the pump was closed and the outbreak ended.
  • Analytic (re-examining data from different outbreaks): the districts with the highest death rates were served by two water companies, Lambeth and Southwark and Vauxhall, both drawing from the Thames downstream from London. Lambeth then moved its intake upstream. Snow developed the hypothesis that water from below London was the source, and tested it by identifying the water source for every house with a cholera death. Houses supplied by Southwark and Vauxhall alone had a cholera death rate of 4.2 per 1,000 people; houses supplied by Lambeth alone, 0.5 per 1,000 (Table 1.2, slide 42).
  • The point: without any knowledge of microbes, he established that water could transmit cholera and that epidemiological information can direct prompt public health action. Koch’s postulates, which established cause and effect between microbes and disease, were published later (1884).

Noncommunicable disease uses the same descriptive groundwork (slide 70): define the population of interest; conceptualize and create measures of exposures and health indicators; take a sample of the population; estimate measures of association between exposures and health indicators; rigorously evaluate whether the association suggests a causal one; assess the evidence for causes working together; and assess whether the result is externally valid for other populations.

12.2 · Objective 2 — What epidemiology is, and the uses of epidemiologic training

The word: epi = upon, demos = people, ology = science: “the science which deals with what falls upon people.” It is a bridge between the biomedical, social and behavioral sciences.

The five W's. What is the health event or diagnosis; Who is the person or population; Where is the location; When is the time period; Why covers causes, risks and transmission.

Where epidemiology has impact (slide 6), each for its own reason: public health (the emphasis on disease prevention); clinical medicine (disease classification and diagnosis); pathophysiology (understanding basic biological mechanisms in disease); biostatistics (quantifying disease frequency and its relationships to antecedents); social sciences (the social context in which disease occurs and presents).

Uses of epidemiological training (slides 7–8):

  • Outbreak response: identifying, reporting and investigating outbreaks in a timely manner.
  • Public health surveillance: setting up, managing and evaluating surveillance systems.
  • Public health communication: developing and delivering it.
  • Disease prioritization: evaluating and prioritizing diseases of concern, including describing the clinical picture: who gets the disease, who dies from it, and what its outcome is.
  • Training and mentoring health professionals.
  • Experimental epidemiology: developing a hypothesis for a health concern and designing an experimental model to test it.
  • Program evaluation and management: evaluating prevention and intervention programs (for example vaccine and clinical trials) and managing their staff and resources.

Genomic epidemiology, one of the most important developments in the field, is the process of monitoring the genetic makeup of the pathogens that cause disease, made possible by advances in sequencing. It is one of the most reliable tools in outbreak investigation:

  • identifying or confirming sources of exposure;
  • understanding disease location and spatial distribution, to link cases or outbreaks in different locations and confirm more definitively that they are part of the same outbreak.

Incidence counts new cases during a period; prevalence counts all cases present. Prevalence rises with incidence and with how long the disease lasts.

Both are measures of the disease burden in a population. Morbidity is having a disease or a symptom of disease: the level of disease in a population, sometimes used to determine the risk of a disease. It is measured by incidence and prevalence. 12.8 · Objective 8 — Association and causation

★ Professor emphasized — “you should be able to tell me whether something is going to be more of an association or a causation”

Expect a described study to classify. A relationship between an exposure and a disease is an association; causation is inferred from association plus Hill’s criteria (also on her closing list).

  • Epidemiology does not determine the cause of a disease in a given individual. It determines the relationship, or association, between an exposure and the frequency of disease in populations. We infer causation from association and other factors.
  • Association: an identifiable relationship between an exposure and a disease. It implies that the exposure might cause the disease. Exposures associated with a difference in disease risk are often called risk factors. Most often, in public health, interventions are designed on the basis of associations.
  • Causation: implies a true mechanism leading from exposure to disease. Finding an association does not make it causal.

The pyramid of associations (slide 44), from its broad base to its narrow top: chance, spurious/artifact, confounded, non-causal, and at the apex causal.

Models of causation (slide 45): a cause is an event or condition that plays a role in producing an occurrence of a disease. It is not generally a single factor: most diseases come from an interplay of factors, from exposure plus additional factors to a whole web.

Hill’s criteria for causal inference (slides 46–52):

The worked outbreak: Legionnaires’ disease (slides 63–68). In late July 1976 an epidemic of fatal respiratory disease struck people attending an American Legion convention in Philadelphia, and some non-attendees, some within days of the convention. Doctors with the first cases tried to contact the state health department, but it was closed for the weekend, and deaths had already mounted by Monday; the CDC and the state health department then took on the investigation. Five months after the first cases, a case-control study showed that spending time in the lobby of Hotel A was a risk factor for illness, and in December 1976 a CDC laboratorian located the source bacteria by continuing to test specimens that had been thought to be dead. Then: implement control and prevention measures, and communicate the findings.

A public health approach (slide 71) runs from the problem to the response:

Policy development (slides 72–73). Rigorously conducted, reported and communicated epidemiological studies can produce major health policy changes. Policy is built on values, ideology, politics and evidence; evidence is the main contribution of epidemiology, and it is not usually the main influence on policy, but it is worth fighting for. The policy cycle, in order:

  1. Assessment of population health: identify the issue affecting population health.
  2. Assessment of potential interventions: identify interventions and provide evidence of their efficacy.
  3. Policy choices: made with tools such as computer modeling, then delivered for a consensus decision.
  4. Policy implementation: set target goals and allocate the resources.
  5. Policy evaluation: collect data on the results of the intervention, and plan future modeling.

When studies change policy. Epidemiological studies can bring about major health policy changes when they are rigorously conducted, reported and communicated.

Youth smoking (slide 74), the worked policy example. By the 1960s smoking had been linked to multiple health problems, and adult smoking fell by the 1980s, but youth smoking did not. The 1994 Surgeon General’s report recommended moving from campaigns aimed at individual young people to treating it as a community and population issue. The American Stop Smoking Intervention Study for Cancer Prevention used the epidemiological evidence that secondhand smoke is dangerous to reframe smoking as a societal rather than personal concern. The measures: eliminating tobacco smoke in public places, less advertising and promotion, less youth access to tobacco, and higher tobacco taxes.

Social and behavioral factors (slide 75) matter because they help explain how diseases spread and why health outcomes vary across populations. This work looks at how income inequality, poverty, race and discrimination affect health, including access to health care; at how behavioral risk factors contribute to disease; and it contributes to policy in these areas.

Environmental epidemiology (slide 76) studies how environmental factors affect human health: exposure to air and water pollution, or to hazardous waste disposal sites.

Occupational health epidemiology studies how workers’ health is affected by workplace conditions and exposure to hazardous substances: it monitors how often workers are injured or ill, identifies which groups of workers are most affected, determines the reasons, and uses the findings to reduce the risk through regulatory practice.

13 · Miscellaneous Bacterial Agents of Disease

Instructional Objectives

  1. Compare and contrast the morphology, medium, laboratory tests, biochemical reactions, diseases, clinical manifestations, methods of treatment, and prevention of miscellaneous bacterial agents of human diseases.
  2. Describe the modes of transmission by which humans acquire miscellaneous bacterial agents of human diseases.
Three odd shapes and three odd lifestyles. The spirochetes (Treponema, Leptospira, Borrelia) are Gram-negative spirals that swim with flagella inside the cell. The curviform Gram-negatives (Vibrio, Campylobacter, Helicobacter) are commas and curved rods of the gut. The unique morphologies are the obligate intracellular rickettsias and chlamydias and the wall-less mycoplasmas. The syllabus points to its Appendix 2 list for this lecture: Treponema pallidum and Borrelia; Rickettsia, Ehrlichia, Anaplasma, Chlamydia and Chlamydophila; Helicobacter; and the mycoplasmas. The deck adds Leptospira, the vibrios, Campylobacter, Coxiella, Bartonella and the bacteria of dental disease, and never names Anaplasma (13.17). One drug recurs: tetracycline, a very broad-spectrum antibiotic that targets the prokaryotic ribosome (30S subunit) (slide 53).

13.1 · Objective 1 — The spirochetes

  • Gram-negative and spiral shaped.
  • Motile by endoflagella, which are found inside the cell (in the periplasmic space, under the outer membrane).
  • Free-living saprobes or commensals of animals. Three genera: Treponema, Leptospira, Borrelia.
  • While most bacteria have circular chromosomes, these narrow cells may have linear double-stranded deoxyribonucleic acid and plasmids, especially Borrelia.

The clinical stages of untreated syphilis (slides 9–10):

  • Primary: a hard chancre at the site of inoculation; it often heals spontaneously, so the illness may go undiagnosed.
  • Secondary: an upper-respiratory-like illness with fever, headache and sore throat, and a red or brown rash on the skin, palms and soles; the rash often disappears spontaneously.
  • Tertiary: reached by about 30% of infections; can last 20 years or longer; numerous complications in susceptible tissues and organs: neurological and cardiovascular symptoms, and gummas. The Argyll Robertson pupil (an irregular pupil with eventual loss of the light reflex) is a tertiary sign.
  • Congenital: transplacental infection of the fetus causes post-natal nasal discharge (“snuffles”), skin eruptions, bone deformation (“Hutchinson’s teeth”) and nervous system abnormalities.

13.4 · Objective 1 — Leptospira and leptospirosis

  • Morphology: tight, regular individual coils with a bend or hook at one or both ends.
  • L. biflexa is a harmless, free-living saprobe; L. interrogans causes leptospirosis, a zoonosis transmitted mainly via mammal urine.
  • The bacteria are shed in urine and infection occurs mainly by contact with contaminated urine, so moisture is required. Those likely to be infected include surfers, water-skiers, veterinarians, farmers and slaughterhouse workers.
  • Targets: kidneys, liver, brain and eyes. Signs: sudden high fever, chills, headache, muscle aches, conjunctivitis and vomiting; long-term infection may affect the kidneys and liver.

13.8 · Objective 1 — Vibrios carried by seafood

Salt-tolerant inhabitants of coastal waters that associate with marine invertebrates.

  • Vibrio parahaemolyticus: gastroenteritis from raw seafood, with symptoms similar to cholera.
  • Seafood vibrios. They are salt-tolerant inhabitants of coastal waters that associate with marine invertebrates.
  • Vibrio vulnificus: gastroenteritis from raw oysters, with serious complications in people with diabetes or liver disease; one of the “flesh-eating” bacteria, and a problem with sewage overflow after hurricanes and other natural disasters.

13.10 · Objective 1 — Helicobacter pylori

  • Gram-negative, motile, curved cells with several polar flagella, discovered in stomach biopsies from ulcer patients by the Australian doctors Warren and Marshall (Nobel Prize in Medicine, 2005).

13.14 · Objective 1 — Mycoplasmas

  • The mollicutes and other cell-wall-deficient bacteria: they naturally lack cell walls and are highly pleomorphic (cell shapes vary widely).
  • They require special lipids from host membranes.
  • Treated with tetracycline or erythromycin. Not penicillin: no peptidoglycan is present for it to act on.
  • Mycoplasma pneumoniae: primary atypical pneumonia; the pathogen spreads slowly over the interior respiratory surfaces, causing fever, chest pain and sore throat.
  • M. genitalium and Ureaplasma urealyticum: weak sexually transmitted pathogens.

13.15 · Objective 1 — Bacteria in dental disease

  • The oral cavity is a complex, dynamic ecosystem of over 400 microbial species.
  • Dental caries (cavities) is a slow, progressive infection of irregular areas of the enamel surface, and the most commonly diagnosed of all human diseases.
  • It begins with colonization by slime-forming Streptococcus species, with cross-adherence of Actinomyces in the biofilm; Streptococcus mutans is commonly found. This forms plaque, a thick, adherent layer harboring masses of bacteria whose acid dissolves enamel; mature biofilms are polymicrobial.
  • Plaque and enamel. Plaque harbors masses of bacteria that produce acid, which dissolves the enamel, leading to dental caries.
  • Plaque left on and between teeth brings secondary invaders such as Lactobacillus, Bacteroides and Fusobacterium.
  • Periodontal disease, a soft-tissue disease: plaque calcified into calculus above and below the gingiva irritates it, causing gingivitis; the pockets between tooth and gingiva are invaded by spirochetes and Gram-negative bacilli; the tooth socket may become involved (periodontitis) and the tooth may be lost.

Two branches after plaque. One branch is acidification and cavitation leading to caries; the other is calculus (tartar) formation, leading to gingivitis, periodontal disease and bone resorption.
