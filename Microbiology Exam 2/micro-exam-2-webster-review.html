★ Professor emphasized

Her one-line version of each type, which is the way to carry them into the exam: type I is IgE on mast cells; type II is IgG or IgM plus complement punching holes in a cell (“Complement has the membrane attack complex that will then punch holes into membranes” [part 4, 1:48]); type III is antigen-antibody complexes lodging in basement membranes and setting up inflammation; type IV is “no antibodies, T cells” [part 4, 6:23]. The question to ask of any example is whether there is an antibody, and if so whether it binds a cell (II) or a soluble antigen (III).

Type I in more detail

Sensitizing dose: on first contact with the allergen, specific B cells form IgE, which attaches to mast cells and basophils; generally no signs or symptoms. Provocative dose: later exposure to the same allergen binds the IgE–mast cell complex (each cell can bind 10,000–40,000 IgE) and the mast cells degranulate, releasing inflammatory cytokines. General targets are the skin, upper respiratory tract, gastrointestinal tract and conjunctiva (rashes, itching, redness, rhinitis, sneezing, diarrhea, tears); systemic targets are smooth muscle, mucous glands and nervous tissue (vascular dilation and constriction changing blood pressure and respiration). Lecture 7, Slide 12.

The mediators drawn on the degranulation diagram are histamine, serotonin and bradykinin, prostaglandins and leukotrienes. Lecture 7, Slide 14. The wheal and flare skin reaction is cutaneous anaphylaxis and is used in diagnosis Lecture 7, Slide 20; systemic anaphylaxis is sudden respiratory and circulatory disruption that can be fatal in minutes Lecture 7, Slide 21. About six hours after the immediate reaction a late-phase reaction follows, mediated by synthesized products such as leukotrienes and drawing in eosinophils. Lecture 7, Slide 23.

Her aside, not on a slide. She named heparin with histamine and the prostaglandins, and gave a mosquito bite as a wheal-and-flare example [part 4, 0:51–1:05]. Neither is on a slide; the slide’s mediators are the five named above.

Type II, type III and type IV in more detail

Type II reactions lyse foreign cells: antibodies of the IgG or IgM class stimulate complement, leading to lysis. Examples: transfusion reactions (ABO blood groups; the Rh factor and hemolytic disease of the newborn) and some autoimmune diseases — autoimmune hemolytic anemia, and myasthenia gravis against acetylcholine receptors. Lecture 7, Slide 32.

Type III: antigen-antibody reaction of IgG and IgM (sometimes IgA, immunoglobulin A) with complement; the complexes deposit in the basement membranes of epithelial tissues, are trapped, and incite a damaging inflammatory response. It requires a large amount of antigen; symptoms are delayed by hours to days; joint, skin and kidney involvement are typical. Lecture 7, Slide 38. Examples: serum sickness (foreign animal proteins such as horse serum for tetanus, or animal hormones or drugs; complexes in the heart, kidneys, skin and joints); the Arthus reaction (localized dermal injury from inflamed blood vessels, a vasculitis, after a second injection of vaccine at a site; normally self-limiting); and autoimmune disorders — post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, systemic lupus erythematosus and rheumatoid arthritis. Lecture 7, Slide 39.

Type IV: delayed hypersensitivity, T cell mediated — a delayed response to antigen involving activation of, and damage by, T cells. Infectious allergy: tuberculosis, leprosy, syphilis, histoplasmosis, toxoplasmosis, candidiasis. Skin responses: the tuberculin skin test, and contact dermatitis from plants, metals and cosmetics. And graft rejection. Lecture 7, Slide 40. In contact dermatitis the first contact is the sensitizing dose; later contact is the reactive dose, with tissue damage from macrophage cytokines and cytotoxic T cells. Lecture 7, Slide 42.

Timing, and a word trap. The Lecture 7 slides give no hours for type IV. The hours are on the Lecture 10 tuberculosis slide: the Mantoux test is read for a red wheal (induration) at 48–72 hours Lecture 10, Slide 76. — which is what she said (“48 to 72 hours after you’re exposed” [part 4, 6:53]). Note that the slides also call type III symptoms “delayed — hours to days” Lecture 7, Slide 38; the name delayed hypersensitivity belongs to type IV, so do not let the word alone decide an answer.

Her aside, not on a slide. Her reason for the delay, “it takes 48 hours or so to develop the new effector T cells” [part 4, 6:45], is not on a slide.

One crossover worth knowing: allergic asthma starts as a type I response (crosslinking of IgE on mast cells by inhaled allergen) but leads to chronic asthma, which the slide labels type IV, mediated by cytokines and eosinophil granules. Lecture 7, Slide 19.

Desensitization — her “allergy shots”

Allergen-specific immunotherapy. The most common form is subcutaneous immunotherapy: small amounts of the allergen are injected, which may stimulate high levels of allergen-specific IgG that block the allergen from the IgE, so the mast cells do not degranulate. It improves reactions in about 80% of those who go through it. Lecture 7, Slide 29. The slide adds that “it’s not that simple”: regulatory T cells are activated that inhibit T cell activation of B cells and release cytokines that reduce inflammation, and mediators are induced that damp local allergic inflammation. Lecture 7, Slide 30. Sublingual immunotherapy puts the allergen under the tongue as a dissolvable tablet or liquid extract; it is used more outside the United States, though tablets are approved in the United States for pollen and dust mite allergens, and trials show it effective for peanut, kiwi, hazelnut, milk and peach allergies. Lecture 7, Slide 31.

Her words and the slide differ; the slide wins. She finished the point with “So you have more IgG defensive ones so that it can’t make the IgE that will cause the mast cells to rupture” [part 4, 4:47]. The slide’s mechanism is that the blocking IgG intercepts the allergen before it reaches the IgE already on the mast cells, so the mast cells do not degranulate. Lecture 7, Slide 29. Learn the slide’s version: the IgE is still there; the allergen never gets to it.

“You should know” is a direct instruction, and it was about compatibility. Both of her examples are type II reactions: antibody binding a cell and complement lysing it.

ABO — the rule the slide gives

  • Donors and recipients are matched for the A, B, O system of blood group antigens. There are no major histocompatibility complex molecules on red blood cells, so ABO is what is critical to match.
  • You carry antibodies in your serum against the ABO antigens you lack.
  • Crossmatching confirms compatibility. Transfusions are temporary.
  • ABO incompatibilities can be fatal, because of complement activation and cell lysis.

Lecture 7, Slide 33.

The cross-match test: the recipient’s serum is tested against the donor’s red cells. The reverse is not done, because even with whole blood there is not enough donor antibody to coat the recipient’s cells densely enough for a reaction. Lecture 7, Slide 34. What a reaction does: destruction of red cells, blockage of the glomeruli, fever and jaundice; and even if complement activation is incomplete, antibody-coated cells may attract natural killer cells or macrophages. Lecture 7, Slide 35.

The table below is that rule applied, nothing more: a donor’s red cells are incompatible when they carry an ABO antigen the recipient’s own cells lack, because the recipient’s serum has antibody against it. Rh and hemolytic disease of the newborn

  • The Rh antigen is present on Rh-positive red cells and not on Rh-negative cells. An Rh-negative person does not produce anti-Rh antibody unless sensitized — by a bad transfusion or an incompatible pregnancy.
  • Hemolytic disease of the newborn (erythroblastosis fetalis) occurs with an Rh-negative mother and an Rh-positive fetus.
  • The first pregnancy is normally fine — it is the sensitization. Subsequent pregnancies have the potential to be hemolytic.
  • Prevention is passive immunization with antibodies against the Rh antigen (Rhogam), which prevents sensitization of the mother.

Lecture 7, Slide 36. Rhogam is given at 26–28 weeks of pregnancy, within 72 hours after birth, and after prenatal invasive tests, abdominal injury or accidental exposure. Lecture 7, Slide 37.

Graft or transplantation rejection runs both ways: the host may reject the graft, and the graft may reject the host. It is associated with the major histocompatibility complex, and different grafts succeed differently because of compatibility. Autograft: a self graft (skin, gum, vein). What autoimmunity is: the immune system has lost tolerance to autoantigens and forms autoantibodies and sensitized T cells against them, destroying self tissue. It involves all hypersensitivity reactions except type I. There are genetic and gender predispositions: autoimmunities run in families, and women are more likely to have them than men. Disruption can be systemic or organ specific. Lecture 7, Slide 52.

How a cancer arises. Cancer cells appear to have genetic alterations that disrupt the normal cell division cycle. Possible causes: errors in mitosis, genetic damage, activation of oncogenes, or retroviruses. The result is new growth of abnormal cells; tumors may be benign (nonspreading, self-contained) or malignant (spreading from the tissue of origin to other sites). Immune surveillance keeps cancer “in check”. Lecture 7, Slide 66.

Transformation: a cancer arises from a single cell that has accumulated multiple mutations; oncogenes are activated by exposure to radiation, chemicals or oncogenic viruses. The slide’s diagram walks one tumor from normal epithelium through three lost tumor suppressor genes and one activated oncogene to an invasive tumor, rapid accumulation of mutations, and metastasis. The necessary characteristics of cancer cells: they stimulate their own growth; ignore growth-inhibiting signals; avoid death by apoptosis; develop a blood supply (angiogenesis); leave their site of origin to invade other tissues (metastasis); replicate constantly; and evade and outrun the immune response. Lecture 7, Slide 69.

Tumor antigens. Unique antigens develop during transformation — more than 1000 identified. Both provide targets for immune defense in a healthy person. Lecture 7, Slide 70.

How successful tumors evade the immune response: tumor cells can secrete TGF-β (transforming growth factor beta) to suppress the immune response and recruit regulatory T cells, which make more TGF-β and interleukin-10 and suppress the T cells specific for tumor antigens. The components

  • Person — age, gender, socioeconomic status (education, occupation, income), marital status, ethnicity, race or genetic profile, behavior and habits. Lecture 12, Slide 34.
  • Place — geographically restricted or widespread (outbreak, epidemic, pandemic); climate effects; urban, suburban or rural; relation to environmental exposure (water, food supply); one cluster or several. Lecture 12, Slide 35.

The worked example: John Snow and cholera

Descriptive: during the Golden Square outbreak in London he marked where cholera cases lived and worked on a map (now called a spot map) along with the water pumps, found more cases near Pump A, questioned residents about their pump, noted few cases near a brewery with its own deep well, and found the common factor was Pump A; the pump was closed and the outbreak ended. Lecture 12, Slide 41. Analytic: re-examining data from different outbreaks, he found the highest death rates in districts served by the Lambeth and Southwark/Vauxhall companies, both drawing water downstream of London; after Lambeth moved its intake upstream in 1852 he hypothesized that water from below London was the source, and tested it by identifying the water source of every house with a cholera death. Without any knowledge of microbes — Koch’s postulates came in 1884 — he established that water could transmit cholera. Lecture 12, Slide 42.

AssociationCausation
An identifiable relationship between an exposure and a disease. Most often, public health interventions are designed on associations Implies there is a true mechanism leading from exposure to disease. Finding an association does not make it causal
