Before you begin

Lecture 9 of Microbiology — Cocci of Medical Importance. Thirty questions, every one cited to the slide it came from. Two bench tests sort the Gram-positive cocci before anything else. Staphylococci grow in irregular clusters and are catalase positive; streptococci grow in chains and are catalase negative. Among the staphylococci, coagulase separates Staphylococcus aureus from the coagulase-negative species. Among the streptococci, hemolysis on blood agar comes next: beta is complete (a clear zone), alpha is partial (green, hence viridans). Bacitracin then picks out group A among the beta-hemolytic streptococci, and optochin picks out Streptococcus pneumoniae among the alpha-hemolytic ones. Lancefield groups A and B are the two to know. Group A, Streptococcus pyogenes, is strictly human, resists phagocytosis through M-protein, and leaves rheumatic fever and glomerulonephritis behind; group B, Streptococcus agalactiae, reaches the newborn during vaginal delivery, which is why pregnant women are screened. The Gram-negative cocci are Neisseria: bean-shaped diplococci, strict human parasites grown on chocolate agar in a carbon dioxide-enriched candle jar. The gonococcus is presumed from diplococci inside neutrophils; the meningococcus spreads in close quarters and kills through endotoxin. Covers every organism on the syllabus list, from Staphylococcus hominis to Acinetobacter baumannii. This set includes picture questions: Gram stains, blood agar plates and a skin photograph, each with its labels removed.
