The external and middle ear, the inner ear with hearing and balance, the nose and paranasal sinuses, neck masses and neoplasms, and the oral cavity, salivary glands and neck — 127 conditions. Lectures 18 and 19 were built from the slides alone, because neither has a recording; where the earlier three carry a point the lecturer made out loud, those two carry only what is on the slide. That is a difference in sourcing, not in coverage.

How to use this. Read it left to right for one condition: the words a question will use to hand it to you, how it presents and what you find on examination, what you order, what you give, how fast the patient has to be seen, and what you tell them. Read it top to bottom down one column to compare across conditions.

The three grey columns are this block’s discriminators. On the ophthalmology chart they were pain, laterality and the pupil. Ear disease does not separate on laterality — nearly everything here is one-sided — so the middle column is the type of hearing loss, and for the nasal rows it carries the discharge, which is that half's most useful sign. Conductive against sensorineural is the axis this whole block turns on: it is the first thing Lecture 16 asks you to distinguish, it is the entire reason Weber and Rinne exist, and it sorts almost every row on this page. The buttons above let you pull out just the conductive causes, or just the painful ones, and read down.

This is the shape of the exam question, stated in the lecture. Lecture 16 stopped on a worked vignette — ear fullness and reduced hearing after a cold, an amber effusion behind an intact drum with reduced mobility — and asked which Weber and Rinne findings are most likely, with the note: “this is what I expect you to get right on the test, because there is a test question like this.” The reverse was described too: given the Weber and Rinne results, name the diagnosis — “I can give you these results and you’ll automatically know if it’s conductive or sensorineural … I have things like wax impaction on there, and that would be the answer, because the other choices wouldn’t be conductive.” That is exactly why the hearing-loss column is the one to read down: in that question the wrong answers are eliminated by loss type before you think about anything else.

Weber and Rinne, once, so the middle column reads quickly. Conductive: Weber lateralises to the bad ear, and bone conduction is equal to or better than air conduction. Sensorineural: Weber lateralises away, to the good ear, and air conduction still beats bone — the same Rinne as a normal ear, which is why Weber is the one that makes the call.

The gold “Vignette giveaway” column is the one to scan when a stem is in front of you. A vignette gives itself away in a handful of words — pain on moving the tragus, hearing is better in background noise, pain out of proportion to the exam, “wet newspaper”, the pain stopped when it ruptured. Every phrase there is language the lecture decks themselves use.

The “How fast” column carries over from the ophthalmology chart, and four rows here earn EMERGENT: malignant otitis externa, carcinoma of the ear canal, sudden sensorineural hearing loss, and vertigo with brainstem signs. Three of those are conditions whose whole teaching point is that they get mistaken for something routine.

The two decks number their slides independently, so “slide 49” alone means nothing — and one row deliberately borrows the other lecture’s photograph, because barotrauma is taught in Lecture 15 but the only haemotympanum in either deck is on Lecture 16’s slide 59. Sixty-six rows have no picture, and that is not an oversight. Lecture 18 is the extreme case: it contains exactly one clinical photograph, the haemangioma on slide 27. Everything else in that deck is an anatomy diagram, a flowchart or a table — four of which are pictures of content with no text at all, and are transcribed into the rows rather than shown as though they were photographs of a disease. Lecture 16 teaches most of its sensorineural causes as histories and audiograms rather than photographs — there is nothing to photograph in ototoxicity, Ménière’s or vestibular neuronitis. The rows that carry a picture are the ones the decks actually picture. Product shots, instrument trays and stock cartoons were left out: a cell that shows a bottle of ear drops does not tell you what the disease looks like.
