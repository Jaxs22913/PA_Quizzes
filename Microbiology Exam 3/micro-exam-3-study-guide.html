14.1 · Objective 1 — Endotoxin, the shared weapon

Endotoxin is a structural component of the Gram-negative outer membrane. Chemically it is lipopolysaccharide; lipid A is the biologically active component of the complex, the part most responsible for pathogenesis. It is released on the lysis of Gram-negative cells, including through antibiotic therapy, and as bacterial cells grow and divide, particularly when they are rapidly proliferating.

Part of the lipopolysaccharideWhat it is
Lipid AThe lipid region to which the polysaccharide is bound; the biologically active component
Core polysaccharideIdentical for large groups of bacteria, with some unique sugars
O antigen polysaccharideUnique for each organism; repeating units of 3–4 different hexoses. The number of repeating units designates the antigen as rough, semi-rough or smooth

What endotoxin does

  • Usually protective. In the gut and airways the first contact between epithelium and Gram-negative bacteria is the outer membrane and its lipopolysaccharide. These are antigens of the innate (nonspecific) immune system, signaling through a lipopolysaccharide binding complex and a Toll-like receptor. Under most circumstances this gives a moderated, protective inflammatory response, which keeps most mucosal infections mucosal and helps maintain our symbiosis with some Gram-negative bacilli.
  • Chronic infection. Some bacteria have adapted by modifying their lipopolysaccharide to evade the immune system.
  • Potentially damaging, through complement, coagulation and a variety of cells:
    • Complement turns on by the classical pathway (antigen-antibody reaction) or the alternative pathway, and can result in hemorrhagic necrosis.
    • Coagulation: tissue injury through coagulation changes, disseminated intravascular coagulation; fibrin deposited in the lungs, liver and spleen; it may trigger bradykinin, a vasodilator in shock.
    • Platelets: altered activity causing aggregation (differs among species). Neutrophils: low levels may enhance phagocytosis, but higher levels may interfere with chemotaxis to the site of infection. Macrophages release pro-coagulants, collagenase, pyrogens, prostaglandins, colony stimulating factor, tumor necrosis factor, interleukin 1 and interleukin 6, nitric oxide and reactive oxygen species.
Low dose against flood. Low levels of endotoxin give a low-level inflammatory response: fever, malaise, aches and pains. A large flood releases the mediators and contributes to septic shock: tumor necrosis factor and interleukin 1 make neutrophils stick to endothelium and activate clotting, forming microthrombi throughout the body; arteries and arterioles dilate, peripheral arterial resistance falls and cardiac output at first rises (warm shock); later cardiac output falls and blood pressure drops, leading to tissue hypoxia, infarction and multisystem organ failure.

14.2 · Objective 1 — Sorting the Gram-negative bacilli

The Gram-negative bacilli are differentiated by three properties: oxygen requirement (aerobic, facultatively anaerobic, obligate anaerobic), the ability to ferment lactose, and the oxidase enzyme.

Aerobic Gram-negative nonenteric bacilli are a large, diverse group of non-spore-forming bacteria, from habitats as varied as the large intestine, animals, the respiratory tract, soil and water. Most are not medically important; some are true pathogens and some opportunists. All use endotoxin as one of their offensive weapons. (The third branch of the scheme, the obligate anaerobes, is Bacteroides.) The medically important aerobic genera fall in three pairs:

Pseudomonas and BurkholderiaOpportunistic pathogens
Brucella and FrancisellaZoonotic pathogens
Bordetella and LegionellaMainly human pathogens

14.3 · Objective 1 — Pseudomonas aeruginosa

HeadingThe pseudomonads, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa
MorphologySmall Gram-negative rods with a single polar flagellum
HabitatFree living, primarily in soil, sea water and fresh water; also colonize plants and animals. Important decomposers and bioremediators (exoenzymes such as protease, amylase, pectinase and cellulase; oil-eating and pesticide-clearing strains); frequent contaminants in homes and clinical settings; food spoilage (dairy, meat, eggs). Identifying signsGrapelike odor; greenish-blue pigment, pyocyanin
HardinessResistant to soaps, dyes, quaternary ammonium disinfectants, drugs and drying; survives in nutrient-poor environments; a major agent of nosocomial infections
Why it contaminates equipmentFrequent contaminant of ventilators, intravenous solutions and anesthesia equipment because it produces biofilms

Virulence and resistance

  • Exotoxin A inhibits protein synthesis.
  • Slime layer: resistance to phagocytosis and biofilm formation. Cells in biofilms adhere by flagella and pili and form microcolonies; three polysaccharides stabilize the biofilm and impair bacterial clearance.
  • Exoenzymes: exoenzyme S induces apoptosis of T cells; others degrade phospholipid.
  • Multidrug resistant, through restricted outer membrane permeability, which limits drug entry, antibiotic-inactivating enzymes, efflux pumps, horizontal transfer of resistance genes and biofilm formation. The slide notes it is one of the top three critical pathogens the World Health Organization prioritized for research into new treatments (with Acinetobacter baumannii and Escherichia coli; see 14.21).

Disease

  • A common cause of nosocomial infection in hosts with burns, neoplastic disease and cystic fibrosis (the slide stars cystic fibrosis with three asterisks). Complications include pneumonia, urinary tract infection, abscesses, otitis and corneal disease; septicemia leads to endocarditis, meningitis and bronchopneumonia.
  • Typical infections in healthy people: skin rashes, urinary tract infection, otitis externa (swimmer’s ear), and corneal ulcers in contact lens wearers.

Treatment and prevention. The antibiotics listed are cephalosporins, aminoglycosides, carbenicillin, polymyxin, quinolones and monobactams. A vaccine is in development, particularly for cystic fibrosis patients: most candidates rely on antibodies against bacterial protein; one uses the PopB protein to stimulate T helper 17 cells, which release interleukin 17 and enhance antibacterial mucosal defenses; one in current testing uses the porin membrane protein OprH and has worked against at least two strains. But in cystic fibrosis, where the infection is often chronic, vaccination may induce increased pulmonary inflammation and tissue damage: it may worsen lung inflammation. Mucosal delivery by liposomes and nanoparticles is being investigated.

HeadingBordetella pertussis

DiseasePertussis, whooping cough: a communicable childhood acute respiratory syndrome, often with severe, life-threatening complications in babies; about half of cases are in children from birth to 4 years

Why pertussis is rising again. Case charts from 1922 to 2026 show the fall after the whole-cell vaccine and a rise since the acellular vaccine. The deck gives three threads:
  • Infants under 2 months are too young to be vaccinated (they cannot get the vaccine), so incidence is highest under 1 year old (in Florida in 2023, over half of reported cases were not up to date on vaccination).
  • The age pattern in older children and adolescents reflects waning immunity from childhood vaccination and the need for booster doses.
  • “Another possible connection”: pertactin-negative strains are now developing. Pertactin is the protein that helps the bacterium adhere to airway linings and a vaccine component, and these strains lack it.

14.8 · Objective 1 — Alcaligenes

An aerobe of the non-fermenting branch (14.2), not on the syllabus list. Lives primarily in soil and water and may become normal flora. Alcaligenes faecalis is the most common clinical species, isolated from feces, sputum and urine; it is occasionally associated with opportunistic pneumonia, septicemia and meningitis.

14.10 · Objective 1 — The Enterobacteriaceae: traits, media, antigens

The enterics are a large family of small, non-spore-forming Gram-negative rods. Many live in soil, water and decaying matter and are common occupants of the large bowel of animals, including humans. They are the most frequent cause of diarrheal disease, by enterotoxins or by invasive infection, and with Pseudomonas they account for almost half of nosocomial infections; they have been isolated from soap dishes, sinks, catheters and tracheal cannulas.

Antigens and virulence factors

Complex surface antigens contribute to pathogenicity and trigger the immune response:

  • H — the flagellar antigen.
  • K — the capsule and/or fimbrial antigen (Vi in Salmonella).
  • O — the somatic or cell wall antigen, which all have.

Other virulence factors: endotoxin, exotoxins, capsules, fimbriae, attachment receptors for host cells, and the ability to transmit virulence genes (toxins, capsules, hemolysins, fimbriae).

14.11 · Objective 1 — Escherichia coli and its four pathotypes

Escherichia coli is the common aerobic, non-fastidious bacterium of the gut, but not the most prevalent: the anaerobic Bacteroides and Bifidobacterium are. Of about 150 strains, some have developed virulence through plasmid transfer; others are opportunists.

15.1 · Objective 1 — True and opportunistic fungal pathogens

Molds and yeasts are widely distributed in air, dust, fomites and the normal flora of humans, plants and animals. Humans are relatively resistant to fungal infections as long as the immune system is competent; immunocompromised status is the serious consideration throughout this lecture. Fungi are relatively nonpathogenic: of about 100,000 fungal species only about 300 (0.3%) have been linked to disease in humans and animals. But fungi are the most common plant pathogens (potato blights, infected grape crops and fruit trees), which can cause huge socio-economic losses such as the potato famines in Ireland and Eastern Europe. Human mycoses (“myco” = fungi) are caused by true fungal pathogens and by opportunistic pathogens.

15.2 · Objective 1 — Thermal dimorphism

The most striking adaptation of a true pathogen to survival and growth in the human host is the ability to switch from hyphal cells to yeast cells, from multicellular to unicellular forms, with the corresponding change in temperature. 15.4 · Objective 1 — Epidemiology, pathogenesis and host defense

  • Most fungal pathogens do not require a host to complete their life cycles, and these infections are generally not communicable to other persons.
  • The exceptions: dermatophytes (literally “skin plants”) and Candida species naturally inhabit the human body and are transmissible from person to person, or by fomites such as shared exercise equipment.
  • True fungal pathogens are distributed in predictable geographical patterns, coinciding with climate and soil requirements (the map in 15.7).
  • Dermatophytoses are the most prevalent fungal diseases, and many cases go undiagnosed or are misdiagnosed (for example as skin-related allergies or autoimmune disorders).

The main antifungal defenses are the integrity of the skin, mucous membranes and intact respiratory cilia. 15.5 · Objective 1 — Diagnosis

Diagnosis and identification need microscopic examination of stained specimens, culture on selective and enriched media, and specific biochemical or serological tests. Sabouraud dextrose agar is commonly used, with chemicals added to inhibit bacterial growth; its fairly low pH inhibits most bacteria, but not fungi.

Hair, skin and nail specimens. The identification pathway applies examination under Wood's light directly to the hairs, alongside a hot potassium hydroxide wet mount, brighteners and periodic acid-Schiff staining.

Germ tube test. Some yeasts, when incubated in serum for 2 to 4 hours, sprout tiny hyphal tubes called germ tubes; the germ tube test sits among the identification tests for isolates.

15.8 · Objective 1 — Histoplasma capsulatum: histoplasmosis, “Ohio Valley fever”

  • The most common true fungal pathogen; causes histoplasmosis. Thermally dimorphic (the two colonies in 15.2).
  • “Ohio Valley fever” is histoplasmosis, caused by Histoplasma capsulatum, the most common true fungal pathogen.
  • Distributed worldwide; known in the eastern and central regions of the United States, and now recognized as endemic in parts of Latin America and Africa.
  • Grows in moist soil with high nitrogen content, especially soil containing bird and bat guano.
  • Not caught from another person. Histoplasmosis is not transmitted person to person, so a spouse cannot catch it from him; people acquire it by inhaling conidia from moist soil enriched with bird or bat guano.
  • Inhaled conidia produce a primary pulmonary infection that may progress to systemic involvement of a variety of organs, the central nervous system, and chronic lung disease. It is NOT transmitted person to person.
  • Treatment: amphotericin B and azoles are common options.

15.9 · Objective 1 — Coccidioides immitis: coccidioidomycosis, “Valley fever”

  • Mainly in the southwestern United States, extending to Mexico and Central and South America. Exposure comes with farming, road construction and repair, archaeological digs, landslides and dust storms. It remains dormant in winter and spring.
  • Significant risk factors: people of African or African-American descent, increasing age (over 40–60 years), and environmental or occupational exposure to dust and soil.
  • Distinctive morphology: block-like arthroconidia in the free-living stage, and spherules containing endospores in the lungs.
  • Lives in alkaline, salty soils in semiarid, hot climates; its spread is greatly enhanced by cycles of drought and heavy rain, followed by windstorms.
  • Arthrospores inhaled from dust create spherules and can form nodules in the lungs.
  • Prevention: oiling roads, planting to minimize erosion, and dust masks to reduce inhalation of spores.

Bread making

Microbes have three main functions: (1) leavening flour-based dough (mainly carbon dioxide and other gases); (2) imparting flavor and aroma (organic acids and alcohols); (3) conditioning the dough to make it pliable and workable (microbial enzymes act on gluten to give elasticity). Beer, wine and other fermented products

  • Glucose + yeast → 2 ethanol + 2 carbon dioxide (C6H12O6 → 2C2H5OH + 2CO2). Evidence of beer brewing since 6000 BCE in Sumeria, Egypt and Babylon. Steps: malting of the starch source, mashing, wort plus hops, lagering, filtration.
  • Wine usually starts with grapes, but other fruit and plant material can be used (apples, oranges, pineapples, peaches, plums).
  • Sometimes the source of yeast for an alcoholic beverage is human saliva: chewing (mastication) of the plant material precedes the brewing.
  • Pickling: salt brine preserves vegetable material; lactic acid may also be present, with vinegar and spices. Cucumbers, cabbage, olives, okra, onions, ginger root and many more can be pickled.
  • Soybeans and their extracts are fermented into tofu and soy sauce (shoyu); also fermented fish sauce, mole (chocolate), tea and coffee.

  • Sporadic outbreaks originate from fecally contaminated meats or vegetables (the Jack in the Box hamburgers, bagged spinach). One of the worst culprits is the O157:H7 serotype.
  • The outbreak and recall list (slide 16): hamburgers (1993), California bagged spinach (2006), refrigerated cookie dough (2009), romaine lettuce (2010, 2018 from Arizona, 2020–2021 from California), 7% of fresh spinach in grocery stores carrying E. coli (a 2013 report), beef products (2019), ground beef from packing companies (2023–2025), and raw milk cheese in the western United States (September–October 2026).

16.6 · Objective 2 — Salmonella

  • The main species are Salmonella typhi, S. enterica, S. enteritidis and S. typhimurium; others are named for where they were first found (S. miami, S. heidelberg, S. sandiego).
  • Associated with many animals, mainly poultry and aquatic reptiles.
  • Salmonellosis can appear as gastroenteritis, enteric fever, septicemia (blood infection), fever, shock and kidney dysfunction. Incubation can be as short as hours or as long as weeks.
  • Young children and the elderly are at greatest risk.
  • Elderly people, on both risk lists. Young children and the elderly are at greatest risk for salmonellosis, and elderly people are also among those at greatest risk for listeriosis, so the elderly are the group on both lists.
  • Typhoid fever (S. typhi) is the most severe enteric fever: the intestinal wall is invaded and the bacteria can spread to the blood and lymph nodes.
  • Typhoid fever, caused by Salmonella typhi, is the most severe enteric fever, marked by invasion of the intestinal (gut) wall, with possible spread to the blood and lymph nodes.

Sources (slide 18): poultry products (chicken, turkey, duck), eggs, pet food, live poultry (such as backyard hens), peanut butter and sushi-grade seafood. 16.7 · Objective 2 — Campylobacter jejuni

  • A leading cause of bacterial diarrheal disease each year in the United States, especially in children.
  • Fever, headache, dizziness and bloody diarrhea, usually rapid in onset.
  • The infectious dose is less than 500 bacterial cells, so even one drop of raw poultry fluid can cause illness.
  • Poultry, including its leftovers. Even one drop of raw poultry fluid can cause Campylobacter jejuni illness, so holiday poultry and its leftovers deserve particular care.
  • Very much under-reported. Be careful with Thanksgiving poultry and leftovers.

The Campylobacter jejuni transmission cycle reaches people by three routes: poultry meat, contaminated water, and milk from cattle.

16.8 · Objective 2 — Listeria monocytogenes

  • Found in birds and mammals, and can persist in soil.
  • Large-scale food recalls: the big ice cream recall of 2016 (Blue Bell, slide 22), cantaloupes, frozen vegetables, poultry (2019).
  • Greatest risk of listeriosis: pregnant women, unborn children, premature babies, elderly people and the immunocompromised.
  • The slide calls it “the #1 cause of food-related deaths in the US” (see 16.17).
  • Number one cause of food-related deaths. Listeria monocytogenes is the number one cause of food-related deaths in the United States.
  • Most vulnerable foods: soft cheeses, raw fruit and vegetables, raw or undercooked meats, deli meats, seafood, smoked seafood, hot dogs, preserved meats.
  • Probably the biggest concern: it grows well from 1 to 40 °C, easily at refrigerator, room or freezer temperatures (the slide’s words; in the recording, it survives in the freezer: see 16.17).

17.3 · Objective 1 — Entamoeba histolytica and amebiasis

Slide 15 opens the infective amebas: Entamoeba histolytica (this section), Naegleria fowleri (17.4) and Acanthamoeba (17.5).

Morphology and hosts

  • Entamoeba histolytica alternates between a large trophozoite, motile by means of pseudopods, and a smaller nonmotile cyst.
  • The trophozoite has a large nucleus and lacks most other organelles.
  • The name: histolytica = histo, tissue + lytica, lysis (slide 19).
  • Humans are the primary hosts. It is ingested.
  • It is carried by 10% of the world population, asymptomatically, and kills 100,000 people per year.

How people acquire it, and where

  • By the fecal oral route (her table, slide 4): cysts are swallowed. The reservoir and source is human, water and food (slide 13).
  • Prevalent in tropical countries; related to fertilization practices and sewage treatment.
  • Epidemics sometimes occur in concentrated populations where water supplies are polluted: prisons, hospitals, juvenile care institutions.
  • Sometimes sexually transmitted.

Life cycle (slides 18–19)

Cysts are swallowed and arrive at the small intestine; the alkaline pH and digestive juices stimulate the cysts to release 4 trophozoites. The trophozoites attach, multiply, actively move about and feed. Cysts are produced after the active phase of disease (slide 23) and leave in the feces.

Disease and clinical manifestations

Asymptomatic in 90% of patients; severity is related to the strain, the amount of inoculum and host conditions. Amebiasis takes two forms, intestinal and extraintestinal (her table names the disease “amebiasis—amebic dysentery”).

17.6 · Objective 1 — Balantidium coli, the intestinal ciliate

  • An occupant of the intestines of domestic animals such as pigs and cattle (slide 13: “zoonotic in pigs”; her table: pigs and cattle as alternate hosts).

17.8 · Objective 1 — Giardia lamblia and giardiasis

Morphology (slide 39)

  • A pathogenic flagellate with a unique symmetrical heart shape and a concave ventral surface that acts like a suction cup.
  • Cysts are small, compact and multinucleate.

Reservoirs and how people acquire it

  • Reservoirs include beavers, cattle, coyotes, cats and humans (slide 13: zoonotic, water and food).
  • Cysts can survive for 2 months in the environment.
  • Usually ingested with water and food; the infectious dose is 10 to 100 cysts. Her table: common in campers. ★ Dr. Webster: “this is the one that you will get going camping”.

Disease, diagnosis, treatment, prevention (slide 41)

  • Cysts enter the duodenum, germinate, and travel to the jejunum to feed and multiply.
  • Giardiasis: diarrhea, abdominal pain.
  • Diagnosis: microscopic diagnosis is difficult because the organism is shed in feces intermittently; polymerase chain reaction analysis.
  • Treatment: quinacrine or metronidazole, or nitazoxanide in children.
  • The agent is killed by boiling, ozone and iodine.

17.11 · Objective 1 — Trypanosoma cruzi and Chagas disease

  • T. cruzi causes Chagas disease, or American trypanosomiasis.
  • Millions of cases in Latin America, but it is becoming more of an issue in the Southern United States: vector migration ★ Starred on the slide. The vector and how people acquire it (slide 49)

  • The reduviid bug (kissing bug) is the vector.

  • A local lesion: Romaña’s sign, also called a chagoma, a swelling of the eyelid with conjunctivitis; with fever and swelling of the lymph nodes, spleen and liver.

  • Chronic inflammation occurs in the organs, especially the heart and brain; Chagas accounts for 30–40% of heart disease in endemic regions.

17.13 · Objective 1 — Plasmodium and malaria

The apicomplexan parasites (slide 55)

  • Sporozoans that lack locomotor organelles in the trophozoite state.
  • They alternate between sexual and asexual phases and between different animal hosts.
  • Most form specialized infective bodies that are transmitted by arthropod vectors, food, water or other means: Plasmodium, Toxoplasma, Cryptosporidium.

Plasmodium, the agent of malaria (slides 56–57)

  • Malaria is the dominant protozoan disease: it threatens 40% of the world’s population each year.
  • Plasmodium is an obligate intracellular sporozoan.
  • Four species (slide 56): P. malariae, P. vivax, P. falciparum and P. ovale (slide 13 lists only the first three; 17.42).
  • The female Anopheles mosquito is the primary vector; malaria also spreads by blood transfusions and mother to fetus.
  • 300–500 million new cases each year (90% in Africa); 2 million deaths each year; 1,000–2,000 cases a year in the United States.

  • Some populations are naturally resistant: sickle cell trait.
  • Increasing drug resistance.

  • Mosquito control is key. ★ Dr. Webster: “once again, vector control is what they do”.

17.14 · Objective 1 — Toxoplasma gondii and toxoplasmosis

The coccidian parasites (slide 63)

Coccidians are zoonotic in domestic animals and birds and exist in stages including dormant oocysts and pseudocysts. The examples: Toxoplasma (this section), Sarcocystis (17.15), Cryptosporidium (17.16), Isospora (17.17), Cyclospora (17.18) and Babesia (17.19); the end of 17.19 sets them side by side.

The organism and how people acquire it (slides 64–65)

  • An intracellular apicomplexan parasite with an extensive distribution, capable of infecting virtually all warm-blooded animals.
  • Seroprevalence indicates that approximately one third of the human population worldwide has been exposed.
  • It lives naturally in cats, which harbor oocysts in the gastrointestinal tract, released through feces; slide 70 calls the cat the feline definitive host.
  • Acquired by ingesting raw meats or substances contaminated by cat feces.
  • Transplacental transmission to the fetus when a pregnant woman has toxoplasmosis: 33% chance of transmission. Her table: pregnant women should not clean the cat box because of the danger of transplacental transmission. ★ Dr. Webster: “if you are with a patient who is pregnant and she has a cat, you tell her, get somebody else to clean the litter box”.

Disease

  • Most cases go unnoticed, with mild symptoms: sore throat, fever, enlarged lymph nodes.
  • Serious complications in immunocompromised individuals, who can suffer brain and heart damage: toxoplasma encephalitis (her table: danger of central nervous system involvement for patients with acquired immunodeficiency syndrome).
  • Congenital: spontaneous abortion, stillbirth, or a spectrum of severe neurological and ocular manifestations in the newborn, including hydrocephalus, intracranial calcifications and chorioretinitis.

Diagnosis, treatment and vaccines (slide 65)

  • Diagnosed by serological tests, culture, histological analysis.
  • Treatment: pyrimethamine and sulfadiazine, long-term administration (Table 23.2: together they inhibit enzymes in the pathway for folate synthesis).
  • Vaccine development is in progress, but hampered by the genetic diversity of T. gondii strains, the complexity of a life cycle with many different stages, and the need to develop a robust and balanced TH1 (T helper 1) cellular immune response. A promising candidate is a genetically engineered live attenuated strain with a crucial transcription factor blocked.

Toxoplasmosis and mental disorders (slides 68 and 70)

  • Studies link cat ownership in childhood with later development of mental illness.
  • Acute toxoplasmosis can cause delusions and hallucinations; latent toxoplasmosis is a risk factor for schizophrenia, anxiety, and possibly obsessive compulsive disorder and addiction; chronic toxoplasmosis is linked to schizophrenia, mood disorders, psychosis and self-directed violence.
  • One Dutch study showed twice the risk of developing schizophrenia if infected with Toxoplasma.
  • Humans may not be the only animal whose mental state is affected: in hyena and lion observation studies, Toxoplasma-infected hyena cubs are bolder and more likely to get close to lions, and have a higher mortality rate. The hypothesis: boldness in hyena young furthers the distribution of Toxoplasma into the feline definitive host.

17.15 · Objective 1 — Sarcocystis and sarcocystosis

  • Sarcocystis species are parasites of cattle, swine and sheep.
  • Two species infect humans: S. hominis (from cattle) and S. suihominis (from pigs) (her memory aid: “that sui tells you the pig”). Humans can be dead-end hosts for other species.
  • Domestic animals are intermediate hosts: they pick up infective cysts while grazing on grass contaminated with human feces.
  • Humans are infected when the undercooked meat is consumed.
  • Symptoms: diarrhea, nausea and abdominal pain with S. hominis and S. suihominis; but muscle cysts, myalgia, weakness and edema from other species.
  • Albendazole and corticosteroids are used to treat the muscular sarcocystosis.

17.16 · Objective 1 — Cryptosporidium: a newly recognized intestinal pathogen

  • An intestinal pathogen that infects a variety of mammals, birds and reptiles (slide 13: free-living, water and food).
  • It exists in tissue and oocyst phases.
  • An infected person or animal can shed 10–100 million parasites in a single bowel movement; the infectious dose is as little as 10 organisms.

How people acquire it

  • Most often by consuming water in recreational settings, or consuming contaminated water, ice or foodstuffs; it is the leading cause of recreational waterborne disease. ★ Dr. Webster: “this we’re starting to hear more and more and more about”.
  • The organism can tolerate chlorine in swimming pools.
  • In the 1990s, 370,000 cases in Milwaukee, Wisconsin, from contaminated water; filtration is required for removal (her table: the major outbreak in Wisconsin from unpurified water).

Disease (slide 73)

  • Ingestion of oocysts gives rise to sporozoites that penetrate intestinal cells.
  • It causes gastroenteritis, headache, sweating, vomiting, abdominal cramps and diarrhea.
  • Patients with acquired immunodeficiency syndrome and other immunocompromised people may suffer chronic persistent diarrhea; young children and the elderly are likely to be infected.
  • Few effective drugs; it is considered a potential agent for bioterrorism.
  • The largest hidden burden among United States diarrheal parasites: an estimated 748,000 a year, with fewer than 2% verified in surveillance data.

17.17 · Objective 1 — Isospora belli and coccidiosis

  • An intracellular intestinal parasite with an oocyst stage. Slide 13: isosporosis, from dogs and other mammals.
  • Transmitted in fecally contaminated food or drink.
  • Infection is usually asymptomatic or self-limited.
  • Symptoms: malaise, nausea and vomiting, diarrhea, fatty stools, abdominal cramping and weight loss.
  • Primarily associated with immunocompromised hosts.
  • Treat with sulfadiazine and pyrimethamine, when required.

17.18 · Objective 1 — Cyclospora cayetanensis and cyclosporiasis

17.20 · Objective 1 — Helminths: biology, hosts and the four life cycles

17.22 · Objective 1 — Nematodes and Ascaris lumbricoides

Nematodes (roundworms), slide 89

One of the most abundant animal groups; 50 species affect humans. Elongated, cylindrical worms with protective cuticles, circular muscles, a complete digestive tract, and separate sexes. They are divided into intestinal nematodes (17.22–17.28) and tissue nematodes (17.29–17.31).

Ascaris lumbricoides (ascariasis), slides 90–92

  • Morphology: a large intestinal roundworm (the common name on slides 6 and 88: roundworm).
  • Where and hosts: indigenous to humans; it spends its larval and adult stages in humans. Most cases in the United States occur in the southeastern states.
  • How people acquire it (Life Cycle A): embryonic eggs are released in feces and spread to other humans through food, drink, or contaminated objects (Table 23.4: fecal pollution of soil with eggs). Ingested eggs hatch into larvae, burrow through the intestine into the circulation, travel to the lungs and pharynx, and are swallowed. Adult worms complete the cycle in the intestine and reproduce: 200,000 eggs a day.
  • Disease: the worms retain motility and do not attach. Severe inflammatory reactions mark the migratory route, and allergic reactions can occur. Heavy worm loads can cause abdominal pain and intestinal obstruction. Lung migration may cause pulmonary symptoms: eosinophilic pneumonitis. It may retard physical and mental development.
  • Diagnosis: microscopic or macroscopic analysis.
  • Treatment: albendazole with mebendazole, ivermectin, and nitazoxanide, for 1–3 days (slide 92). Table 23.5 lists albendazole, ivermectin, mebendazole and piperazine for ascariasis.

17.23 · Objective 1 — Trichuris trichiura (whipworm)

  • Common name: whipworm (trichuriasis).
  • Hosts and where: humans are the sole host. Trichuriasis has its highest incidence in the tropics; in the United States, in the South.
  • How people acquire it (Life Cycle A): eggs (Table 23.4: fecal pollution of soil with eggs) are swallowed and hatch in the intestines; the larvae attach, penetrate the outer wall and develop into adults. Females lay 3,000–5,000 eggs daily.
  • Disease: worms can pierce capillaries and cause localized hemorrhage. Heavy infestations can cause dysentery and rectal prolapse, and can be fatal in children.
  • Treatment: mebendazole is the drug of choice, with albendazole as an alternative (Table 23.5: mebendazole for trichuriasis).

17.24 · Objective 1 — Enterobius vermicularis (pinworm)

  • Common names: pinworm or seatworm (enterobiasis). Enterobiasis is the most common worm disease of children in temperate zones. ★ Starred on the slide. 17.25 · Objective 1 — Hookworms: Necator americanus and Ancylostoma duodenale

  • Morphology: characteristic curved ends and hooked mouths. Two species: Necator americanus (the New World hookworm) and Ancylostoma duodenale (the Old World hookworm), Table 23.4.
  • How people acquire it (Life Cycle B): humans shed eggs in feces, which hatch into filariform larvae that burrow into the skin of bare feet (slide 6: “transmitted through skin on soles of feet”). The larvae travel from the blood to the lungs, proceed up the bronchi and throat and are swallowed; the worms mature and reproduce in the small intestine and complete the cycle.
  • Disease: may cause pneumonia, nausea, vomiting, cramps, and bloody diarrhea. Blood loss is significant: anemia.
  • Treatment: albendazole, mebendazole or pyrantel pamoate (Table 23.5 also lists piperazine and thiabendazole for hookworm).

17.26 · Objective 1 — Angiostrongylus cantonensis (rat lungworm)

  • What it is: the rat lungworm, a nematode that causes eosinophilic meningitis. Snails are intermediate hosts.
  • How people acquire it: by ingesting contaminated snails, water, or vegetables (slide 7: “acquired by eating larvae in snails or contaminated water or vegetables”).
  • Disease: incubation period 1 day to 6 weeks. Symptoms of bacterial meningitis: nausea, vomiting, headaches, stiff neck.
  • Diagnosis: lumbar puncture shows elevated eosinophils instead of neutrophils.
  • Outcome: some patients recover on their own, because the parasite does not survive long in the human body; others experience coma and death. The slides name no treatment.
  • Prevention: snail and slug control and washing contaminated fruits and vegetables.

17.27 · Objective 1 — Strongyloides stercoralis (threadworm)

  • Morphology and where: the threadworm: tiny roundworms that complete the life cycle in humans or in moist soil (Table 23.4: humans; may live free).
  • How people acquire it (Life Cycle B): larvae penetrate the skin, migrate to the lungs, are swallowed, and complete development in the intestine. It can reinfect the same host without leaving the body.
  • Disease: heavy worm loads can cause pneumonitis and eosinophilia, bloody diarrhea, liver enlargement, bowel obstruction, and malabsorption.
  • Treatment: ivermectin, with albendazole as the alternative (Table 23.5: ivermectin and thiabendazole for strongyloidiasis).

17.28 · Objective 1 — Trichinella spiralis and trichinellosis

  • Names: trichinellosis; slide 7 and Table 23.5 say trichinosis, Table 23.4 the trichina worm.
  • Hosts: the life cycle is entirely within the mammalian host (Table 23.4: pigs, wild mammals). The slide assigns it Life Cycle C.
  • How people acquire it: from eating undercooked pork or bear meat (Table 23.4: consumption of meat containing larvae).

17.34 · Objective 1 — Trematodes and Schistosoma (blood flukes)

Trematodes or flukes (slide 111)

Flatworms with ovoid, leaflike bodies. They have digestive, excretory, neuromuscular, and reproductive systems but lack circulatory and respiratory systems. Animals such as snails or fish are usually the intermediate hosts, and humans are the definitive hosts.

Schistosoma and schistosomiasis (slides 112–114)

  • Treatment: praziquantel, oxamniquine (Table 23.5: metrifonate, niridazole and praziquantel for schistosomiasis).
  • Prevention: snail control.

17.37 · Objective 1 — Cestodes and Taenia saginata (beef tapeworm)

Cestode (tapeworm) infestations, slide 118

  • Flatworms, Cycle C.
  • Long, very thin, ribbonlike bodies composed of sacs (proglottids) and a scolex that grips the intestine.
  • No mouth: nutrients are absorbed through the body. Each proglottid is an independent unit adapted to absorbing food and making and releasing eggs.
  • Humans are the definitive host, animals the intermediate host. The two named: Taenia saginata and Taenia solium. (The slide also links a video.)

Taenia saginata, slide 119

  • The beef tapeworm, Cycle C and modified A; it causes taeniasis.
  • Morphology: very large, up to 2,000 proglottids.
  • Hosts: humans are the definitive host. Animals are infected by grazing on land contaminated with human feces.
  • How people acquire it: eating raw beef in which the larval form has encysted (Table 23.4: consumption of undercooked or raw beef). In humans, the larva attaches to the small intestine and becomes an adult.
  • Disease: few symptoms: vague abdominal pain and nausea; proglottids in the stool. Slide 8 instead says it “causes weight loss and wasting in host” (17.42); it also says the beef tapeworm absorbs food directly through the cuticle and reproduces via proglottids.

17.38 · Objective 1 — Taenia solium (pork tapeworm) and cysticercosis

  • The pork tapeworm. It causes two diseases, depending on what is swallowed:
    • Taeniasis if a person ingests contaminated meat (Table 23.4: undercooked or raw pork; slide 8: “Taeniasis if ingest undercooked meat”). The cysts release larvae, which develop into tapeworms that attach to the intestine and may remain for years.
    • Cysticercosis if a person ingests embryonated eggs (from contaminated food or water). The eggs hatch in the intestine, releasing tapeworm larvae that migrate to all tissues and encyst. Cysticercosis is one of the five neglected parasitic infections on slide 10.
  • Disease: the cysts are most damaging if they lodge in heart muscle, eye, or brain; they may cause seizures and psychiatric disturbances. Slide 8: the larvae migrate to the musculature or brain, producing lesions.
  • Treatment: antiparasitic drugs in combination with anti-inflammatory drugs.

17.40 · Objective 1 — The arthropod vectors

Arthropods have an exoskeleton and jointed legs; the group includes arachnids and crustaceans. Many must feed on the blood and tissue fluid of a host during the life cycle: they are ectoparasites. Those of medical importance transmit infectious microbes in the process of feeding: they are biological vectors.

17.42 Where the slides disagree, and where current practice differs

  • How many Plasmodium species. Slide 13’s table lists three (P. vivax, P. falciparum, P. malariae); slide 56 says “4 species” and adds P. ovale. Learn slide 56’s four.
  • The name of Naegleria’s disease. Slide 29’s title is “Primary Acute Meningoencephalitis” (followed by its abbreviation), and her table on slide 4 says “Primary Acute Meningoencephalitis”; slide 24’s map and slide 27 say primary amebic meningoencephalitis, and slide 30’s table “primary amoebic meningoencephalitis”. Primary amebic meningoencephalitis is the usual name; both are the same disease.
  • Acanthamoeba and contact lenses. Her table on slide 4 gives one disease, “Granulomatous Amoebic Encephalitis”, with “Found in contact lens wearers”. Slide 31 separates two diseases: amebic keratitis in contact lens wearers (eye pain, redness, irritation; does not typically disseminate) and granulomatous amebic encephalitis, entering through a break in the skin or the nasal passages; slide 32’s life cycle agrees with slide 31 (the eye gives keratitis; the nose and skin give the encephalitis). The contact lens belongs to the keratitis.
  • Naegleria case counts. Slide 4: “only about 200 cases reported worldwide”; slide 27: 381 global confirmed cases through 2018. In the United States: slide 24’s map, 157 cases (1962–2022); slide 25, 164 cases and 4 survivors (1962–2023); slide 27, 180 confirmed cases (1937–2025) and 4 survivors out of 167 cases (1962–2025). The windows differ; what agrees is rare, more than 97% fatal, 4 known United States survivors.
  • Who dies of Naegleria. Slide 26 says the estimated 16 deaths a year are “equally distributed between males and females”; slide 27’s table gives males aged 10–14 as the group with the highest case count, and slide 28 says most cases are children and young adults. Slide 26 also calls the 16 a year deaths, where slide 27 calls about 16 a year the estimated true case burden and fewer than 10 the typical annual count.
  • Is Toxoplasma vector-borne? Slide 13’s table gives its source as “Zoonotic/vector-borne”; slides 64–65 describe no vector: it is acquired by ingesting raw meats or substances contaminated by cat feces, and transplacentally. Learn slides 64–65. (Slide 13 also calls Cryptosporidium “free-living”, where slide 72 says it infects mammals, birds and reptiles.)
  • Gametes or gametocytes. Slide 58 says merozoites “differentiate into gametes (Gametocytic phase)”; slide 59’s figure says some red blood cells give rise to male and female gametocytes, which infect the mosquito. Both describe the stage the mosquito picks up. Also: slide 58’s 5–16 days is when merozoites enter the circulation; slide 60’s 10–16 days is the incubation period.
  • Misspelled drug names in Table 23.2 (slide 14). “Hetronidazole” (for Giardia) is metronidazole: its mechanism cell says “See above”, pointing to metronidazole, and slide 41 names metronidazole. “Miefloquine” is mefloquine (spelled correctly in the next column and on slide 61). “Eflomithine” is eflornithine (slide 47). “Benzonidazole” (Table 23.2 and slide 49) is benznidazole. Slide 31’s “trimethroprim” and “ketokonazole” are trimethoprim and ketoconazole, and its rifampicin is slide 30’s rifampin (two names for one drug). The tables above keep the printed spellings with the correct name beside them.
  • Other spellings. Slide 3 writes “Tryponosoma” (twice), “Leishmani sp.”, “Trichuris trichura”, “Angiostroglylus cantonensis”, “Stronglyoides stercoralis”, “Wucherica Bancroft”, “Shistoma sp.” and “Paragoniumus westermani”; her table (slides 4–8) writes “Trichuris trichura”, “PInworm”, “Angiostronglylus Cantonensis”, “Tse tse fly”, “Wucherica bancroft”, “Shistosoma sp.” and “Paragoniumus westermani”. They are Trypanosoma, Leishmania, Trichuris trichiura, pinworm, Angiostrongylus cantonensis, Strongyloides stercoralis, tsetse fly, Wuchereria bancrofti, Schistosoma and Paragonimus westermani, as written in this guide. Slide 49’s “Romana’s sign” is Romaña’s sign; slide 71’s “sarcosystosis” is sarcocystosis (as its title spells it). The deck’s British spellings (“amoebic”, “amoebomas”) are written in American spelling here, and its abbreviations are written out in full.
  • African sleeping sickness drugs (slide 47). The slide lists “Treatment before neurological involvement melarsoprol, suramin, eflornithine”. In current practice suramin and pentamidine are the early-stage drugs, and melarsoprol and eflornithine are the drugs used once the central nervous system is involved. Learn the three names as the slide gives them, and that treatment should start before neurological involvement.
  • Isospora treatment (slide 74). The slide gives sulfadiazine and pyrimethamine, the same pair as for toxoplasmosis. Current references (where the organism is now named Cystoisospora belli) use trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole first, with pyrimethamine for people who cannot take sulfa drugs. Learn the slide’s pair.
  • Malaria numbers (slide 56). 300–500 million new cases and 2 million deaths a year are older figures; recent World Health Organization reports give roughly 250 million cases and about 600,000 deaths a year, still mostly in Africa. The numbers are context; the 40% of the world threatened and 90% in Africa are the slide’s points.
  • How Schistosoma gets in. Table 23.4 (slide 84) gives all three species as spread by “Ingestion of fresh water containing larval stage”. Slide 8 says “Individuals acquire by contact with water in which the snail lives”, slide 112 “This larva penetrates human skin”, slide 113 lists “Itchiness at entry site”, and slide 114’s cycle says “Cercariae penetrate skin”. The guide teaches skin penetration (four slides and the diagram; current practice agrees: cercariae penetrate the skin during contact with fresh water). Know that the table says ingestion.
  • Taenia saginata symptoms. Slide 8 says the beef tapeworm “Causes weight loss and wasting in host”; slide 119 says it “Causes few symptoms; vague abdominal pain and nausea; proglottids in stool”. The guide teaches slide 119, the organism’s own slide.
  • Treating Opisthorchis. Slide 116 gives “triclabendazole with bithionol as an alternative” for the Chinese liver fluke; Table 23.5 (slide 87) lists neither drug and gives praziquantel for “Schistosomiasis, other flukes, tapeworm”, and slide 116 itself gives praziquantel for Paragonimus. In current practice praziquantel is the drug for Opisthorchis/Clonorchis, and triclabendazole is the drug for Fasciola (slide 117 names no treatment for Fasciola). Keyed as slide 116 says.
  • What is in the fish. Slide 116 says people eat fish “containing cercariae”. In slide 117’s Fasciola cycle the cercariae encyst and the stage eaten is the “metacercariae”; current references likewise call the stage in the fish a metacercaria (an encysted cercaria). Not asked.
  • Trichinella and Cycle C. Slide 101 assigns Trichinella to Life Cycle C but also says its “Life cycle [is] entirely within mammalian host”. Slide 82’s Cycle C has eggs released in feces and a food animal grazing on contaminated grass, while slide 102’s trichinellosis cycle has no eggs: adults form new larvae that burrow into the circulation and encyst in muscle. For Trichinella, learn slides 101–102; the shared point with Cycle C is the encysted larva in undercooked meat.
  • How the soil worms get in. Table 23.4 gives “Fecal pollution of soil with eggs” for the hookworms and Strongyloides, under its own heading “Infective in larval stage”; slides 96 and 100 say the larvae penetrate the skin. Both hold: feces put the eggs in the soil, and the larva that hatches is the infective stage. Slide 96 has the eggs hatch “into filariform larvae”; slide 97’s cycle has a rhabditiform larva hatch first and become the filariform larva that penetrates the skin.
  • Who is the definitive host of a tapeworm. Slide 118 says of cestodes “Humans definitive host, animals intermediate”. That holds for Taenia taeniasis, but slide 124 makes dogs (and foxes and coyotes) the definitive hosts of Echinococcus, with people carrying the cysts, and in cysticercosis (slides 121–122) the person carries the larval cysts of Taenia solium.
  • Slide 116’s Paragonimus line is garbled: “humans worms migrate to lungs infected by eating undercooked crustaceans; intestinal”. Read it as: humans are infected by eating undercooked crustaceans, and the worms migrate to the lungs. The trailing “intestinal” is not explained.
  • “Mectizan” (slide 104). The slide treats bancroftian filariasis with “Albendazole, mectizan (only for filarial stage)”. Mectizan is a brand name of ivermectin (not stated in the deck); Table 23.5 lists ivermectin for onchocerciasis, ascariasis and strongyloidiasis, and diethylcarbamazine (kills microfilariae, not the adult worm) for filariasis. Not asked.
  • Drug lists that read oddly. Slide 92’s “albendazole with mebendazole, ivermectin, and nitazoxanide—1-3 days” for Ascaris reads as a list of options rather than a four-drug combination (current practice uses one drug, usually albendazole or mebendazole). Slide 113 gives praziquantel and oxamniquine for schistosomiasis while Table 23.5 gives metrifonate, niridazole and praziquantel; praziquantel is the drug both name, and the one used today.
  • Isospora’s source. Slide 13’s table gives Isospora belli “Dogs, other mammals”; slide 74 says “Transmitted in fecally contaminated food or drink”. The 17.41 table gives both.
  • Spellings and names. Organism names are written correctly here: slide 88’s “Trichuris trichuria” (Trichuris trichiura), slide 98’s “Angiostronglylus”, slide 104’s “Wuchereia” and slides 3 and 8’s “Wucherica bancroft” (Wuchereria bancrofti), slide 107’s “Loa Loa”, slide 124’s “Echinococcocus”, slides 3 and 8’s “Shistosoma” and “Paragoniumus”. Table 23.6 is transcribed as printed, including “Human erhlichosis” (ehrlichiosis) and “Apicomplex protozoan”. Table 23.5’s “trichinosis” and slide 101’s “trichinellosis” are the same disease.
