14.1 · Objective 1 — Endotoxin, the shared weapon

Endotoxin is a structural component of the Gram-negative outer membrane. Chemically it is lipopolysaccharide; lipid A is the biologically active component of the complex, the part most responsible for pathogenesis. It is released on the lysis of Gram-negative cells, including through antibiotic therapy, and as bacterial cells grow and divide, particularly when they are rapidly proliferating.

Part of the lipopolysaccharideWhat it is
Lipid AThe lipid region to which the polysaccharide is bound; the biologically active component
Core polysaccharideIdentical for large groups of bacteria, with some unique sugars
O antigen polysaccharideUnique for each organism; repeating units of 3–4 different hexoses. The number of repeating units designates the antigen as rough, semi-rough or smooth

What endotoxin does

  • Usually protective. In the gut and airways the first contact between epithelium and Gram-negative bacteria is the outer membrane and its lipopolysaccharide. These are antigens of the innate (nonspecific) immune system, signaling through a lipopolysaccharide binding complex and a Toll-like receptor. Under most circumstances this gives a moderated, protective inflammatory response, which keeps most mucosal infections mucosal and helps maintain our symbiosis with some Gram-negative bacilli.
  • Chronic infection. Some bacteria have adapted by modifying their lipopolysaccharide to evade the immune system.
  • Potentially damaging, through complement, coagulation and a variety of cells:
    • Complement turns on by the classical pathway (antigen-antibody reaction) or the alternative pathway, and can result in hemorrhagic necrosis.
    • Coagulation: tissue injury through coagulation changes, disseminated intravascular coagulation; fibrin deposited in the lungs, liver and spleen; it may trigger bradykinin, a vasodilator in shock.
    • Platelets: altered activity causing aggregation (differs among species). Neutrophils: low levels may enhance phagocytosis, but higher levels may interfere with chemotaxis to the site of infection. Macrophages release pro-coagulants, collagenase, pyrogens, prostaglandins, colony stimulating factor, tumor necrosis factor, interleukin 1 and interleukin 6, nitric oxide and reactive oxygen species.
Low dose against flood. Low levels of endotoxin give a low-level inflammatory response: fever, malaise, aches and pains. A large flood releases the mediators and contributes to septic shock: tumor necrosis factor and interleukin 1 make neutrophils stick to endothelium and activate clotting, forming microthrombi throughout the body; arteries and arterioles dilate, peripheral arterial resistance falls and cardiac output at first rises (warm shock); later cardiac output falls and blood pressure drops, leading to tissue hypoxia, infarction and multisystem organ failure.

14.2 · Objective 1 — Sorting the Gram-negative bacilli

The Gram-negative bacilli are differentiated by three properties: oxygen requirement (aerobic, facultatively anaerobic, obligate anaerobic), the ability to ferment lactose, and the oxidase enzyme.

Aerobic Gram-negative nonenteric bacilli are a large, diverse group of non-spore-forming bacteria, from habitats as varied as the large intestine, animals, the respiratory tract, soil and water. Most are not medically important; some are true pathogens and some opportunists. All use endotoxin as one of their offensive weapons. (The third branch of the scheme, the obligate anaerobes, is Bacteroides.) The medically important aerobic genera fall in three pairs:

Pseudomonas and BurkholderiaOpportunistic pathogens
Brucella and FrancisellaZoonotic pathogens
Bordetella and LegionellaMainly human pathogens

Virulence and resistance

  • Exotoxin A inhibits protein synthesis.
  • Slime layer: resistance to phagocytosis and biofilm formation. Cells in biofilms adhere by flagella and pili and form microcolonies; three polysaccharides stabilize the biofilm and impair bacterial clearance.
  • Exoenzymes: exoenzyme S induces apoptosis of T cells; others degrade phospholipid.
  • Multidrug resistant, through restricted outer membrane permeability, which limits drug entry, antibiotic-inactivating enzymes, efflux pumps, horizontal transfer of resistance genes and biofilm formation. The slide notes it is one of the top three critical pathogens the World Health Organization prioritized for research into new treatments (with Acinetobacter baumannii and Escherichia coli; see 14.21).

Disease

  • A common cause of nosocomial infection in hosts with burns, neoplastic disease and cystic fibrosis (the slide stars cystic fibrosis with three asterisks). Complications include pneumonia, urinary tract infection, abscesses, otitis and corneal disease; septicemia leads to endocarditis, meningitis and bronchopneumonia.
  • Typical infections in healthy people: skin rashes, urinary tract infection, otitis externa (swimmer’s ear), and corneal ulcers in contact lens wearers.

Treatment and prevention. The antibiotics listed are cephalosporins, aminoglycosides, carbenicillin, polymyxin, quinolones and monobactams. A vaccine is in development, particularly for cystic fibrosis patients: most candidates rely on antibodies against bacterial protein; one uses the PopB protein to stimulate T helper 17 cells, which release interleukin 17 and enhance antibacterial mucosal defenses; one in current testing uses the porin membrane protein OprH and has worked against at least two strains. But in cystic fibrosis, where the infection is often chronic, vaccination may induce increased pulmonary inflammation and tissue damage: it may worsen lung inflammation. Mucosal delivery by liposomes and nanoparticles is being investigated.

HeadingBordetella pertussis

Why pertussis is rising again. Case charts from 1922 to 2026 show the fall after the whole-cell vaccine and a rise since the acellular vaccine. The deck gives three threads:
  • Infants under 2 months are too young to be vaccinated (they cannot get the vaccine), so incidence is highest under 1 year old (in Florida in 2023, over half of reported cases were not up to date on vaccination).
  • The age pattern in older children and adolescents reflects waning immunity from childhood vaccination and the need for booster doses.
  • “Another possible connection”: pertactin-negative strains are now developing. Pertactin is the protein that helps the bacterium adhere to airway linings and a vaccine component, and these strains lack it.

14.8 · Objective 1 — Alcaligenes

An aerobe of the non-fermenting branch (14.2), not on the syllabus list. Lives primarily in soil and water and may become normal flora. Alcaligenes faecalis is the most common clinical species, isolated from feces, sputum and urine; it is occasionally associated with opportunistic pneumonia, septicemia and meningitis.

14.10 · Objective 1 — The Enterobacteriaceae: traits, media, antigens

The enterics are a large family of small, non-spore-forming Gram-negative rods. Many live in soil, water and decaying matter and are common occupants of the large bowel of animals, including humans. They are the most frequent cause of diarrheal disease, by enterotoxins or by invasive infection, and with Pseudomonas they account for almost half of nosocomial infections; they have been isolated from soap dishes, sinks, catheters and tracheal cannulas.

Antigens and virulence factors

Complex surface antigens contribute to pathogenicity and trigger the immune response:

  • H — the flagellar antigen.
  • K — the capsule and/or fimbrial antigen (Vi in Salmonella).
  • O — the somatic or cell wall antigen, which all have.

Other virulence factors: endotoxin, exotoxins, capsules, fimbriae, attachment receptors for host cells, and the ability to transmit virulence genes (toxins, capsules, hemolysins, fimbriae).

14.11 · Objective 1 — Escherichia coli and its four pathotypes

Escherichia coli is the common aerobic, non-fastidious bacterium of the gut, but not the most prevalent: the anaerobic Bacteroides and Bifidobacterium are. Of about 150 strains, some have developed virulence through plasmid transfer; others are opportunists.

15.1 · Objective 1 — True and opportunistic fungal pathogens

Molds and yeasts are widely distributed in air, dust, fomites and the normal flora of humans, plants and animals. Humans are relatively resistant to fungal infections as long as the immune system is competent; immunocompromised status is the serious consideration throughout this lecture. Fungi are relatively nonpathogenic: of about 100,000 fungal species only about 300 (0.3%) have been linked to disease in humans and animals. But fungi are the most common plant pathogens (potato blights, infected grape crops and fruit trees), which can cause huge socio-economic losses such as the potato famines in Ireland and Eastern Europe. Human mycoses (“myco” = fungi) are caused by true fungal pathogens and by opportunistic pathogens.

15.2 · Objective 1 — Thermal dimorphism

The most striking adaptation of a true pathogen to survival and growth in the human host is the ability to switch from hyphal cells to yeast cells, from multicellular to unicellular forms, with the corresponding change in temperature. 15.3 · Objective 1 — The fungal priority pathogens list

The World Health Organization announced its first-ever fungal priority pathogens list in October 2022, to complement its bacterial priority pathogens list of 2017. 15.4 · Objective 1 — Epidemiology, pathogenesis and host defense

  • Most fungal pathogens do not require a host to complete their life cycles, and these infections are generally not communicable to other persons.
  • The exceptions: dermatophytes (literally “skin plants”) and Candida species naturally inhabit the human body and are transmissible from person to person, or by fomites such as shared exercise equipment.
  • True fungal pathogens are distributed in predictable geographical patterns, coinciding with climate and soil requirements (the map in 15.7).
  • Dermatophytoses are the most prevalent fungal diseases, and many cases go undiagnosed or are misdiagnosed (for example as skin-related allergies or autoimmune disorders).

Host defense

The main antifungal defenses are the integrity of the skin, mucous membranes and intact respiratory cilia. The most important defenses are cell-mediated immunity (T cells and their cytokines), phagocytosis and inflammation. 15.5 · Objective 1 — Diagnosis

Diagnosis and identification need microscopic examination of stained specimens, culture on selective and enriched media, and specific biochemical or serological tests. Sabouraud dextrose agar is commonly used, with chemicals added to inhibit bacterial growth; its fairly low pH inhibits most bacteria, but not fungi.

Hair, skin and nail specimens. The identification pathway applies examination under Wood's light directly to the hairs, alongside a hot potassium hydroxide wet mount, brighteners and periodic acid-Schiff staining.

Germ tube test. Some yeasts, when incubated in serum for 2 to 4 hours, sprout tiny hyphal tubes called germ tubes; the germ tube test sits among the identification tests for isolates.

Bread making

Microbes have three main functions: (1) leavening flour-based dough (mainly carbon dioxide and other gases); (2) imparting flavor and aroma (organic acids and alcohols); (3) conditioning the dough to make it pliable and workable (microbial enzymes act on gluten to give elasticity). Beer, wine and other fermented products

  • Glucose + yeast → 2 ethanol + 2 carbon dioxide (C6H12O6 → 2C2H5OH + 2CO2). Evidence of beer brewing since 6000 BCE in Sumeria, Egypt and Babylon. Steps: malting of the starch source, mashing, wort plus hops, lagering, filtration.
  • Wine usually starts with grapes, but other fruit and plant material can be used (apples, oranges, pineapples, peaches, plums).
  • Sometimes the source of yeast for an alcoholic beverage is human saliva: chewing (mastication) of the plant material precedes the brewing.
  • Pickling: salt brine preserves vegetable material; lactic acid may also be present, with vinegar and spices. Cucumbers, cabbage, olives, okra, onions, ginger root and many more can be pickled.
  • Soybeans and their extracts are fermented into tofu and soy sauce (shoyu); also fermented fish sauce, mole (chocolate), tea and coffee.

Milk and dairy

  • Milk begins sterile: water, minerals, protein, butterfat, lactose and other sugars. Microbes are introduced as it exits the female mammal’s body and by milking utensils; it is highly susceptible to microbial growth because of its nutrient density.
  • How microbes first get into milk. Milk begins as a sterile mixture; microbes are introduced on leaving the animal, as it exits the female mammal's body, and from milking utensils.
  • Fermentation of milk can be natural or induced (cheese or yogurt making). Cheese making is about 5000 years old (cow, goat or sheep milk; some cultures horse or water buffalo). The first fermented dairy products were probably due to accidental contamination with human skin, nose or oral bacteria and fungi. Cheese making involves yeast, bacteria and molds (multicellular fungi).
  • Yogurt is a fermented milk made with bacteria such as Lactobacillus and Streptococcus; other fermented milks are kefir, koumiss and buttermilk.

  • Macroscopic fungi and algae are obvious foods: mushrooms, truffles, seaweed. 17.3 · Objective 1 — Entamoeba histolytica and amebiasis

Slide 15 opens the infective amebas: Entamoeba histolytica (this section), Naegleria fowleri (17.4) and Acanthamoeba (17.5).

Morphology and hosts

  • Entamoeba histolytica alternates between a large trophozoite, motile by means of pseudopods, and a smaller nonmotile cyst.
  • The trophozoite has a large nucleus and lacks most other organelles.
  • The name: histolytica = histo, tissue + lytica, lysis (slide 19).
  • Humans are the primary hosts. It is ingested.
  • It is carried by 10% of the world population, asymptomatically, and kills 100,000 people per year.

How people acquire it, and where

  • By the fecal oral route (her table, slide 4): cysts are swallowed. The reservoir and source is human, water and food (slide 13).
  • Prevalent in tropical countries; related to fertilization practices and sewage treatment.
  • Epidemics sometimes occur in concentrated populations where water supplies are polluted: prisons, hospitals, juvenile care institutions.
  • Sometimes sexually transmitted.

Life cycle (slides 18–19)

Cysts are swallowed and arrive at the small intestine; the alkaline pH and digestive juices stimulate the cysts to release 4 trophozoites. The trophozoites attach, multiply, actively move about and feed. Cysts are produced after the active phase of disease (slide 23) and leave in the feces.

Disease and clinical manifestations

Asymptomatic in 90% of patients; severity is related to the strain, the amount of inoculum and host conditions. Amebiasis takes two forms, intestinal and extraintestinal (her table names the disease “amebiasis—amebic dysentery”).

17.6 · Objective 1 — Balantidium coli, the intestinal ciliate

  • An occupant of the intestines of domestic animals such as pigs and cattle (slide 13: “zoonotic in pigs”; her table: pigs and cattle as alternate hosts).

17.8 · Objective 1 — Giardia lamblia and giardiasis

Morphology (slide 39)

  • A pathogenic flagellate with a unique symmetrical heart shape and a concave ventral surface that acts like a suction cup.
  • Cysts are small, compact and multinucleate.

Reservoirs and how people acquire it

  • Reservoirs include beavers, cattle, coyotes, cats and humans (slide 13: zoonotic, water and food).
  • Cysts can survive for 2 months in the environment.
  • Usually ingested with water and food; the infectious dose is 10 to 100 cysts. Her table: common in campers. ★ Dr. Webster: “this is the one that you will get going camping”.

Disease, diagnosis, treatment, prevention (slide 41)

  • Cysts enter the duodenum, germinate, and travel to the jejunum to feed and multiply.
  • Giardiasis: diarrhea, abdominal pain.
  • Diagnosis: microscopic diagnosis is difficult because the organism is shed in feces intermittently; polymerase chain reaction analysis.
  • Treatment: quinacrine or metronidazole, or nitazoxanide in children.
  • The agent is killed by boiling, ozone and iodine.

17.11 · Objective 1 — Trypanosoma cruzi and Chagas disease

  • T. cruzi causes Chagas disease, or American trypanosomiasis.
  • Millions of cases in Latin America, but it is becoming more of an issue in the Southern United States: vector migration ★ Starred on the slide. Dr. Webster: it is the one “you are more likely to see in your clinical work”, “because of the migration of the vector. The vector and how people acquire it (slide 49)

  • The reduviid bug (kissing bug) is the vector.

  • A local lesion: Romaña’s sign, also called a chagoma, a swelling of the eyelid with conjunctivitis; with fever and swelling of the lymph nodes, spleen and liver.

  • Chronic inflammation occurs in the organs, especially the heart and brain; Chagas accounts for 30–40% of heart disease in endemic regions.

17.13 · Objective 1 — Plasmodium and malaria

The apicomplexan parasites (slide 55)

  • Sporozoans that lack locomotor organelles in the trophozoite state.
  • They alternate between sexual and asexual phases and between different animal hosts.
  • Most form specialized infective bodies that are transmitted by arthropod vectors, food, water or other means: Plasmodium, Toxoplasma, Cryptosporidium.

Plasmodium, the agent of malaria (slides 56–57)

  • Malaria is the dominant protozoan disease: it threatens 40% of the world’s population each year.
  • Plasmodium is an obligate intracellular sporozoan.
  • Four species (slide 56): P. malariae, P. vivax, P. falciparum and P. ovale (slide 13 lists only the first three; 17.42).
  • The female Anopheles mosquito is the primary vector; malaria also spreads by blood transfusions and mother to fetus.
  • 300–500 million new cases each year (90% in Africa); 2 million deaths each year; 1,000–2,000 cases a year in the United States.

  • Some populations are naturally resistant: sickle cell trait.
  • Increasing drug resistance.
  • Most therapy is based on quinine, which prevents the parasite from processing hemoglobin in its food vacuoles.

  • Mosquito control is key. ★ Dr. Webster: “once again, vector control is what they do”.
  • United States cases: 2022, 1,620 (Florida 48); 2023, 2,120 (Florida 58, with 8 non-travel cases of malaria in Florida); 2024, 1,576 (Florida 57). ★ Dr. Webster: locally acquired cases in Florida, with no travel, “so that’s why you might see this one”.

17.14 · Objective 1 — Toxoplasma gondii and toxoplasmosis

The coccidian parasites (slide 63)

Coccidians are zoonotic in domestic animals and birds and exist in stages including dormant oocysts and pseudocysts. The examples: Toxoplasma (this section), Sarcocystis (17.15), Cryptosporidium (17.16), Isospora (17.17), Cyclospora (17.18) and Babesia (17.19); the end of 17.19 sets them side by side.

The organism and how people acquire it (slides 64–65)

  • An intracellular apicomplexan parasite with an extensive distribution, capable of infecting virtually all warm-blooded animals.
  • Seroprevalence indicates that approximately one third of the human population worldwide has been exposed.
  • It lives naturally in cats, which harbor oocysts in the gastrointestinal tract, released through feces; slide 70 calls the cat the feline definitive host.
  • Acquired by ingesting raw meats or substances contaminated by cat feces.
  • Transplacental transmission to the fetus when a pregnant woman has toxoplasmosis: 33% chance of transmission. Her table: pregnant women should not clean the cat box because of the danger of transplacental transmission. ★ Dr. Webster: “if you are with a patient who is pregnant and she has a cat, you tell her, get somebody else to clean the litter box”.

Disease

  • Most cases go unnoticed, with mild symptoms: sore throat, fever, enlarged lymph nodes.
  • Serious complications in immunocompromised individuals, who can suffer brain and heart damage: toxoplasma encephalitis (her table: danger of central nervous system involvement for patients with acquired immunodeficiency syndrome).
  • Congenital: spontaneous abortion, stillbirth, or a spectrum of severe neurological and ocular manifestations in the newborn, including hydrocephalus, intracranial calcifications and chorioretinitis.

Diagnosis, treatment and vaccines (slide 65)

  • Diagnosed by serological tests, culture, histological analysis.
  • Treatment: pyrimethamine and sulfadiazine, long-term administration (Table 23.2: together they inhibit enzymes in the pathway for folate synthesis).
  • Vaccine development is in progress, but hampered by the genetic diversity of T. gondii strains, the complexity of a life cycle with many different stages, and the need to develop a robust and balanced TH1 (T helper 1) cellular immune response. A promising candidate is a genetically engineered live attenuated strain with a crucial transcription factor blocked.

Toxoplasmosis and mental disorders (slides 68 and 70)

  • Studies link cat ownership in childhood with later development of mental illness.
  • Acute toxoplasmosis can cause delusions and hallucinations; latent toxoplasmosis is a risk factor for schizophrenia, anxiety, and possibly obsessive compulsive disorder and addiction; chronic toxoplasmosis is linked to schizophrenia, mood disorders, psychosis and self-directed violence.
  • One Dutch study showed twice the risk of developing schizophrenia if infected with Toxoplasma.
  • Humans may not be the only animal whose mental state is affected: in hyena and lion observation studies, Toxoplasma-infected hyena cubs are bolder and more likely to get close to lions, and have a higher mortality rate. The hypothesis: boldness in hyena young furthers the distribution of Toxoplasma into the feline definitive host.

17.15 · Objective 1 — Sarcocystis and sarcocystosis

  • Sarcocystis species are parasites of cattle, swine and sheep.
  • Two species infect humans: S. hominis (from cattle) and S. suihominis (from pigs) (her memory aid: “that sui tells you the pig”). Humans can be dead-end hosts for other species.
  • Domestic animals are intermediate hosts: they pick up infective cysts while grazing on grass contaminated with human feces.
  • Humans are infected when the undercooked meat is consumed.
  • Symptoms: diarrhea, nausea and abdominal pain with S. hominis and S. suihominis; but muscle cysts, myalgia, weakness and edema from other species.
  • Albendazole and corticosteroids are used to treat the muscular sarcocystosis.

17.16 · Objective 1 — Cryptosporidium: a newly recognized intestinal pathogen

  • An intestinal pathogen that infects a variety of mammals, birds and reptiles (slide 13: free-living, water and food).
  • It exists in tissue and oocyst phases.
  • An infected person or animal can shed 10–100 million parasites in a single bowel movement; the infectious dose is as little as 10 organisms.

How people acquire it

  • Most often by consuming water in recreational settings, or consuming contaminated water, ice or foodstuffs; it is the leading cause of recreational waterborne disease. ★ Dr. Webster: “this we’re starting to hear more and more and more about”.
  • The organism can tolerate chlorine in swimming pools.
  • In the 1990s, 370,000 cases in Milwaukee, Wisconsin, from contaminated water; filtration is required for removal (her table: the major outbreak in Wisconsin from unpurified water).

Disease (slide 73)

  • Ingestion of oocysts gives rise to sporozoites that penetrate intestinal cells.
  • It causes gastroenteritis, headache, sweating, vomiting, abdominal cramps and diarrhea.
  • Patients with acquired immunodeficiency syndrome and other immunocompromised people may suffer chronic persistent diarrhea; young children and the elderly are likely to be infected.
  • Few effective drugs; it is considered a potential agent for bioterrorism.
  • The largest hidden burden among United States diarrheal parasites: an estimated 748,000 a year, with fewer than 2% verified in surveillance data.

17.17 · Objective 1 — Isospora belli and coccidiosis

  • An intracellular intestinal parasite with an oocyst stage. Slide 13: isosporosis, from dogs and other mammals.
  • Transmitted in fecally contaminated food or drink.
  • Infection is usually asymptomatic or self-limited.
  • Symptoms: malaise, nausea and vomiting, diarrhea, fatty stools, abdominal cramping and weight loss.
  • Primarily associated with immunocompromised hosts.
  • Treat with sulfadiazine and pyrimethamine, when required.

17.18 · Objective 1 — Cyclospora cayetanensis and cyclosporiasis

  • An emerging protozoan pathogen; it causes cyclosporiasis.
  • Oral-fecal transmission: fresh produce and water (slide 13: water, fresh produce).
  • Oocysts enter the small intestine and invade the mucosa.
  • Symptoms: watery diarrhea, stomach cramps, bloating, fever, muscle aches.
  • Diagnosis can be complicated: multiple stool samples must be tested. The slide 76 picture is an acid-fast stain of a fecal smear.
  • Treatment: trimethoprim and sulfamethoxazole.

17.19 · Objective 1 — Babesia species and babesiosis (piroplasmosis)

  • Babesia was the first protozoan found to cause a disease: redwater fever of cattle (also called bovine babesiosis, Texas fever, or tick fever).
  • It was the first protozoan found to be associated with a vector: the tick.
  • Human babesiosis: Babesia microti, carried by the blacklegged deer tick. A relatively rare zoonosis.
  • Flu-like symptoms, but the parasite attacks red blood cells: hemolytic anemia can result.
  • Serious for those without a spleen, the immunocompromised, those with pre-existing liver or kidney disease, and those older than 50.

Helminth pathology arises from worms feeding on and migrating through tissues, from the accumulation of worms in organs, causing blockages, and from toxic worm secretions. 17.22 · Objective 1 — Nematodes and Ascaris lumbricoides

Nematodes (roundworms), slide 89

One of the most abundant animal groups; 50 species affect humans. Elongated, cylindrical worms with protective cuticles, circular muscles, a complete digestive tract, and separate sexes. They are divided into intestinal nematodes (17.22–17.28) and tissue nematodes (17.29–17.31).

Ascaris lumbricoides (ascariasis), slides 90–92

  • Morphology: a large intestinal roundworm (the common name on slides 6 and 88: roundworm).
  • Where and hosts: indigenous to humans; it spends its larval and adult stages in humans. Most cases in the United States occur in the southeastern states.
  • How people acquire it (Life Cycle A): embryonic eggs are released in feces and spread to other humans through food, drink, or contaminated objects (Table 23.4: fecal pollution of soil with eggs). Ingested eggs hatch into larvae, burrow through the intestine into the circulation, travel to the lungs and pharynx, and are swallowed. Adult worms complete the cycle in the intestine and reproduce: 200,000 eggs a day.
  • Disease: the worms retain motility and do not attach. Severe inflammatory reactions mark the migratory route, and allergic reactions can occur. Heavy worm loads can cause abdominal pain and intestinal obstruction. Lung migration may cause pulmonary symptoms: eosinophilic pneumonitis. It may retard physical and mental development.
  • Diagnosis: microscopic or macroscopic analysis.
  • Treatment: albendazole with mebendazole, ivermectin, and nitazoxanide, for 1–3 days (slide 92). Table 23.5 lists albendazole, ivermectin, mebendazole and piperazine for ascariasis.

17.23 · Objective 1 — Trichuris trichiura (whipworm)

  • Common name: whipworm (trichuriasis).
  • Hosts and where: humans are the sole host. Trichuriasis has its highest incidence in the tropics; in the United States, in the South.
  • How people acquire it (Life Cycle A): eggs (Table 23.4: fecal pollution of soil with eggs) are swallowed and hatch in the intestines; the larvae attach, penetrate the outer wall and develop into adults. Females lay 3,000–5,000 eggs daily.
  • Disease: worms can pierce capillaries and cause localized hemorrhage. Heavy infestations can cause dysentery and rectal prolapse, and can be fatal in children.
  • Treatment: mebendazole is the drug of choice, with albendazole as an alternative (Table 23.5: mebendazole for trichuriasis).

17.24 · Objective 1 — Enterobius vermicularis (pinworm)

  • Common names: pinworm or seatworm (enterobiasis). Enterobiasis is the most common worm disease of children in temperate zones. ★ Starred on the slide. Slide 6: very common in daycare situations. ★ Dr. Webster: “the one that you will almost surely see in your clinical life”, “the scourge of the daycare center”.
  • How people acquire it: eggs are picked up from the surroundings and swallowed; slide 6 and Table 23.4: transmission by direct (close) contact and fomites. Self-inoculation is common.
  • Life cycle: after hatching in the small intestine, the eggs develop into adults. Anal itching occurs when mature females emerge from the intestine to release eggs.
  • Diagnosis: the tape test (slide 6: “Diagnosed with Scotch tape test”).
  • Treatment: pyrantel pamoate (Table 23.5: pyrantel and piperazine for pinworm).

17.25 · Objective 1 — Hookworms: Necator americanus and Ancylostoma duodenale

  • Morphology: characteristic curved ends and hooked mouths. Two species: Necator americanus (the New World hookworm) and Ancylostoma duodenale (the Old World hookworm), Table 23.4.
  • How people acquire it (Life Cycle B): humans shed eggs in feces, which hatch into filariform larvae that burrow into the skin of bare feet (slide 6: “transmitted through skin on soles of feet”). The larvae travel from the blood to the lungs, proceed up the bronchi and throat and are swallowed; the worms mature and reproduce in the small intestine and complete the cycle.
  • Disease: may cause pneumonia, nausea, vomiting, cramps, and bloody diarrhea. Blood loss is significant: anemia.
  • Treatment: albendazole, mebendazole or pyrantel pamoate (Table 23.5 also lists piperazine and thiabendazole for hookworm).

17.26 · Objective 1 — Angiostrongylus cantonensis (rat lungworm)

  • What it is: the rat lungworm, a nematode that causes eosinophilic meningitis. Snails are intermediate hosts.
  • How people acquire it: by ingesting contaminated snails, water, or vegetables (slide 7: “acquired by eating larvae in snails or contaminated water or vegetables”).
  • Disease: incubation period 1 day to 6 weeks. Symptoms of bacterial meningitis: nausea, vomiting, headaches, stiff neck.
  • Diagnosis: lumbar puncture shows elevated eosinophils instead of neutrophils.
  • Outcome: some patients recover on their own, because the parasite does not survive long in the human body; others experience coma and death. The slides name no treatment.
  • Prevention: snail and slug control and washing contaminated fruits and vegetables.

17.27 · Objective 1 — Strongyloides stercoralis (threadworm)

  • Morphology and where: the threadworm: tiny roundworms that complete the life cycle in humans or in moist soil (Table 23.4: humans; may live free).
  • How people acquire it (Life Cycle B): larvae penetrate the skin, migrate to the lungs, are swallowed, and complete development in the intestine. It can reinfect the same host without leaving the body.
  • Disease: heavy worm loads can cause pneumonitis and eosinophilia, bloody diarrhea, liver enlargement, bowel obstruction, and malabsorption.
  • Treatment: ivermectin, with albendazole as the alternative (Table 23.5: ivermectin and thiabendazole for strongyloidiasis).

17.28 · Objective 1 — Trichinella spiralis and trichinellosis

  • Names: trichinellosis; slide 7 and Table 23.5 say trichinosis, Table 23.4 the trichina worm.
  • Hosts: the life cycle is entirely within the mammalian host (Table 23.4: pigs, wild mammals). The slide assigns it Life Cycle C.
  • How people acquire it: from eating undercooked pork or bear meat (Table 23.4: consumption of meat containing larvae).
  • In the body: larvae migrate from the intestine to the blood vessels, muscle, heart, and brain, where they form cysts. Slide 7: “encystment in muscle will cause symptoms”.
  • Disease: first symptoms flulike, with diarrhea; second symptoms muscle and joint pain, shortness of breath, pronounced eosinophilia. Occasional life-threatening manifestations: myocarditis, central nervous system involvement, and pneumonitis.
  • Treatment: steroids, plus mebendazole or albendazole. No cure after the larvae have encysted. ★ Dr. Webster said it twice (“You cannot cure it. You cannot cure it.”) and: “Do not eat your pork rare.” (Table 23.5 lists mebendazole, pyrantel and thiabendazole for trichinosis.)

17.30 · Objective 1 — Onchocerca volvulus and river blindness

17.34 · Objective 1 — Trematodes and Schistosoma (blood flukes)

Trematodes or flukes (slide 111)

Flatworms with ovoid, leaflike bodies. They have digestive, excretory, neuromuscular, and reproductive systems but lack circulatory and respiratory systems. Animals such as snails or fish are usually the intermediate hosts, and humans are the definitive hosts.

Schistosoma and schistosomiasis (slides 112–114)

  • Species and where: Schistosoma mansoni, S. japonicum and S. haematobium, the blood flukes. Schistosomiasis is a prominent parasitic disease of Africa, South America, the Middle East and the Far East: 100 million people.
  • Life Cycle D: adult flukes live in humans, who release eggs into water. The early larva (miracidium) develops in a freshwater snail into a second larva (cercaria).
  • How people acquire it: the cercaria penetrates human skin and moves into the liver to mature (slide 8: individuals acquire it by contact with water in which the snail lives). The adults migrate to the intestine or bladder, feed on blood, and shed eggs into urine and feces. Table 23.4 (slide 84) instead says “ingestion of fresh water containing larval stage” (17.42).
  • Symptoms: itchiness at the entry site, fever, chills, diarrhea, cough, enlargement and inflammation of the liver, spleen and bladder.
  • Treatment: praziquantel, oxamniquine (Table 23.5: metrifonate, niridazole and praziquantel for schistosomiasis).
  • Prevention: snail control.

Taenia saginata, slide 119

  • The beef tapeworm, Cycle C and modified A; it causes taeniasis.
  • Morphology: very large, up to 2,000 proglottids.
  • Hosts: humans are the definitive host. Animals are infected by grazing on land contaminated with human feces.

17.38 · Objective 1 — Taenia solium (pork tapeworm) and cysticercosis

  • The pork tapeworm. It causes two diseases, depending on what is swallowed:
    • Taeniasis if a person ingests contaminated meat (Table 23.4: undercooked or raw pork; slide 8: “Taeniasis if ingest undercooked meat”). The cysts release larvae, which develop into tapeworms that attach to the intestine and may remain for years.
    • Cysticercosis if a person ingests embryonated eggs (from contaminated food or water). The eggs hatch in the intestine, releasing tapeworm larvae that migrate to all tissues and encyst. Cysticercosis is one of the five neglected parasitic infections on slide 10.
  • Disease: the cysts are most damaging if they lodge in heart muscle, eye, or brain; they may cause seizures and psychiatric disturbances. Slide 8: the larvae migrate to the musculature or brain, producing lesions.
  • Treatment: antiparasitic drugs in combination with anti-inflammatory drugs.

18.2 · Objective 1 — Viruses in human disease and viral structure (slides 8–12)

Viruses in human infections and diseases (slide 8):

  • Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites.
  • Animal viruses are categorized according to nucleic acid, capsid, and presence or absence of envelope.
  • There are multiple families of deoxyribonucleic acid and ribonucleic acid viruses.
  • Most deoxyribonucleic acid viruses are double-stranded, but there are some single-stranded deoxyribonucleic acid viruses as well.
  • All ribonucleic acid viruses are single-stranded except for the double-stranded ribonucleic acid reoviruses.

Viral structure (slide 9)

  • All viruses have capsids: protein coats that enclose and protect their nucleic acid. Capsids are helical, icosahedral or complex (the phage).
  • They contain genetic material: deoxyribonucleic acid or ribonucleic acid.
  • They may have an envelope or spikes (animal viruses).
    • The envelope is obtained during budding of the virus through the cell membrane. ★ Her Exam 3 review: “Where do they get that envelope?”
    • The envelope is a mechanism to assist in evading the immune system.

Where viruses came from (slide 12; background, not tested). The total mass of viruses on earth is equivalent to 75 million blue whales. There is thought to be no one common ancestor: viruses may have evolved multiple times in the history of life on earth, and may do so again. Three ideas: they escaped or devolved from whole cells, ditching cellular machinery to live a simple life; they descended from independent genetic elements before cellular life evolved; or a hybrid, an independent genetic element that stole from primitive cells.

18.3 · Objective 1 — Phage replication: lytic, lysogenic and chronic cycles (slides 11, 13–21)

Viruses impact the genome of cellular life: phage viruses carry out transduction in bacteria. An estimated 8% of human deoxyribonucleic acid is of viral origin: mammalian ancestors acquired a gene essential for placental development from a viral source. Bacteriophage viruses have bacterial host cells, but may impact human health. Their replication methods are the lytic cycle, the lysogenic cycle and the chronic cycle (filamentous phages) (slide 13).

The lytic cycle (slide 14)

StepWhat happens

PenetrationViral lysozyme and a tail pin mechanism breach the wall and inject the viral deoxyribonucleic acid into the bacterial host.
ReplicationThe virus turns off normal metabolism and turns the bacterium into a viral factory, first replicating the deoxyribonucleic acid, then the capsid components.

MaturationCompletion of viral formation.
ReleaseViral lysozyme causes the host cell wall to burst, releasing the new virions.

The lysogenic cycle (slide 15)

  • Adsorption and penetration are the same as in the lytic cycle, so the viral deoxyribonucleic acid is in the host cell.
  • Instead of turning off bacterial metabolism, the viral deoxyribonucleic acid integrates into the bacterial host chromosome and becomes a prophage.
  • The prophage-integrated bacterium may persist for an extended period, making generations of prophage bacteria.
  • A shock of some kind induces the prophage to release from the host chromosome and start a lytic cycle, which goes through replication, assembly, maturation and release of new virions.

The chronic cycle (slide 16)

  • Filamentous phages: flexible rods that contain single-stranded deoxyribonucleic acid coding for about 10 genes.
  • They infect mostly Gram-negative bacteria, but have been found to infect the Gram-positive Propionibacterium freudenreichii (used in the manufacturing of Swiss cheese).
  • They typically attach to pili or walls but do not use lysozyme; instead there appears to be a compatibility between virion capsid proteins and receptor proteins in the host. The actual mechanism of deoxyribonucleic acid entrance is yet to be fully understood.
  • Once in the host, the deoxyribonucleic acid and capsid components are replicated and assembled into virions.
  • Virions are extruded from the host cell but do not lyse the host, just slow its growth rate.
  • A cooperative arrangement: the host delivers phages throughout the environment and the phage provides benefits to the host, toxin production and addition to biofilms.

Viruses and autoimmune disorders (slide 29)

18.9 · Objective 4 — Variola virus and smallpox (slides 34–42, 45)

History: from quarantine to variolation to vaccination (slides 34–38; background)

  • Smallpox was recognized as a major health threat for centuries, described in Africa, Asia and Europe since 400 BC, spread along world-wide trade routes and reached the Americas in the 16th century. It requires a human host, so it tended to be present in densely populated areas, with epidemics at regular intervals. Most medical traditions focused on prevention rather than a cure, with quarantine a prevalent method (slide 34).
  • Variolation: the deliberate introduction of smallpox as a preventive measure. First documented in China in the 15th century, by nasal insufflation (powdered smallpox material blown up the nose); also practiced in the Middle East and much of Africa. Western medicine ignored a letter describing it by Dr. Emmanuel Timoni of Constantinople, published in 1714, until it was noticed by Lady Mary Wortley Montagu and the Boston preacher Cotton Mather (slide 35).
  • Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, who had lost her brother to smallpox and survived it herself, had her son and her daughter variolated; the practice spread despite deaths from the bloodletting and deep incisions done with it. The Sutton family of English physicians developed a milder method and by 1770 had treated over 300,000 patients (slide 36). John Williamson (“Johnnie Notions”), self-taught in Scotland, reduced the virulence of the material and was reputed never to have lost a patient. England was the international center of variolation. Cotton Mather learned of it from Onesimus, a man he enslaved; in 1775 George Washington ordered the Continental Army variolated (slide 37).
  • Transition to vaccination, Edward Jenner and James Phipps, 1796: variolation did not always work and sometimes failed to protect from an epidemic; collateral smallpox cases spread by variolated people began to outweigh the benefits; vaccination was a less harmful method, and variolation was made illegal in some countries (slide 38).

Orf virus (slide 54)

  • A parapox virus.
  • Primarily a virus of ruminant animals, sheep and goats: it causes sores on the mouth; in animals the disease is called sore mouth, scabby mouth or contagious ecthyma.
  • It can be transmitted to humans through handling of animals: feeding, shearing, bites.
  • More serious in the immunocompromised.

18.14 · Objective 4 — The herpesviruses: eight that infect humans (slides 55–57)

The herpesviruses: common, persistent human viruses (slide 55):

  • All members show latency and cause recurrent infection; the viral deoxyribonucleic acid forms an episome.
  • Most people are infected with members of this family, with few symptoms.
  • Clinical complications of latency and recurrent infections become more severe with advancing age, cancer chemotherapy, or other conditions that compromise the immune defenses.
  • Common and serious opportunists among patients with acquired immunodeficiency syndrome; may be associated with cancers.
  • Large, enveloped, icosahedral, double-stranded deoxyribonucleic acid; they replicate within the nucleus.

Shared facts (slides 58–59): humans are the only natural reservoir, and humans are susceptible to 2 varieties. It can be spread without visible lesions. ★ Dr. Webster: “here’s the fun part. It can be spread even if you cannot see visible lesions.” Transmission is by direct exposure to secretions containing the virus; active lesions are the most significant source, but genital herpes can be transmitted in the absence of lesions. The virus multiplies in sensory neurons and moves to the ganglia. Recurrent infection is triggered by fever, ultraviolet radiation, stress and mechanical injury; the newly formed viruses migrate to the body surface and produce a local skin or membrane lesion.

Herpes simplex 1 in children and adults (slides 60–62)

  • Herpes labialis (fever blisters, cold sores): the most common recurrent herpes simplex 1 infection; vesicles on the mucocutaneous junction of the lips or adjacent skin; itching and tingling before the vesicles form; the lesion crusts over in 2–3 days and heals.
  • Herpetic gingivostomatitis: infection of the oropharynx in young children; fever, sore throat, swollen lymph nodes.
  • Herpetic keratitis (ocular herpes): inflammation of the eye; a gritty feeling, conjunctivitis, sharp pain and sensitivity to light.
  • Herpetic whitlow: herpes simplex 1 or 2 penetrates a break in the skin and causes a localized infection, usually on one finger; extremely painful and itchy.
  • Herpes gladiatorum (mat herpes): a skin infection common in people playing sports like wrestling, basketball or rugby; may be prevented by showering immediately after practice or games, and by not sharing razors, deodorants or towels.
  • Herpetic meningoencephalitis: may occur during the initial infection or from reactivation of a previous infection; if not treated it may be fatal.
  • Those with underlying immunodeficiency are prone to severe, disseminated herpes.

Herpes simplex 2: genital herpes (slide 63)

Genital herpes (herpes genitalia) starts with malaise, anorexia, fever, and bilateral swelling and tenderness in the groin; then clusters of sensitive vesicles on the genitalia, perineum and buttocks; urethritis, painful urination, cervicitis, itching; the vesicles ulcerate. Recurrent bouts are usually less severe, triggered by menstruation, stress and concurrent bacterial infection.

Herpes of the newborn (slides 64–65)

  • Caused by herpes simplex 1 and 2; potentially fatal in the neonate and fetus.
  • The infant is contaminated by the mother before or during birth; also hand transmission from mother to infant.
  • Infection of the mouth, skin, eyes and central nervous system. Mortality: 30% if confined to the skin, eyes and mouth; 50–80% if the central nervous system is involved.
  • Prevention: preventive screening of pregnant women; delivery by cesarean section if there is an outbreak at the time of birth.

Diagnosis, treatment and control (slides 66–67)

  • Vesicles and exudate are the typical diagnostic signs; scrapings from the base of lesions show giant multinucleated cells and inclusion bodies; culture and specific tests for severe or disseminated herpes simplex; direct fluorescent antibody tests.
  • Treatment (not tested): acyclovir, famciclovir, valacyclovir; topical medications.

18.16 · Objective 4 — Varicella zoster virus: chickenpox and shingles (slides 68–70)

  • Causes chickenpox and shingles; humans are the only natural host.
  • Transmitted by respiratory droplets and contact with fluid from active skin lesions.
  • Most infectious 24–48 hours prior to the development of lesions. ★ Dr. Webster: “most infectious in the 24 to 48 hours before you ever see lesions”; “again it is most infectious before the lesions ever hit.”
  • Primary infection is chickenpox, with characteristic vesicles. The virus enters neurons and remains latent.
  • Later, reactivation results in shingles, with vesicles localized to dermatomes (areas of skin along specific nerve pathways); inflammation of the ganglia gives pain down the nerve pathways. More common in older patients.
  • Shingles patients can infect unvaccinated individuals, particularly children, causing chickenpox, not shingles. ★ Dr. Webster: “they will not get shingles, they will get chicken pox.”
  • There may be issues with secondary infections.
  • Treatment: treat symptoms in uncomplicated infections; acyclovir, famciclovir, interferon for systemic disease (drugs not tested).
  • A live attenuated vaccine for chickenpox and shingles.

18.17 · Objective 4 — Cytomegalovirus (slides 71–74)

  • Cytomegaloviruses produce giant cells with nuclear and cytoplasmic inclusions; also called the salivary gland virus or cytomegalic inclusion virus.
  • Transmitted in saliva, respiratory mucus, breast milk, urine, semen, cervical secretions, or in tissues (transplants).
  • Commonly latent in various tissues.
  • It establishes persistent infections by interfering with antigen presentation to CD8 T cells. It reduces cell-surface major histocompatibility complex class I, so infected cells are vulnerable to attack by natural killer cells; selection results in a population of natural killer cells designed to work when cytomegalovirus comes out of latency.
  • Most infections are asymptomatic (50% of women and 10% of newborns have been exposed) (slide 72).
  • 3 groups develop a more virulent form: fetuses, newborns and immunodeficient adults.
  • Newborns may show an enlarged liver and spleen, jaundice, capillary bleeding, microcephaly and ocular inflammation; it may be fatal. Babies who survive develop neurological sequelae, hearing and visual disturbances and mental retardation. This is the teratogen of 18.6.
  • Perinatal infection: mostly asymptomatic, or pneumonitis and a mononucleosis-like syndrome.
  • Patients with acquired immunodeficiency syndrome: cytomegalovirus mononucleosis, disseminated infection, retinitis. Most transplant patients: pneumonitis, hepatitis, myocarditis, meningoencephalitis (slide 74).
  • Before treatment it must be differentiated from other infections: microscopic analysis, monoclonal antibodies, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay.
  • Treatment is reserved for the immunocompromised (ganciclovir, foscarnet; not tested).
  • No vaccine yet: many are at different stages of development, but it is difficult because of the lack of an animal model.

18.18 · Objective 4 — Epstein-Barr virus (slides 75–80)

  • A ubiquitous virus; it infects lymphoid tissue and salivary glands.
  • Transmission: direct, oral contact and contamination with saliva.
  • In industrialized countries, the college-age population is vulnerable to infectious mononucleosis (mono, or the kissing disease). By mid-life, 90–95% of all people are infected.
  • Infectious mononucleosis: sore throat, high fever, cervical lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly; develops after a 30–50 day incubation.
  • Dormancy in B cells; it can be reactivated; may be asymptomatic.

Tumors and other complications (slides 77–78)

  • Burkitt lymphoma: a B cell malignancy; it usually develops in the jaw and grossly swells the cheek; central African children 5–7 years old; may be associated with chronic coinfections with malaria.
  • Nasopharyngeal carcinoma: a malignancy of epithelial cells; occurs in adults 30–60, older Chinese and African men, those with a family history and those who eat salt-cured foods.
  • Anyone with an immune deficiency is highly susceptible to Epstein-Barr virus; patients with acquired immunodeficiency syndrome get hairy leukoplakia.
  • Chronic fatigue syndrome: a possible link (slide 78; also called myalgic encephalomyelitis or systemic exertion intolerance disease, names she said you do not need). Symptoms: disabling fatigue, memory loss, sore throat, tender muscles, swollen lymph glands, joint pain, headaches, abnormal levels of CD4 T cells, low levels of natural killer cells, an overactive inflammatory response.

Diagnosis, treatment and prevention (slides 79–80)

  • A differential blood count shows lymphocytosis, neutropenia and large atypical lymphocytes; serological assays detect antibodies and antigen.
  • Treatment is directed at relief of the symptoms of fever and sore throat; disseminated disease may be treated with intravenous gamma globulin, interferon, acyclovir and monoclonal antibodies (drugs not tested).
  • No vaccines are available for prevention, but several therapeutic vaccines exist for Epstein-Barr virus related cancers (slide 79 says available; the chart says in clinical trials: 18.27).

18.19 · Objective 4 — Human herpesviruses 6, 7 and 8; Kaposi sarcoma (slides 81–84)

Human herpesvirus 6 (slides 81–83)

  • Also called the human B-lymphotropic virus.
  • Transmitted by close contact with saliva and other secretions; very common (95% of the population).
  • Causes roseola, an acute febrile disease in babies 2–12 months old: it begins with fever, followed by a faint maculopapular rash; usually self-limited.
  • Adults may get mononucleosis-like symptoms, lymphadenopathy and hepatitis.
  • Over 70% of multiple sclerosis patients show signs of infection, with active viruses in the lesions. An association (not causation) with Hodgkin’s lymphoma, oral carcinoma and certain T-cell leukemias.
  • No vaccine (chart).

Human herpesvirus 7 (slide 83)

Closely related to human herpesvirus 6; it causes similar diseases (her chart: roseola, by contact with saliva) and is also associated with cancers. No vaccine (chart).

Human herpesvirus 8 and Kaposi sarcoma (slides 83–84)

  • Kaposi’s sarcoma-associated virus, or human herpesvirus 8, is linked with the common tumor of patients with acquired immunodeficiency syndrome; it may also be involved in multiple myeloma, primary effusion lymphoma and some forms of Castleman’s disease. ★ Dr. Webster: “probably the one that you’d be most familiar with because it causes Kaposi’s sarcoma”.
  • Kaposi’s sarcoma: small, painless, discolored lumps on the skin, but it may disseminate to internal organs.
  • Her chart: transmitted by saliva, sexual contact, blood transfusion, organ transplant; no vaccine.

18.20 · Objective 4 — Hepatitis B virus (slides 85–91)

★ Lecturer emphasized — “you need to know that one”

“Three principal viruses associated with hepatitis, hepatitis B, which is the deoxyribonucleic acid virus, you need to know that one, and then Dr. Fair will talk to you about hepatitis A and C next week because they are ribonucleic acid viruses.” [part 2, 35:10–35:25]

The viral agents of hepatitis (slide 85): hepatitis is an inflammatory disease of liver cells that may result from several viruses: necrosis of hepatocytes and a mononuclear response that disrupts liver structure. It interferes with the liver’s excretion of bile pigments, so bilirubin accumulates in blood and tissues, causing jaundice, a yellow tinge in the skin and eyes. Three principal viruses: hepatitis B (a deoxyribonucleic acid virus), hepatitis A and hepatitis C (ribonucleic acid viruses).

The hepadnaviruses (slide 86)

  • Enveloped deoxyribonucleic acid viruses.
  • Difficult to culture in the lab: the deoxyribonucleic acid must be isolated from hepatitis B and injected into human liver cancer cells (the medium).
  • An unusual genome containing both double- and single-stranded deoxyribonucleic acid.
  • Tropism for the liver; may be involved in liver cell carcinoma.

Hepatitis B virus and disease (slides 87–89)

  • It multiplies exclusively in the liver, which continuously seeds the blood with viruses (chronic): 107 virions per milliliter of blood.
  • Minute amounts of blood and blood products can transmit infection; it is also sexually transmitted.
  • An issue for health care workers. ★ Dr. Webster: “for healthcare workers, this is why you have to avoid the needle sticks”. Her chart: a great risk to health care workers because of a very low infective dose.
  • A high incidence among the same population as human immunodeficiency virus.
  • It can become a chronic infection and increases the risk of liver cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma (the oncogenic example of 18.6).
  • Pathogenesis (slide 88): the virus enters through a break in the skin or mucous membrane, or by injection into the bloodstream; it reaches the liver cells, multiplies and releases viruses into the blood; an average 7-week incubation.
  • Most show few overt symptoms and eventually develop immunity. Some have malaise, fever, chills, anorexia, abdominal discomfort and diarrhea. In more severe disease: fever, jaundice, rash and arthritis, which may be worsened by co-infection with hepatitis D (the delta agent, a circular ribonucleic acid virus). A small number develop chronic liver disease: necrosis and cirrhosis.

Diagnosis, management and prevention (slides 90–91)

  • Diagnosis: examination of risk factors; serological tests to detect viral antibodies or antigen; radioimmunoassay and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay tests for surface antigens.
  • Screening of blood for transfusion, semen for sperm banks, organs for transplant, and routine prenatal testing of all pregnant women.
  • Mild cases are managed by treating symptoms and supportive care; chronic infections are treated with interferon.
  • Passive immunization with hepatitis B immune globulin for persons exposed or possibly exposed, including health care workers or neonates born to infected mothers.
  • Primary prevention is vaccination, for high-risk individuals and encouraged for all newborns and infants.
    • Vaccines derived from surface antigen from cloned yeast: 3 doses with boosters.
    • A vaccine derived from purified, sterile antigen extracted from carrier blood, mainly for people with yeast allergies.

18.21 · Objective 4 — Adenoviruses (slides 92–95)

  • Nonenveloped, double-stranded deoxyribonucleic acid; an acute infection.
  • Over 50 types are associated with human disease.
  • They infect lymphoid tissue, respiratory and intestinal epithelia and the conjunctiva.
  • Oncogenic in animals, not in humans.
  • Spread by respiratory and ocular secretions (her chart adds ingestion).
  • Cause colds, pharyngitis, conjunctivitis, keratoconjunctivitis, acute hemorrhagic cystitis, infantile gastroenteritis.
  • Severe cases may be treated with interferon or antivirals.
  • Type 41 adenovirus is associated with hepatitis in children aged 1–6 (slide 94): liver enzymes are remarkably increased; the children have vomiting, diarrhea, jaundice and abdominal pain; some advanced to acute liver failure. Some of the most serious cases show a co-infection with adeno-associated virus (a parvovirus); at this time only an association, with more data needed for causation (18.25).
  • Vaccine: a polyvalent vaccine against types 4 and 7, which the slide calls inactivated; some military personnel are vaccinated in training environments; given as an oral vaccine to personnel 17–50 years old (18.27 on “inactivated”).

18.22 · Objective 4 — Papilloma and polyoma viruses; human papillomavirus (slides 96–100)

Papilloma and polyoma viruses (slide 96): small, nonenveloped, double-stranded deoxyribonucleic acid; circular deoxyribonucleic acid; they cause persistent infections and tumors.

Human papillomavirus (slides 97–99)

  • Papilloma: a squamous epithelial growth, wart, or verruca; caused by 100 different strains of human papillomavirus.
  • Common seed warts: painless, elevated, rough growths, on the fingers and elsewhere.
  • Plantar warts: deep, painful, on the soles of the feet.
  • Genital warts: the slide calls them the most common sexually transmitted disease in the United States; they range from tiny, flat, inconspicuous bumps to extensive, branching, cauliflower-like masses.
  • Transmissible through direct contact or contaminated fomites (her chart: including sexual contact); incubation 2 weeks to more than a year.
  • Nine human papillomavirus types increase the risk of reproductive cancer; 2 account for 70% of metastatic tumors. Slide 28: papillomaviruses cause cervical and penile cancer (18.6).
  • Early detection through inspection of the genitals and, for women, a Pap smear to screen for abnormal cervical cells. ★ Dr. Webster: “this is why early detection is really important”.
  • Most common warts regress over time; they can be removed by direct chemical application (podophyllin) and physical removal by cauterization, freezing or laser surgery. Warts can recur.
  • Two effective human papillomavirus vaccines, Gardasil and Cervarix (her chart: vaccines available for genital warts).

18.23 · Objective 4 — Polyomaviruses: JC and BK viruses (slides 101–102)

18.24 · Objective 4 — Parvoviruses and parvovirus B19 (slides 103–105)

  • Parvoviruses: nonenveloped, single-stranded deoxyribonucleic acid; a small diameter and genome size.
  • In animals they cause distemper in cats, enteric disease in dogs and fatal cardiac infection in puppies.
  • B19 causes erythema infectiosum (fifth disease), a rash of childhood: children may have fever and a rash on the cheeks. It is the fifth rash disease of childhood; the other 4 are chickenpox, measles, rubella and roseola.
  • Severe fatal anemia can result if a pregnant woman transmits the virus to the fetus, which can result in hydrops fetalis. ★ Dr. Webster: “if a pregnant woman gets it, it can be very, very serious”.
  • In adults it can cause a polyarthropathy syndrome with massive swelling of the joints, or cessation of red blood cell production; most common in the immunocompromised.
  • Her chart: spread by droplet infection; the vaccine cell is blank.

18.25 · Objective 4 — Adeno-associated virus (slides 94, 106)

  • Adeno-associated virus is a defective parvovirus: it cannot replicate in the host cell without adenovirus.
  • It is now used as a viral vector for human gene therapy: currently approved to treat lipoprotein lipase deficiency, with multiple clinical trials for cystic fibrosis, genetic eye diseases, macular degeneration, hemophilia and neurodegenerative diseases.
  • By itself it is not known to cause disease, but some evidence suggests that as a coinfection with an adenovirus that causes hepatitis it makes the infection more severe (slide 94: only an association so far).
