Before you begin

Lecture 17 of Microbiology — Parasites of Medical Importance. Thirty questions, every one cited to the slide it came from and weighted by the minutes Dr. Webster spent on each organism (most on malaria, the helminth life cycles, Entamoeba, Toxoplasma and the trypanosomes). Thirty-three organisms do not fit in thirty questions, so the two sets split the smaller ones between them; take both. Protozoa are sorted by how they move: the amebas (Entamoeba, Naegleria, Acanthamoeba), the ciliate Balantidium, the flagellates (Trichomonas, Giardia, and the blood-borne trypanosomes and Leishmania) and the apicomplexans (Plasmodium, Toxoplasma, Cryptosporidium and the other coccidians, Babesia). Helminths are the roundworms, flukes and tapeworms, each following one of four life-cycle patterns; know the definitive, intermediate and transport hosts. For every organism, know how people acquire it (objective 2), what it does, and how it is found and prevented. Every set asks what she stressed: antiprotozoan drugs are few and toxic, helminth pathology (“something you should know”), pregnant women and the cat litter box, and Chagas disease moving north with its vector. No question asks which drug treats what, a number, a hemoflagellate stage name, or the summary charts she said not to memorize.
