1 · The memory system

Memory aid — one picture for every heart maneuver

The left ventricle is a water tank. Blood comes in through the back door (the mitral valve) and leaves through the front door (the aortic valve) to the body. Every heart maneuver does one of four things to that tank.

  1. Fill it or drain it. Squatting and raising the legs push the blood stored in the legs back to the heart — the tank fills. PD2 s62 Valsalva and standing up quickly leave blood in the legs and chest — the tank drains. PD2 s61 PD2 s62
  2. More water, more noise. Almost every murmur gets louder on a full tank and softer on an empty one: aortic stenosis (more blood rushing past the narrow front door PD2 s62), mitral and aortic regurgitation CMS L23 s48 CMS L23 s28, and the innocent flow murmur, which fades when the patient stands, sits up or bears down. PD2 s66
  3. Two rebels get worse on an EMPTY tank. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is a bulging wall (the thick septum) beside the front door: a full tank holds the walls apart, the outflow obstruction lessens and the murmur softens PD2 s62; drain the tank and the bulge closes in on the door — louder. PD2 s61 Mitral valve prolapse is a floppy back-door flap: on a draining tank its murmur increases Handout; fill the tank and the click moves later in systole. PD2 s62
    So: a murmur that gets LOUDER with Valsalva or standing is one of the two rebels — and a click means prolapse.
  4. Lean on the front door. A sustained handgrip raises the pressure the heart pumps against (afterload). Handout Blood that can go backward does: mitral regurgitation, aortic regurgitation and a ventricular septal defect get louder. PD2 s63 The murmurs of blood leaving through the front door — aortic stenosis and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy — get softer. CMS L23 s17 Handout
  5. A breath feeds the right side. Breathing in pulls more blood into the right heart, so right-sided sounds get louder on inspiration: RILE — Right Inspiration, Left Expiration. CMS L23 s10 A holosystolic murmur that grows on inspiration is tricuspid, not mitral (the Carvallo sign). CMS L23 s71
  6. Bring the tank to the microphone. Left side down rolls the apex against the chest wall — for the low sounds at the apex, with the bell: S3, S4, mitral stenosis. PD2 s40 PD2 s42 PD2 s79 Sit up, lean forward, breathe out and hold — for the high sounds along the left sternal border: aortic regurgitation and the pericardial rub. PD2 s77 PD2 s81

And for the legs — arteries hate elevation, veins hate hanging

Arteries push blood down to the foot; veins carry it back up. Raise a leg with sick arteries and the blood cannot climb: the foot goes pale; let it hang and it flushes deep red (dependent rubor) — peripheral artery disease. CMS L27 s30 PD2 s91 Let a leg with sick veins hang and blood pools: dusky, swollen, varicose PD2 s93 CMS L26 s40 — and raising it brings relief. CMS L26 s6

Where the picture breaks. It is a way to hold the direction of each change, not the physiology. The slides give results, not a flap-and-strings mechanism for prolapse. Mitral stenosis and pulmonic stenosis also sit on the deck’s handgrip list PD2 s63 though neither is a backward leak — learn those two as list facts. And one exception to rule 5: the pulmonic ejection sound gets softer on inspiration. PD2 s38

★ Marked in the lecture

The deck puts this in capitals on the hypertrophic cardiomyopathy maneuvers slide: “IMPORTANT TO LEARN THIS MURMUR SO YOU DON’T SIGN OFF INCORRECTLY ON A SPORTS PHYSICAL.” PD2 s60 Rules 2 and 3 are how you catch it: an innocent murmur softens when the athlete stands or bears down; hypertrophic cardiomyopathy gets louder.

3 · Positions: bring the sound to the stethoscope

Do these after the routine five areas. The deck lists two special positions. PD2 s59

Left lateral decubitus — left side down, bell at the apex

Do: Roll the patient onto the left side. Find the apical impulse and listen there with the bell, held lightly. PD2 s59 PD2 s79

Say: “I’m going to have you roll onto your left side so I can listen at the apex with the bell for low-pitched sounds.”

If you findIt points to
A low sound just after S2, with the bell — Ken-TUC-ky PD2 s40S3. Over age 40: Heart failure or volume overload from aortic or mitral regurgitation. Normal in children, young adults up to 35–40 and late pregnancy. PD2 s40
A dull, low sound just before S1 — TEN-nes-see PD2 s42S4, a stiff ventricle: hypertensive heart disease, aortic stenosis, ischemic and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Normal in trained athletes and older adults. PD2 s42
A low-pitched rumble in mid to late diastole at the apex, after an opening snap PD2 s79 CMS L23 s38Mitral stenosis.
A holosystolic, blowing murmur at the apex CMS L23 s48Mitral regurgitation — then follow it into the left axilla. PD2 s74

Sitting up, leaning forward, breath out and held

Do: Sit the patient up, leaning forward; ask them to breathe all the way out and hold it. Listen along the left sternal border with the diaphragm. PD2 s59 CMS L31 s17

Say: “Please sit up and lean forward, breathe all the way out, and hold your breath for a few seconds while I listen.”

If you findIt points to
A high-pitched, blowing, decrescendo murmur right after S2 PD2 s77Aortic regurgitation. New, with tearing chest or back pain and unequal arm pressures: Aortic dissection. CMS L20 s29
A scratchy, scraping sound through systole and diastole, loudest at Erb’s point PD2 s81Pericardial friction rub → Acute pericarditis. It stays with the breath held; a pleural rub is heard only during inspiration. CMS L31 s17
A harsh midsystolic crescendo-decrescendo murmur at the right second intercostal spaceAortic stenosis — heard better sitting and leaning forward. PD2 s67 CMS L23 s17

4 · Fill or drain the tank: Valsalva, standing, squatting, leg raise

These are the maneuvers that separate the systolic murmurs — above all, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and mitral valve prolapse from aortic stenosis. PD2 s60

Valsalva — drains the tank

Do: Patient supine. Ask them to bear down, like straining for a bowel movement, or to push against your hand on the mid-abdomen. Forceful expiration against a closed airway raises the pressure in the chest and decreases left ventricular filling (preload). PD2 s61

Say: “I’d like you to bear down as if you’re straining, and keep holding while I listen.”

If you findIt points to
The murmur gets LOUDERHypertrophic cardiomyopathy. PD2 s61 With a mid-systolic click instead: Mitral valve prolapse (its murmur increases). Handout
The murmur gets softer or does not changeAortic stenosis. PD2 s61 A grade 1–3 murmur in a young person that softens: an innocent flow murmur. PD2 s66
A holosystolic murmur at the apex gets softerMitral regurgitation. CMS L23 s48

The deck adds that Valsalva can also identify heart failure and pulmonary hypertension. PD2 s61

Standing up quickly from a squat — drains the tank

Do: Patient squats; listen; then the patient stands up quickly while you listen. Blood moves to the legs, less returns to the heart, and left ventricular preload falls. PD2 s62

Say: “I’m going to have you squat down, then stand up quickly with me while I keep listening.”

If you findIt points to
The murmur gets LOUDERHypertrophic cardiomyopathy. PD2 s62 PD2 s70 Mitral valve prolapse also increases. Handout
The murmur gets softerAortic stenosis. PD2 s62 An innocent murmur also softens on standing. PD2 s66

Squatting from standing, or raising the legs — fills the tank

Do: From standing, have the patient squat while you listen; or, if they cannot squat, raise their legs while they lie supine. Blood stored in the legs returns to the heart and preload rises. PD2 s62

Say: “Now I’m going to raise your legs for a moment while I listen.”

If you findIt points to
The murmur gets LOUDERAortic stenosis — more blood rushing past the narrow valve. PD2 s62 Mitral regurgitation and aortic regurgitation also increase. CMS L23 s48 CMS L23 s28
The murmur gets softerHypertrophic cardiomyopathy — the outflow obstruction lessens. PD2 s62
The click moves later in systoleMitral valve prolapse. PD2 s62

Read across: with every fill-or-drain maneuver, aortic stenosis and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy move in opposite directions, and prolapse moves with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (handgrip is the exception: it softens both). The click is what separates prolapse from hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

5 · Handgrip: lean on the front door

Sustained isometric handgrip

Do: Have the patient squeeze both hands as hard as they can and hold while you listen. It raises systemic arterial pressure (afterload). PD2 s63 Handout

Say: “Squeeze my hands as hard as you can and keep squeezing while I listen.”

If you findIt points to
A holosystolic murmur at the apex gets LOUDERMitral regurgitation. PD2 s63 CMS L23 s48
A blowing early diastolic murmur gets LOUDERAortic regurgitation. PD2 s63 CMS L23 s28
A diastolic rumble at the apex gets LOUDERMitral stenosis. PD2 s63 CMS L23 s38
A systolic murmur gets LOUDERVentricular septal defect (or, by the slide, pulmonic stenosis). PD2 s63
A midsystolic ejection murmur gets softerAortic stenosis CMS L23 s17 Handout or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Handout

The slide’s handgrip list and the handout disagree about the mitral valve: the slide has mitral regurgitation louder, the handout has mitral valve prolapse softer. Learn each as its source gives it.

6 · Breathing: RILE

Right-sided sounds get louder on Inspiration, Left-sided on Expiration. CMS L23 s10 Ask the patient to take a slow deep breath in and out while you stay on the spot.

Deep breath in, then out

Do: Listen through a slow, deep breath at the lower left sternal border and the pulmonic area; for neck veins, watch them through the breath.

Say: “Take a slow, deep breath in for me… and all the way out.”

If you findIt points to
A holosystolic murmur at the lower left sternal border gets louder on inspirationTricuspid regurgitation — the Carvallo sign, which distinguishes it from mitral regurgitation. CMS L23 s71 PD2 s73
A holosystolic murmur at the apex, radiating to the axilla, without that inspiratory increaseMitral regurgitation — the Carvallo sign is what separates the two. CMS L23 s71 PD2 s74
An early diastolic murmur in the pulmonic area that grows on inspirationPulmonic regurgitation. PD2 s78 CMS L23 s63
S2 splits into two sounds on inspiration and fuses on expirationNormal — physiologic splitting of S2. PD2 s15
The scratchy sound stops when the breath is heldA pleural rub, not a pericardial one (a pleural rub is heard only during inspiration). CMS L31 s17
The neck veins rise on inspiration instead of fallingKussmaul sign: constrictive pericarditis or restrictive cardiomyopathy. CMS L31 s39 CMS L28 s92

Two inspiration exceptions: the pulmonic ejection sound gets softer on inspiration, and the aortic ejection sound does not vary. PD2 s38

7 · Vital-sign maneuvers

Say these out loud even when the vitals form already has the numbers: they are how you rule a diagnosis in or out.

Orthostatic vital signs

Do: Blood pressure and heart rate supine, then standing; read within 3 minutes. CMS L21 s13 CMS L20 s39

Say: “I’d like to check your blood pressure lying down and again after you stand.”

If you findIt points to
Systolic falls more than 20 or diastolic more than 10 within 3 minutes (or systolic more than 30 with a high baseline or supine hypertension)Orthostatic hypotension. CMS L21 s13
… and the heart rate rises a lot (68 → 92)Non-neurogenic: volume loss or medicines — diarrhea, hemorrhage, diuretics. CMS L21 s34–36
… and the heart rate barely rises (72 → 76)Neurogenic: autonomic failure — diabetes, Parkinson disease. CMS L21 s30 CMS L21 s36
Heart rate rises more than 30 (or above 120) within 10 minutes of standingPostural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome. CMS L21 s18
Normal orthostatic readings, with a trigger such as fear, pain, heat or long standingVasovagal syncope. CMS L21 s14

Blood pressure in both arms, and radial with femoral pulses

Do: Measure the pressure in both arms (at the first visit CMS L20 s39; with tearing pain, to look for a difference CMS L20 s29). Then feel the radial and femoral pulses at the same time. PD2 s102

Say: “I’m going to check the pressure in both arms and feel the pulses at your wrist and groin together.”

If you findIt points to
Arms differ by more than 10–15 mmHgSubclavian stenosis or other vascular disease. CMS L20 s39
Unequal arm pressures or a pulse deficit, with tearing pain maximal at onsetAortic dissection — and normal pulses do not exclude it. CMS L20 s29
The femoral pulse lags behind the radialCoarctation of the aorta; measure the leg pressure. PD2 s102 CMS L20 s39 CMS L20 s40

Pulsus paradoxus

Do: With a manual cuff, deflate slowly: note the pressure where sounds appear only on expiration, then where they are heard throughout the breath. Textbook

Say: “I’m going to watch how your blood pressure changes as you breathe.”

If you findIt points to
Systolic pressure falls with inspiration — by more than 10 mmHg CMS L31 s59 Slide notesCardiac tamponade, with Beck triad: low pressure, distended neck veins, muffled heart sounds. CMS L31 s54 CMS L31 s59 Also seen in constrictive pericarditis. CMS L31 s44

Hepatojugular reflux

Do: Press firmly over the liver and watch the neck veins. CMS L28 s33

Say: “I’m going to press on your belly for a few seconds while I watch your neck.”

If you findIt points to
The neck veins distend with pressure over the liverVenous congestion: Heart failure (right-sided failure backs up into the neck veins CMS L25 s10), dilated or restrictive cardiomyopathy. CMS L28 s33 CMS L28 s93

8 · The legs and hands: arteries hate elevation, veins hate hanging

Always compare both sides. PD2 s86

Pulses — grade every one, 0 to 4+

Do: Radial, brachial, femoral, popliteal, posterior tibial and dorsalis pedis, both sides. PD2 s98–104 4+ bounding, 3+ increased, 2+ brisk (normal), 1+ diminished, 0 absent. PD2 s86

Say: “Pulses are 2+ and symmetric in the upper and lower extremities.”

If you findIt points to
Absent or diminished distal pulsesPeripheral artery disease (atherosclerosis), a systemic embolism, or small-vessel disease such as diabetes. PD2 s96 CMS L20 s40
Bounding pulses with a wide pulse pressureAortic regurgitation. CMS L23 s28
A double systolic peak (pulsus bisferiens)Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. CMS L28 s67
A weak, delayed carotid with a forceful apex, felt togetherAortic stenosis, severe. CMS L23 s17

Capillary refill

Do: Press on a fingernail, release, and time how long the nail bed takes to turn pink. PD2 s85

Say: “Capillary refill is two to three seconds.”

If you findIt points to
Pink within 2–3 secondsNormal. PD2 s85
The nail bed flashes red to pale with each heartbeat (Quincke pulse) PD2 s85Classically aortic regurgitation. Textbook

Pitting edema — press, then grade

Do: Press firmly with the thumb for at least 2 seconds over the dorsum of the foot, behind the medial malleolus and over the shin. Grade it and say how high it goes. PD2 s88
1+ 2 mm, gone quickly · 2+ 4 mm, gone in 10–15 s · 3+ 6 mm, lasts over 1 min · 4+ 8 mm, lasts over 2 min PD2 s89

Say: “There is 2+ pitting edema of both lower legs to the mid-shin.”

If you findIt points to
Both legs, with raised neck veinsHeart failure (right-sided). CMS L25 s10
One leg, swollen, warm and tenderDeep vein thrombosis — swelling is 97% sensitive. CMS L26 s51
Edema with brown (hemosiderin) staining, ulcers over the malleoli, normal pulsesChronic venous insufficiency. PD2 s93
No edema, with cool, shiny, hairless skin and weak pulsesPeripheral artery disease. PD2 s91

Raise the leg, then let it hang (Buerger test)

Do: Supine: raise one leg to 45 degrees for 1 minute and look at the foot. Then sit the patient up with the legs hanging over the side of the bed. CMS L27 s30 For veins, look at the legs with the patient standing. CMS L26 s40

Say: “I’m going to raise your leg for a minute, then have you sit with your legs hanging over the side.”

If you findIt points to
Pale when raised, then deep red when hanging (dependent rubor)Peripheral artery disease — the arteries cannot push blood uphill. CMS L27 s30 PD2 s91
Dusky, bluish foot when hanging, brown pigmentation, normal pulsesChronic venous insufficiency. PD2 s93
Dilated, tortuous veins seen and felt when the patient standsVaricose veins — long-standing ones progress to chronic venous insufficiency. CMS L26 s40

Ankle-brachial index

Do: Supine for 10 minutes. Doppler and cuff: brachial pressure (two readings averaged in each arm; use the higher arm), then dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial at the ankle, cuff just above the malleoli. Take the higher ankle pressure ÷ the brachial systolic pressure, for each leg, to 2 decimal places. PD2 s110–112 CMS L27 s27

Say: “I would order an ankle-brachial index to look for arterial disease in the legs.”

If you findIt points to
0.90–1.40Normal. PD2 s110
Below 0.90Peripheral artery disease. PD2 s113
Below 0.50Severe peripheral artery disease. PD2 s113
Above 1.40A noncompressible, calcified vessel (diabetes, for example) — the number is not reliable. PD2 s113 CMS L27 s42

The Clinical Medicine deck uses slightly different cut-offs (normal above 0.9 to 1.3; claudication 0.4–0.9; rest pain or tissue loss 0–0.4). CMS L27 s42 For this station, use the Physical Diagnosis numbers above.

Allen test

Do: The patient clenches a fist for 30 seconds; press on both the radial and ulnar arteries; the patient opens the hand (it is white); release the ulnar artery and watch the hand. Repeat, releasing the radial. PD2 s108

Say: “I’m checking that both arteries supply the hand.”

If you findIt points to
The hand pinks up when you release the arteryThat artery is patent — normal. PD2 s108
The hand stays whiteThat artery is not patent: poor arterial supply to the hand from it. PD2 s108

Homan sign

Do: Keep the knee bent and quickly, forcefully dorsiflex the foot at the ankle. Do it on the leg you are worried about. PD2 s114

Say: “I’m going to bend your foot up quickly; tell me if that hurts.”

If you findIt points to
Pain behind the kneeDeep vein thrombosis. PD2 s114
A negative resultDoes not rule it out: the sign is neither sensitive nor specific. CMS L26 s51 Score the Wells criteria and get a venous duplex ultrasound. CMS L26 s54 CMS L26 s57

Sources, and where they disagree

  • Clinical Medicine and Surgery: Lecture 20 Hypertension (Jaquith); Lecture 21 Hypotension, 23 Valvular Heart Disease, 25 Heart Failure, 27 Arterial Occlusive Disease and Aneurysms, 28 Cardiomyopathy (Carter); Lecture 26 Venous Disorders (Shah); Lecture 31 Pericardial Disease.
  • Ankle-brachial index: the Physical Diagnosis deck gives normal 0.90–1.40, below 0.90 disease, below 0.50 severe, above 1.40 calcified; the Clinical Medicine deck gives normal above 0.9 to 1.3, above 1.3 calcified, and 0.4 as the rest-pain line.   • Handgrip: the Physical Diagnosis slide lists mitral regurgitation, pulmonic stenosis, ventricular septal defect, aortic regurgitation and mitral stenosis as louder; the handout lists aortic stenosis, mitral valve prolapse and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy as softer. Both are given as their sources say.
  • Mitral stenosis and Valsalva: the Clinical Medicine valvular slide also lists mitral stenosis as louder with Valsalva and exertion (“increasing blood into the left atrium”). CMS L23 s38 It is left out of the filling table above, which follows the Physical Diagnosis deck.
  • Venous ulcer site: the Physical Diagnosis deck puts it over the medial and lateral malleolus PD2 s93; the Venous Disorders deck puts it above the ankle, on the medial or anterior side. CMS L26 s15
  • Not on a slide: the pulsus paradoxus cut-off of more than 10 mmHg is in the speaker notes of Pericardial Disease slide 59 (the slide itself shows the inspiratory fall); the cuff technique for pulsus paradoxus and the meaning of a Quincke pulse are textbook.
