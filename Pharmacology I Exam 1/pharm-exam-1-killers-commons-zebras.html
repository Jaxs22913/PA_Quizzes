Why a framework beats a list. The side-effects reference on this site is organised by drug, which is the right shape for looking something up and the wrong shape for revising. This one is organised the way he said he thinks about them, which is the shape the question comes in. Use both: the full side-effects chart is here.

How to use this his framework, not a list

Asked which adverse effects to focus on, Dr. Wood did not give a list — he gave three buckets and told the class to sort everything into them. The sorting is the revision. Take any side effect you have learned and ask: would this kill them, does it happen to nearly everyone on the group, or is it the one strange thing that belongs to this drug alone?

Where he said to aim: “Any time you see those killers or the zebras, those are always things you should look at.” The commons cover a good share of what he asks and mostly come free with the group; the killers and the zebras are each worth about a question.
What is his and what is not. The nine entries badged his example are effects he named himself at the review. The rest are the lecture decks’ own adverse effects sorted by his criteria, each citing its slide. He did not assign those buckets — his rule did, and you can check every one against the slide beside it.

Killers 7

Dangerous. Immediate discontinuation and evaluation. Not necessarily the commonest — these are the ones you warn the patient about in advance, so that they do not decide that skin sloughing off is a normal side effect.
Anaphylaxishis example
Penicillins — the whole class, and the carbapenems and monobactam
The deck asks for monitoring for signs and symptoms of anaphylaxis on the natural penicillins, the penicillinase-resistant penicillins, the antipseudomonal penicillins, aztreonam and the carbapenems — five separate slides. Stop the drug and evaluate.

Commons 6

What actually happens, often. These belong to the GROUP, not the drug. Asked whether the common effects of penicillins, cephalosporins and carbapenems need learning separately, he said “no, they’re all the same” — so they are listed by group here, and the effort goes where a class breaks the pattern.
Every antibiotichis example
Gastrointestinal upset — nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea
His own example, and the whole point of the bucket. Almost every antibiotic causes this in somebody. Tell the patient it is expected; it may or may not warrant changing therapy.

Zebras 9

Uncommon, but unique to one drug. “Whenever you hear hoofs beating, normally you should be thinking horses. Sometimes it’s a zebra.” Each is worth about one question — and together with the killers they are where he told the class to look.
Disulfiram-like reaction with alcoholhis example
metronidazole
His own example of a zebra. Uncommon in practice — a patient taking it for an infection is often not drinking — but unique to this drug, and it costs nothing to warn them.
Antibiotics, Antivirals, and Antifungals · slide 86
Red Man syndromehis example
vancomycin
His own example. An infusion-related reaction, not an allergy, which is why the answer is to slow the infusion rather than to stop the drug. He specified two hours.

tetracyclines
He pointed at the tetracyclines by name as where a class breaks the common pattern. Doxycycline discolours more than the others; avoid under 8 years old and in the second and third trimesters.
